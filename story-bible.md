## THE CORE

**Premise:**
Ellen Daly, 58, spent 31 years as an FAA air-traffic controller until federal law retired her at 56. Two years later she does the first big thing she has ever chosen: she buys Kettle Lanes, a ten-lane 1958 candlepin alley in Ambrose, New Hampshire, a lake town that empties out after the summer people leave. The former owner, 84-year-old Arlene Fortier, carries a $250,000 note that comes due March 1. To refinance, Ellen has to show the bank a profitable winter. Ray Gagnon, 61, the widowed hardware-store owner who has coached the alley's winter league for 30 years, lost the purchase by eight days. He holds a right of first refusal, he has the only key that matters, and he is the only person in town who can keep her 1950s pinsetters running. If she fails, the alley is his. He has every reason to watch her fail, and an inconvenient habit of helping.

**Emotional question:**
Can a person who has always carried everything alone learn to share the load without feeling she has been relieved of it?

**Tone:**
Dry, warm, brisk.

**This story is NOT:**
- Not a makeover fantasy. No one rescues the alley with a windfall, a viral video, a rich relative, or Ray's money. Every dollar of the margin comes from a decision Ellen made earlier.
- Not sentimental women's fiction. No grief montages, no "healing journey," no epiphanies delivered by children or snow. Feeling shows up in what people do with their hands and what they choose not to say.
- Not a villain story. Nobody is a villain: not Ray, not Arlene, not the bank, not the league, not Kate. The conflict comes from two people who each want something reasonable for the same building.

**Target prose register:**
Close third person, past tense, one POV per section (alternating Ellen and Ray; the outline's POV plan is authoritative). Short-to-mid sentences with an occasional long one for momentum. Comedy comes from precision, juxtaposition, and understatement, not from exclamation, slapstick narration, or characters laughing at their own jokes. Dialogue carries at least half of every scene. Metaphors come from the POV character's world: Ellen thinks in sequences, intervals, headings, and numbers. Ray thinks in tolerances, wear, grain, and weather. One figure of speech per paragraph at most, and many paragraphs have none. Enter scenes late and leave early. Never summarize the previous chapter at the top of a new one. Specific nouns beat adjectives: "a 1979 Bowl-Mor table" rather than "an old machine."

## CHARACTERS

### Ellen Daly

- **Age, appearance:** 58. Five foot six, square-shouldered. Silver hair cut short every five weeks to the day. Reading glasses on a black cord she resents and won't give up. Navy fleece vest over a pressed oxford shirt, sleeves rolled exactly twice. A plain steel wristwatch she still sets against the atomic clock on Sundays. Bad left knee from a ski fall in 2009 that she will not discuss.
- **Background (facts arrive in single lines, never paragraphs of history; feelings and motives get short interior passages, see World rule 10):** Hired by the FAA at 25. Early years in a tower working paper flight strips. Most of her career at Boston Consolidated TRACON in Merrimack, NH, working arrivals into Logan. One loss of separation in 31 years, at 2 a.m. near the end of a midnight shift. She reported it herself and retrained, and she has not forgiven herself for it. Mandatory retirement at 56. Divorced amicably about 20 years ago; her ex is never on the page and never discussed beyond one line. One daughter, Kate. Sold her house in Merrimack to fund the down payment. Lives on her FERS pension and takes no salary from the alley, which matters to the money (see Money table).
- **Wound:** The job she was best at was taken from her by statute at the end of the month she turned fifty-six, with a sheet cake. And on the worst night of her career she sounded perfect on the frequency while everything inside her came apart. She learned that her calm can hide anything, so she has stopped expecting anyone to notice when she needs help.
- **Want:** To make Kettle Lanes pay on her own by March 1. It has to be her decisions and her numbers, so she knows she can carry what she chose.
- **Need:** To learn that being relieved isn't failing. A position can be shared without being surrendered. The D-side isn't a demotion.
- **Greatest strength / how it's also their flaw:** Composure and sequencing. She can take forty problems and put them in order. The flaw: she briefs people instead of asking them. She plans a contingency for every failure except other people's feelings, and she treats accepting help as a loss of control.
- **What they would never do:** Raise her voice at work. Fudge a number, even to herself. Take money she didn't earn. Leave a job without a proper handover. Do a repair she isn't qualified for. She hires the certified technician and holds the flashlight.
- **Speech pattern:** Short declarative sentences. Exact numbers ("forty-one," never "about forty"). She answers the question that was actually asked, literally, which is where much of her comedy comes from. She reads instructions back. When she's angry she gets slower, quieter, and stops using contractions. Her humor is deadpan understatement; she never signals that she's joking. When cornered she goes procedural ("Let's take those one at a time"). She almost never says "I feel." She says "I'm aware."
- **ATC-phrase rule (binding for prose-writer and voice-editor):**
  1. In ordinary scenes, Ellen uses **at most one** ATC phrase per scene in dialogue and **three per chapter** in total, counting her POV narration.
  2. In the three set pieces built on her phraseology (Ch 01 PA lane assignments, Ch 05 radio sequencing, Ch 09 storm day), the cap rises to **six per chapter**.
  3. Every phrase must be clear from context the first time the reader meets it. No unexplained acronyms on the page. "TRACON" may appear once per chapter at most. Never use "ATSAP," "MOR," or "7110.65" in prose.
  4. Never put two ATC phrases in one sentence. Never use one where plain English would be funnier.
  5. When other characters repeat or mock her phrases (the league's fake pilot-speak, Ray's "say request"), it doesn't count against her cap, but it does count toward the scene's texture. Don't let a scene become a jargon routine.
  6. Approved core vocabulary: cleared, unable, say again, stand by, say intentions, affirmative, negative, hold short, holding, sequence, number two (in line), traffic (twelve o'clock, one mile), ground stop, miles-in-trail, readback, relief briefing, the D-side. Anything else needs a reason.
  7. Her interior voice thinks in order, intervals, and numbers, not in jargon.
- **Sample dialogue lines:**
  - "I heard you. I'm giving you the chance to say something else."
  - "Forty-one bowlers, ten lanes, one fryer. That isn't a crowd. That's a sequence."
  - "Lane seven is unable. Lane seven, you're with lane eight. Nobody panic. Nobody's in the air."
  - "I'm aware that I can't bowl, Mr. Gagnon. I'm also aware that I own the lanes."
  - "I don't need it to be easy. I need it to be mine."
- **Behavioral tics:**
  1. She labels things: masking tape and a Sharpie, block capitals, flight-strip format (lane, time, party name, count, remarks).
  2. Whenever she walks into a room she counts the people in it. Under her breath, if it's been a bad day.
  3. She stands to eat and to take bad news. She sits down only when the decision has been made.

### Ray Gagnon

- **Age, appearance:** 61. Six foot one, a little stooped from forty years of counters. Gray crew cut he gets done at the barber next to the pharmacy every other Saturday. Big hands with nicked knuckles. A carpenter's pencil behind his right ear, reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and forgotten there. Brown canvas work jacket with GAGNON & SON stitched over the pocket. His key ring is heavy, and on it is the Kettle Lanes key with an orange paper tag reading "R.G. — 1996" in Arlene's handwriting.
- **Background:** Grew up above the hardware store his father, Lucien, opened in 1964. Married Linda Gagnon (née Bouchard) in 1988; she was the best woman candlepin bowler in the county and holds the women's house record at Kettle Lanes (187, February 1998). She died six years ago of a stroke at 55, on a Wednesday in March (March 4, 2020, before the shutdown; never date it on the page). She had bowled the night before. He has coached the Kettle Lanes Winter League since 1996, and Arlene gave him a key that year so he could open on league nights. He has been Arlene's unpaid mechanic for most of the last twenty years. Son Danny runs the store day to day. His SBA-backed financing to buy the alley was approved eight days after Arlene accepted Ellen's offer.
- **Wound:** When Linda died, he froze everything that was still the way she left it: her bowling ball in locker 14, her chair behind the store counter, the league exactly as she bowled it. He believes that keeping things the same is a form of loyalty. Losing the alley by eight days felt like losing the last room she was in.
- **Want:** To own Kettle Lanes and keep it exactly as it was.
- **Need:** To see that a museum isn't loyalty. Linda would have entered the Open on day one. He needs to want to be asked more than he wants to own.
- **Greatest strength / how it's also their flaw:** Steadiness and loyalty. He shows up, every time, with the right part. The flaw: he says what he feels by fixing things instead of saying it. He helps everyone and lets nothing change. He will repair a machine at midnight rather than have one honest conversation at noon.
- **What they would never do:** Leave a machine broken on league night. Profit from someone else's mistake on purpose. Lie, even to be kind (he goes quiet instead). Speak against Ellen in public. In Ch 07 he disagrees with her and still walks over to stand beside her at the counter when the teams leave. Bowl in anything but his own shoes.
- **Speech pattern:** New Hampshire understatement. Few words, and the important ones come last. He answers personal questions with a story about somebody else ("Fella I knew in Tilton..."). He asks questions he already knows the answers to. He diagnoses things by sound. He calls Ellen "Mrs. Daly" when he's irritated, which she corrects to "Ms." every time, until the day she doesn't. **No phonetic dialect** in his dialogue: no dropped g's spelled out, and "ayuh" no more than once in the whole book.
- **Sample dialogue lines:**
  - "Not my alley. I just know where it hurts."
  - "Your table on seven's been slipping since Thanksgiving. You can hear it from the parking lot if you know what you're listening for."
  - "Fella I knew bought a boat the week his wife left. Boat was fine. He couldn't swim."
  - "I didn't fix it for you. Thursday league's got a match."
  - "I don't want to own it anymore. I want to be asked."
- **Behavioral tics:**
  1. He fixes things absently while he talks: tightens a loose screw on a chair, straightens a crooked ball on the rack, pockets a bent nail.
  2. He jingles his key ring when he's deciding something. When he has decided, he closes his fist around it.
  3. He touches the trophy-case glass with one knuckle as he passes, without looking at it.

### Denise Lavoie

- **Age, appearance:** 61. Small and wiry, with a dark-dyed pixie cut that's growing out. Cat-eye readers. A red Kettle Lanes polo from 2011, faded pink at the shoulders. She owns a hat with earflaps and wears it to bowl when the building is cold.
- **Background:** Has worked the Kettle Lanes counter for 22 years, since 2004. Divorced from Roger, who remarried a dental hygienist in Laconia; she is entirely over it and enjoys proving so. Best woman bowler in town. Her team has finished second in the championship four times, twice to Linda's team. She holds the hardware-store pool sheet on Ellen. **Book 2 lead.** Seed only: one exchange with Gil Arsenault in Ch 08.
- **Wound:** Twenty-two years of being the most competent person in a building she doesn't own, for an owner who never asked her opinion. Always second.
- **Want:** To win the Linda Gagnon trophy before her knees give out.
- **Need:** To be asked. Ellen is the first owner who asks her anything, and Denise's skepticism dissolves the moment Ellen takes one of her suggestions and gives her the credit.
- **Greatest strength / how it's also their flaw:** She knows everyone's average, everyone's business, and every way the alley loses money. The flaw: she's so used to being right without being heard that she delivers advice as sarcasm. People hear the joke and miss the advice.
- **What they would never do:** Bowl badly on purpose, even to let a child win. Be cruel in the pool. It's a town sport, and she pulls a square if it starts to hurt someone. Leave the counter during a rush.
- **Speech pattern:** Talky where Ellen and Ray are spare: she runs sentences together, answers her own questions, and wanders into gossip before landing the point. One-liners and rhetorical questions. Calls Ellen "honey" when she's about to say something hard. Refers to people by their bowling averages ("the 98 in the green coat"). Never finishes a compliment without a qualifier.
- **Sample dialogue lines:**
  - "Honey, this town's had a pool on you since the closing. I'm just the one holding the sheet."
  - "He's a hundred-and-four average with a hundred-and-forty heart. Don't tell him I said either."
  - "Don't thank me. Raise my pay. Then thank me."
  - "That's a nice plan. Who's working the fryer while you're having it?"
- **Behavioral tics:**
  1. She does the newspaper crossword in pen at the counter and never looks up when she answers the phone.
  2. She sprays rental shoes with a precise two-count, toe and heel, and grades them out loud ("You'll live").
  3. She keeps a running tally of the snack bar in her head and corrects the register from memory.

### Hollis Pike

- **Age, appearance:** 66. Big through the chest, slow-moving, with a white beard trimmed square. Blaze-orange watch cap in every season. Wool pants, felt-lined boots, and a spud bar in the truck at all times. His reading glasses are mended with electrical tape, which he considers a permanent repair.
- **Background:** Retired after 28 years as a New Hampshire Fish and Game conservation officer. In winter he runs a contract plow route of back roads and private drives around the lake. Chairs the Great Kettle Derby committee. Ray's best friend since grade school, and his teammate (he bowls second). Never married. **Book 3 lead.** His Book 1 thread is getting the derby through the storm (Ch 09).
- **Wound:** Over 28 years he pulled people and snowmobiles out of lakes, and called off more derbies than he can count. He is the man who has to say "not safe" to a whole town, and he has been resented for it more than thanked.
- **Want:** One derby weekend where nobody gets hurt and nobody blames him.
- **Need (Book 3 territory, only hinted here):** To let someone look after him.
- **Greatest strength / how it's also their flaw:** He is procedural, patient, and impossible to rush. The flaw: he asks blunt personal questions the way he used to ask for a fishing license, and he doesn't notice that people find it alarming.
- **What they would never do:** Fudge a safety call. Let anyone drive onto ice he hasn't measured himself. Gossip. He asks you directly instead.
- **Speech pattern:** Questions, mostly. Weather and ice in exact measurements. No speculation; he says "Don't know yet" more than anyone in town. When he's being funny there's no change in tone.
- **Sample dialogue lines:**
  - "You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?"
  - "Inch and a half of black ice under four of white. That's walking ice. That's not truck ice."
  - "Ice doesn't care what you paid for the truck."
  - "What's Plan E?"
- **Behavioral tics:**
  1. He takes off his cap to think and puts it back on to speak.
  2. He writes everything in a pocket-size waterproof notebook with a golf pencil.
  3. He checks the sky before he answers any question asked outdoors, even a question about lunch.

### Arlene Fortier

- **Age, appearance:** 84. Five feet tall and straight as a broom. White hair set every Friday. A cardigan with tissues up the sleeve and a magnifier on a chain. Moves slowly and decides fast.
- **Background:** Bought Kettle Lanes in 1971 with her husband, Normand, who died in 1989. She ran it alone for 37 years, knowing it was slowly losing ground. She promised Ray, more than once and loosely, that he'd "get first crack." She sold to Ellen anyway. To make up for it, she made a right of first refusal for Ray a condition of the sale. She is carrying a $250,000 note with a March 1 balloon because her continuing-care community entrance fee is due when her unit opens. She is not a series lead; her thread closes when she's paid and moves (Ch 10).
- **Wound:** She watched the alley go stale under her own hand, and she watched Ray turn into the alley's curator after Linda died. She could not bring herself to sell to either kind of standing still.
- **Want:** Her money on March 1, a clean conscience, and the alley alive after her.
- **Need:** To tell Ray the truth about why she chose Ellen. She does, in Ch 08.
- **Greatest strength / how it's also their flaw:** Unsentimental clarity. She can price anything. The flaw: she makes kind decisions in unkind ways and lets people think the worst of her rather than explain.
- **What they would never do:** Forgive the note or extend it. She needs the money, and saying so plainly is a point of pride. Apologize in words; she apologizes with objects (the key tag).
- **Speech pattern:** Blunt, and old-fashioned without being folksy. Full names when she means it ("Raymond"). Uses "dear" as a weapon. Talks about money the way other people talk about weather. Every so often a Québécois phrase from her mother's kitchen surfaces ("Voyons donc" when she thinks you're being foolish). Keep it to one per scene, and never any word for God.
- **Sample dialogue lines:**
  - "You'd have kept it a museum. I wanted it to live."
  - "I sold a building, Raymond. I didn't sell you."
  - "Voyons donc. Nobody makes money on Tuesday nights. Normand knew it in 1979."
  - "Don't be sweet to me, dear, I'm eighty-four. Be on time."
- **Behavioral tics:**
  1. She licks her thumb and counts anything handed to her: bills, receipts, photographs.
  2. A police scanner runs on her kitchen counter, turned low, and she comments on the calls mid-conversation.
  3. She labels moving boxes in the same green-ledger handwriting she used for fifty-five years of books.

## RELATIONSHIPS

- **Ellen → Ray:** He's the most competent person in her building and the one person who wins if she fails. She respects his work, distrusts his motives, and is unsettled by how restful it is to stand next to someone who notices things before she says them.
- **Ray → Ellen:** She's the woman who took what he'd planned for the rest of his life by eight days, and the first person since Linda who makes him laugh out loud. He tells himself he helps her for the league. By Ch 04 he knows better.
- **Ellen → Denise:** First an obstacle with a crossword, then her best adviser. Ellen earns her by acting on what Denise says and saying so in front of people.
- **Denise → Ellen:** "Another owner who'll never ask me anything." Until Ellen asks. After that, fiercely loyal and still sarcastic.
- **Ray → Denise:** Old friends. She was Linda's rival and friend, and she's the only person who teases him about Linda without his flinching.
- **Denise → Ray:** She holds the pool sheet (he refused to buy a square, which she respects), and she's been waiting six years for him to want something new.
- **Ray → Hollis:** Best friends since grade school. Hollis asks the questions Ray won't ask himself.
- **Hollis → Ellen:** He treats her like a well-run operation: high praise, delivered as questions. He's the first in town to take her side, because her derby plan has contingencies.
- **Ellen → Hollis:** A fellow safety professional. They understand each other in a few words, which makes Ray jealous for a reason he can't name.
- **Denise → Hollis:** Teammates of the town. They bicker about who's the better bowler (her, by eleven pins of average) and have each other's backs.
- **Ray → Arlene:** Loves her like an aunt. He has been angry at her since the sale and hasn't said it, because she's 84.
- **Arlene → Ray:** Guilty and certain she did right. The key tag is her apology.
- **Ellen → Arlene:** The seller, the creditor, and the woman who chose her. Ellen doesn't yet know why, and is a little afraid to ask.
- **Arlene → Ellen:** She bet on Ellen and won't say so. She expects to be paid on time, as one professional to another.
- **Ellen → Kate:** Wants her daughter's respect for this decision, and knows Kate remembers the shifts Ellen worked.
- **Kate → Ellen:** Worried. Is this a choice or a crisis? She comes around when she sees her mother run the storm day.
- **Kate → Ray (Ch 09):** Reads him like a patient. Approves of him by the second sentence, then says something embarrassing about it.
- **Ray → Danny:** Proud of him, and in his way. Giving Danny the store outright is Ray stepping out of his own frozen life.
- **Danny → Ray:** Wants his father to have something of his own. He's the first to say "she's good for you," and he says it like an inventory note.
- **Ellen → Judy:** Respect. Judy is the only person in town who gives her clear criteria.
- **Judy → Ellen:** Likes her and can't help her beyond the numbers. Rooting for her within the rules.
- **Ellen → Verna:** Amused and slightly afraid. Verna is the only person who out-procedures her.
- **Verna → Ellen:** "A woman who can read minutes." High praise.
- **Ray → Gil:** A friendly rival of thirty years who just took two of his teams. He hates asking Gil for anything.
- **Gil → Ray:** Fond, smug, and fair.
- **Denise → Gil (seed):** Unimpressed by the overcoat. Quietly impressed that he remembered a spare she picked up in 2015.

## WORLD & SETTING

**Time and place:**
Ambrose, New Hampshire, an invented town in the Lakes Region on Great Kettle Lake (the name comes from the glacial "kettle" that formed it). About 2,300 year-round residents, swelling to roughly 9,000 in summer. The story runs from Tuesday, December 1 to Monday, March 1 (the weekdays match the 2026–27 calendar; the year is never named), with a short coda at April ice-out. Christmas happens off the page.

Town geography (invented; don't attach it to real state route numbers):
- **Main Street:** runs downhill to the town dock. Granite Lakes Savings Bank, Ambrose Pharmacy (Arlene's apartment upstairs), Leclerc's law office, the barber, the town hall with its plow-ban notices taped inside the door, and Gagnon & Son Hardware at the bottom near the dock.
- **Kettle Road:** runs along the north shore. Kettle Lanes sits on a rise across from the town beach, a quarter mile out of the center.
- **Dock Road and the public access:** where derby trucks park and Hollis measures the ice.
- **Halsey Falls:** the next town over, forty minutes away by the winter road. Home of Pine Ridge Lanes.
- Manchester, the Merrimack TRACON, and Logan are real and offstage. Mention them sparingly.

**Atmosphere:**
A town in its off-season, keeping itself going. Summer cottages shuttered with plywood. Plow trucks at 4 a.m. Smoke from woodstoves. A lake that turns into a neighborhood when it freezes: bob-houses in loose rows, trucks parked on the ice by people Hollis disapproves of. Everyone knows everyone's business, and most of them mean well about it. The cold is a working condition, not scenery. Snow is shoveled, plowed, measured, and complained about, and nobody calls it magical.

### Key Locations

**World rules:**
1. **No rescue money.** No windfall, inheritance, viral fame, celebrity, grant, or loan from Ray, Kate, or anyone else. Ray never puts money in. The final $30,850 / $25,600 comes from Ellen's own decisions.
2. **Nobody does work they aren't qualified for.** Ellen hires certified technicians and licensed contractors and holds the flashlight. Ray fixes pinsetters (he has twenty years of experience) and does not fix the boiler.
3. **The calendar is fixed** (see outline). Dec 1 is a Tuesday, Feb 27 a Saturday, March 1 a Monday. League nights are Tuesdays and Thursdays. Christmas is off the page.
4. **The league:** the Kettle Lanes Winter League, sanctioned under the state candlepin association and ICBA. It bowls in two divisions, Tuesday and Thursday nights, about 40 bowlers each (ten teams of four, plus substitutes), three strings per bowler, two teams per lane pair. Verna Tibbetts is secretary; Ray is the coach and unofficial commissioner. The championship (the Linda Gagnon Memorial) has traditionally been held on the last Saturday of February, with the top teams from both divisions on all ten lanes.
5. **Candlepin rules are real** (see Research notes). No one bowls 300. Nobody beats 245.
6. **Weather behaves like Lakes Region weather.** Nor'easters and February storms are normal. The book uses exactly two weather crises: the Ch 04 nor'easter and the Ch 09 storm. Cold is texture, never a third crisis. When the power goes out, the oil boiler goes out too (it needs electricity for the burner and circulator), so the building starts cooling.
7. **No secrets carried for more than a chapter.** Ellen tells Ray about the ledger at the start of Ch 06. The reader sees Ray's release in Ch 08, and Ellen learns of it in Ch 09.
8. **Nobody dies, falls ill, or has an accident to move the plot.** Linda's death is six years past and stays there.
9. **No villain.** The two teams who quit are hurt, not wicked. Gil is fair. The bank is the bank.
10. **Ellen and Ray need inner lives on the page (added after the Ch 01 cold read).** In every chapter, the POV character gets at least three short interior passages (2–4 sentences each) that show what they want, what they're afraid of, or what they remember. Write them plainly, in their own idiom, not as therapy-speak. Ellen's must, early and often, make clear why she bought the alley (the first thing she ever chose; a room full of people who need sequencing again) and what she fears (that her calm hides failure until it's too late). Ray's must make clear what the alley holds for him (Linda, the last unchanged room).
11. **Name the game.** Use the word "candlepin" in Ch 01 the first time Ellen holds a ball, with one natural line on how it differs from ordinary bowling (small ball, no holes, three balls a frame, fallen pins stay on the lane). After that, explain a rule only when it matters to the scene.
12. **Vary scene endings.** No more than half the scenes in a chapter end on a quip or a punchline. The others end on an image, an action, a silence, or an unanswered question.
13. **Distinct voices.** Dry deadpan belongs to Ellen and Ray. Secondary characters are not wits: Judy is plain and businesslike, and the Bench is funny through what it gets wrong, not through polished one-liners. Denise is talky and salty, Hollis asks questions and measures things, Danny is quick and transactional, Verna is formal and procedural, Arlene is blunt. If you could swap two speakers' lines without noticing, rewrite one of them.
14. **No convenient coincidences.** When chance helps or hurts the leads, a character must have arranged it. Example: Hollis puts Ellen on lane 5 for the ceremonial ball because Ray is on 6, on purpose.
15. **Every chapter moves the stakes on the page (added after the Ch 02 cold read).** Each chapter must visibly change the money, the deadline, or the town's opinion, in a scene, not in narration. Every scene needs a comic beat somewhere in its middle, not only at the end. Quiet, sad stretches run no longer than a page.
16. **Don't explain what a scene has shown.** Never end a scene or a paragraph with a line that states its meaning ("A man who meant to buy a building didn't…"). Trust the reader.
17. **Ration the running bits (added after the Ch 03 cold read).** A gag or motif that has landed three times is retired or changed. Retired: other characters being impressed by Ellen's readbacks. Ray's key ring appears at most once per chapter and never as a closing image. Don't let the building hand over symbols on cue: when a light, sound, or object lands on a meaningful name or word, someone must have caused it, or the moment passes with no reaction shot.
18. **Vary scene endings (added after the Ch 03 cold read).** No more than two scenes per chapter end on a still image or a placed object. Others end mid-motion, mid-conversation, or by cutting away early. Never let the last line of a scene be the tidiest line in it.
19. **Keep the leads opposed until Ch 07 (added after the Ch 03 cold read).** Ray helps because of the machines, the league, and Linda, and he still plans to own the alley on March 2. Every chapter through Ch 06 shows, in a scene, something the two of them want that the other can't give.
20. **Ellen doesn't win every round (added after the Ch 04 cold read).** In every chapter, at least one exchange that matters ends with Ellen having no good answer, being wrong, or paying a price for being right. Never hand her a competence beat (finding the panel in the dark, the perfect comeback) to settle an argument. The town pushes back for real; people don't fall in line after one grumble. Vary sentence length in dialogue: not everyone talks in four-word procedural lines, and some characters ramble, hedge, or repeat themselves.

**Clean rules (binding):**
- **Closed door, and more.** Ellen and Ray never spend a night together, on or off the page, and nothing implies they do. The Ch 04 overnight is chaperoned by the 31 stranded bowlers and the story should make that plain and funny. Nobody goes into a bedroom together.
- **Kisses only.** No more than four on-page kisses in the book: first kiss (Ch 05), the second at 3 a.m. waiting for the technician (Ch 06), the declaration (Ch 10), and optionally one in the coda. The outline's romance-beats table is authoritative. Describe them through context and consequence (mittens, the neon buzz, who speaks first), not through mouths, tongues, bodies pressed together, or heat. One embrace per kiss scene at most. No lingering descriptions of bodies; physical attraction is noticing hands, competence, a laugh.
- **No innuendo** beyond the gentlest double meaning that a 75-year-old church reader would smile at. No "pins" or "balls" jokes. Check every bowling sentence for accidental double meanings.
- **No swearing at all.** No "damn," "hell," "crap," or "God" or "Lord" or "Jesus" used as exclamations (including "Oh my God," "Lord knows," "Jeez," "Geez," "Cripes," and "Jeezum crow"). No censored swears ("f—"), and no "she swore" or "he cursed under his breath."
  - **Ellen's substitutes:** understatement and procedure. "Well. That's suboptimal." "Unable." "Negative." In real extremity: "Oh, for crying out loud." She says "Good grief" only when someone else has already said it.
  - **Ray's substitutes:** "For Pete's sake." "Son of a gun." "Holy mackerel" (once, in Ch 08 at Pine Ridge, and he hears himself say it). Mostly silence and a jingle of keys.
  - **Others:** Denise says "Oh, good grief" and "Lord love a—" is banned, so she says "Heaven help us" sparingly. Hollis says "Wicked" as an intensifier (a New Hampshire habit), and never swears. Arlene says "Voyons donc." Kate says "Mom."
- **No affairs, no infidelity,** no flirtation with married people. Gil and Denise are both single.
- **No alcohol scenes.** The snack bar is dry. Pine Ridge has a lounge in the background; no one in the cast drinks on the page.
- **No mockery of faith.** Church suppers and the Grange can exist without commentary.

### Money table (checked and revised)

**How the deal works (recommended framing; small fixes to the outline's mechanics):**
- Purchase price $390,000. Ellen put down $140,000 (savings plus the sale of her Merrimack house). Arlene carries a **$250,000 note, interest-only at 6% ($1,250 a month, due on the 1st), secured by a mortgage on the property, with the balloon due March 1.** Arlene is a private lender, so her lawyer set a real interest rate. That interest is a real monthly cash cost the outline didn't list.
- **Why so short:** Arlene's continuing-care entrance fee is due when her unit opens in early March. **Why Ellen agreed:** no bank would lend on Arlene's books. Judy told her plainly: "Run it one winter. Show me." The note is a bridge to that test.
- **Why Judy's numbers are $30,000 and $25,000 (make this the explicit logic in Ch 03):** At the small-business pace of the alley, the other nine months (spring, summer, fall) net out to about zero: leagues in spring offset slow summers and the autumn startup. So the winter quarter *is* the year. A 20-year, $250,000 loan at about 7.25% costs about **$23,700 a year** in payments. Judy's committee wants a **debt-service coverage ratio of 1.25**, and 1.25 × $23,700 ≈ **$29,600**, which she rounds to **$30,000** in winter net operating income. The **$25,000** is a liquidity requirement measured on Feb 28, before closing: working capital in the bank so a single bad month doesn't sink the loan. Both are standard community-bank criteria, applied to a seasonal business.
- **Why the default gives Ray the alley (recommended fix):** A default doesn't instantly return the building to Arlene; New Hampshire foreclosure takes weeks of notice. The cleaner and more plausible mechanism: **Arlene's sale was conditioned on a recorded right of first refusal for Ray, binding on any future sale of the property.** If Ellen can't refinance by March 1, the only way to pay Arlene in time is to sell, and any sale goes to Ray first. He has financing in hand (he keeps his lender's commitment alive), so he is the only realistic buyer at speed. The town's version: "If she misses, she has to sell, and Ray gets first crack." Use that line and skip any courtroom language.
- **Why Ray's $55,000 (Ch 07) genuinely closes the gap:** It does not raise net operating income. As an equity contribution, $48,000 would pay the note down to $202,000, which cuts annual debt service to about $19,160 and the income hurdle to about **$23,950**, under her $24,000 projection *without* raising league rates. The other $7,000 goes to working capital, lifting her Feb 28 cash projection to about $26,000, over the floor. It closes both gaps. That's why the offer is real and why refusing it costs her something. (Ray worked it out with Danny on the store's adding machine; he should be able to say the numbers.)
- **The refinance closing on March 1:** Plausible only if pre-staged. In Ch 03 Judy orders the appraisal and the environmental review (a 1958 building with an oil tank) right away, so that by March 1 the only open condition is the winter numbers. Ellen closes her books Sunday night from the register reports and bank statements, and Judy reviews them Monday morning. Approval, closing at Leclerc's office, and Arlene's payoff by wire all happen that afternoon. Don't skip the pre-staging line in Ch 03. (Author decision: no extension clause.)

## STYLE REFERENCE

*The author chose original samples written for this book over excerpts from published novels. The published books below are for reference only.*

**Bible-builder's recommendations (the author picks the actual paragraphs):**

1. **Helen Simonson, *Major Pettigrew's Last Stand*.** A later-in-life courtship in a small community, clean on the page, with dry social comedy. *Take:* grown-up restraint; comedy from manners and misunderstanding; feeling shown through what people refuse to say. Best for Ellen–Ray dialogue scenes and town set pieces.
2. **Richard Russo, *Nobody's Fool*.** A small New England town in winter, men who talk sideways, work and weather as daily texture. *Take:* the rhythm of town talk, the hardware-counter Bench, and Ray's interiority. *Caution:* the book has heavy profanity; take the rhythm only.
3. **Linda Holmes, *Evvie Drake Starts Over*.** Small-town Maine, two grown-ups with history, quick banter, warmth without syrup. *Take:* banter rhythm and how a scene turns from funny to tender in one line. *Caution:* a few swears; copy cadence, not vocabulary.
4. **Anne Tyler, *Redhead by the Side of the Road*.** A precise, routine-bound protagonist observed with affection; ordinary detail made exact. *Take:* the comedy of a person who orders the world, and the plain, specific sentences. Best for Ellen's interiority.
5. **Fredrik Backman, *A Man Called Ove*.** An exacting older person, deadpan comedy, tenderness under exasperation. *Take:* deadpan precision and running gags that pay off. *Caution:* avoid its sentimental crescendos, which are exactly the "Hallmark" risk this book must dodge.

## FORBIDDEN PHRASES

Never use these in prose. Flag and replace on sight.

- couldn't help but
- a wave of
- in that moment
- she/he realized
- his/her eyes widened
- a testament to
- the weight of
- piercing gaze
- heart pounding
- breath caught
- she/he found herself/himself
- seemed to feel

**Genre clichés ("Hallmark" sentiment):**
- small-town charm / quaint / cozy / bustling / picturesque / postcard-perfect
- felt like home / finally home / home is where
- the heart of the town / the heart of the community
- second chance at love / new chapter / fresh start / starting over
- for the first time in a long time
- butterflies (in her stomach)
- let out a breath she didn't know she was holding
- something shifted (between them)
- the air between them / electricity / electric / sparks flew
- a slow smile spread
- twinkle / twinkled / sparkle in his eyes / mischievous glint
- warmth spread through her chest / warmed her heart
- comfortable silence
- maybe, just maybe
- and somehow, that was enough
- snow began to fall (as a kiss begins), or any snowfall on emotional cue
- the whole town cheered / burst into applause (one ovation allowed in the book, at the Open, and it's for Denise)
- Christmas miracle / magic of the season

**AI-sounding habits (ban the pattern, not just the words):**
- tapestry, symphony of, dance (as a metaphor), palpable, unspoken, navigate (feelings), journey (emotional), delve, testament, resonate
- "It wasn't X. It was Y." and "Not X, but Y." Allow at most one per chapter.
- Groups of three adjectives or three parallel clauses. Allow at most one triplet per scene.
- Ending a scene or chapter on a one-line aphorism or moral. The last line of a scene should be an action or a line of dialogue.
- A beat / a long beat / after a beat, as a stage direction
- chuckled, smirked, grinned (no more than once each per chapter); padded (to the kitchen); nestled
- Em dashes: no more than about four per chapter in narration
- Starting consecutive paragraphs with the same word or with the character's name
- Characters explaining their feelings in complete therapeutic sentences ("I think I've been afraid of change since Linda died"). People in this book don't talk like that. They talk around it.
- Recapping what the reader just saw at the start of a scene or chapter

**Filler to cut (reader complaint #3):**
- **No meal-by-meal narration.** Food appears only when it serves the plot or the comedy (the Ch 04 snack-bar rations, the Ch 05 party pizzas as a logistics problem). Never describe what someone had for breakfast. No "steaming mug," no "aroma of freshly brewed coffee," no "hearty breakfast," no "nursing a cup of coffee."
- **No errands, driving, or getting dressed** unless something happens during them.
- **No weather openings** ("The snow fell softly on Ambrose...") unless the weather is the plot (Ch 04, Ch 06, Ch 09).
