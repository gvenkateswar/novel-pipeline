# Chapter Two: Right of First Refusal

The key cutter whined up to speed, and Ray fed the blank in against the guide.

The plow driver on the other side of the counter had snow in his eyebrows and half a key in his palm, snapped clean at the shoulder. Ray had clamped the broken half beside the new blank and was tracing the cuts by feel, one notch at a time. Out at the curb three trucks sat idling with their doors shut and their radios on. A dispatcher was asking somebody about the hill on Ledge Road.

"Clean through the third cut," said Normie, from his stool by the coffee pot. "No chatter. That's a veteran move, folks."

"He's slowing down on the tip," said the one beside him.

"He's always slowed down on the tip. That's style."

Ray took the new key to the wire wheel and ran both edges until the burrs came off. He tried it against the broken half under the lamp. The two lined up.

The driver paid in exact change, quarters last, and pointed his chin at the pallet of rock salt by the door. "Two bags on Ledoux's."

"I'll write it."

The bell went, and the cold came in around the driver's legs and settled on the floor.

Danny came in next with his tablet under his arm and snow on his fleece. He set the tablet on the counter, screen up, and went to hang his jacket. KETTLE LANES / REKEY / ALL EXTERIOR / 0800.

"Sorted the pin kit last night," Danny said from the back. "Front and back should be the same Schlage, if Arlene never changed them. Bulkhead's the Corbin if it's still on there, so there's a new cylinder in the box. Nine blanks on the hook. Cut them or I will."

Ray opened the job box anyway. The pin stacks sat in their compartments in order, each one in a twist of paper numbered in Danny's small print. He unwrapped the first and tipped the pins into his palm.

Danny came back, saw the box open, and went to the coffee pot without saying anything.

Behind the register, at the height of a man's eyes, the pool sheet was tacked to the paneling with four pushpins. KETTLE LANES. NEW OWNER. HOW LONG? Somebody had added two more rows along the bottom in a different marker when the first ones filled. The first square, DEC 1, was empty, and nobody was going to buy it now. Nearly every square after it held initials. The last, down in the right-hand corner, was empty. MARCH 1.

The bank's commitment letter on an SBA loan was folded in quarters behind his driver's license. Its date fell eight days after Arlene shook Ellen Daly's hand at her kitchen table. He knew the date without unfolding it. He still knew Linda's average, too. Paper carried like that wore through at the folds. He had carried it every day since anyway, the way a man keeps a part for a machine that's already been sold.

"Last one, Ray." Normie had come up to the counter. He held the pen out across it, the one that lived in the cup by the sheet, cap end first. "Five dollars. You know the date better than anybody."

Ray took the pen.

He pulled the account book over, found LEDOUX PLOWING on its page, and wrote out a slip. Two bags rock salt. He dated it, initialed it, and tore the white copy off for the drawer.

"And the veteran declines," Normie told the coffee pot.

Ray put the pen back in the cup, uncapped end down, and went to the back for his pinning kit.

* * *

The bell went at seven-thirty, and Ellen Daly came down the worn path in the floor with salt on her boots. She stopped at the pallet, lifted two bags of rock salt onto the counter, and set a sheet of paper on top of them.

Danny reached for the scanner. "Kettle Lanes account?"

"Yes. Put my cell on it." She read off ten digits, and Danny typed them in. "And I'd like a receipt."

"You'll get one either way."

The sheet was a printout from some candlepin forum, the banner a cartoon pin in a bow tie, and under it a list of part numbers with three lines run through in yellow highlighter. Ray didn't pick it up. He read it upside down.

"I'd like you to listen to something," Ellen said.

She took her phone out of her vest and laid it flat on the counter between the salt bags. Then she pressed play and took her hand away.

Ray closed his eyes.

"And he's closing the eyes, folks," Normie said, low. "This is where it happens."

On the phone a ball hit, and the wood went over. The sweep came down. Then the table, and under the table the sound Ray had been waiting for: the motor's pull went thin, like a saw finding a knot, and caught a half second late, and then let go entirely. Four seconds of hum. Nothing.

"Motor," Normie said.

"Belt," said the second man. "That's a belt."

"It's the new management," said the third, and the other two let that one go by.

Ray opened his eyes. Ellen hadn't touched the phone. It lay dead still on the counter like an exhibit. She hadn't explained the clip or apologized for bringing it, and she wasn't filling the quiet now. Linda used to talk right over a running machine. Ellen let it run.

"Play it again?" Ellen said.

"No need." He tapped the counter once beside the phone. "That's not the table. That's the clutch letting go a half second late. What you smelled was the facing. Motor quit to save itself."

"The clutch."

"Yes."

She looked at the forum list, at the highlighted lines.

"Half of those haven't been made since the seventies," Ray said. "Nobody makes a Bowl-Mor clutch. You get one off a place that closed."

"Understood."

"Leave the breaker on seven off, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms."

"Nobody reaches into the pit after a pin. Not a kid, not a captain. And nobody tries it once to see." He waited.

"The breaker on seven stays off," Ellen said. "No one reaches into the pit. No one runs it to see."

The Bench went quiet for that. Normie looked at the second man, and the second man raised his eyebrows over his coffee.

"There's a spare," Ray said. "On a nail in your pit. Third from the left over the bench. My writing on the tape. I took it out of a house that shut down in 2011, before they hauled the machines."

He stopped there and waited for the readback.

It didn't come. Ellen had an index card on the salt bag and a pen out, and she had already written it while he was talking. She turned the card around with one finger so he could read it. PIT / NAIL 3 FROM L / CLUTCH / R.G. TAPE.

He read it. It was right.

"So she's got the part," Normie said to the coffee pot. "And he knows where the part is. And I notice, folks, nobody has said one word about whose hands are going to put it in."

"Normie," said Danny.

"I'm just calling it."

Ellen put the card in her vest pocket. She picked up the receipt Danny tore off for her and read it all the way to the bottom before she folded it.

Danny lifted the job box onto the counter beside the salt. "Eight o'clock," he said. "You want me to take it?"

Ray picked up the pinning kit and the carton with the bulkhead cylinder and went out to his truck ahead of her.

* * *

He sat in the lot with the engine running. At ten to eight her car pulled in beside his truck, and she let herself in the front with her old key and did not look over. At eight by his watch he knocked, and stood on the outer mat with the kit at his feet.

Through the door glass the building ran away dark to the far wall. Down past the snack bar, a little gray light from the glass-block window band lay on the trophy case. Ellen came out of the office in her vest and unlocked the door from the inside, and only then did Ray pick up the kit.

He did the back door first, because it was the one Arlene always did first. Schlage, five-pin, as Danny had guessed. Cylinder out, plug pushed through onto the follower so the springs stayed in their chambers, old pins tipped into the tray. New pins in from Danny's twists, one chamber at a time, until the new key lifted every stack flush with the plug. The bulkhead was the Corbin, rusted into its housing. It came out only with a punch and would not be going back in. He put Danny's new cylinder in its place, tried the key six times, and left it.

The front door he saved for last. Ellen held it open with her hip and stood inside it.

He knelt on the mat with the plug in his hand and the tray beside his knee. Past Ellen's boots, down the length of the building, he could see the center shelf of the trophy case, and the frame with the 187 in it.

Everything down there was where Linda had left it. Her ball sat in locker 14, and the orange settees she had grumbled about for twenty years were still orange. He had meant to keep it that way. You did that with a good tool sometimes, left the edge alone because it was the last edge somebody else had put on it.

He seated the last pin, slid the plug home, and put the cylinder back in the door.

Then he took his own ring out of his jacket and found the brass key with the orange tag. R.G. — 1996, in Arlene's green ink. He put it in the lock. It went all the way in. It didn't turn. He worked it back out and let the ring hang from his fingers.

"I'll buy it from you," he said, without getting up. "Same number you paid. Your money down comes back to you, all of it. I take over Arlene's note. Closing costs are mine. The bank's already said yes to me once." He kept his eyes on the lock. "You'd walk away whole. You wouldn't lose a dollar."

Her phone rang in her vest.

"One minute," Ellen said.

She took the phone out and looked at it and stayed on her feet. "Kate. You're early. You usually call from the garage at quarter past."

Ray put his pin tray in order. Nothing in it had been out of order.

"I'm aware," Ellen said. She listened a while. "Ray Gagnon. The man with the right of first refusal. He's offered to buy me out." A pause. "Yes. Exactly what I paid."

Kate went on for some time, fast. He couldn't make out a word.

"Scale of one to ten," Ellen said, "I'm a three."

More.

"I heard you." She waited for whatever came after that. "Drive carefully. Go to sleep."

She put the phone back in her vest and did not look at it again.

"Unable," she said.

He looked up at her then.

"I worked thirty-one years," Ellen said. She said it slowly, with a space after each sentence. "I did not decide when to stop. A federal statute decided. I was fifty-six at the end of the month and I was done that day. This building is the first thing I have chosen since. I have owned it for one day. I am not going to sell it back to you on the second."

He had heard her talk like this once, on the step, over the note. It made each word sound like it had been checked.

"Your daughter thinks you should take it," he said.

"My daughter is a very good nurse. She thinks most people should take what they are offered and lie down."

Ray stood up. His left knee told him about the mat. He took the three new rings out of the kit, a set of three keys on each, and counted them into her palm one ring at a time.

"Front," he said. "Back. Bulkhead. Squirt graphite in the bulkhead before it freezes."

She closed her hand on them.

He weighed his own ring once, and the keys jingled against the orange tag, every key on it still good for something except one. Then he pocketed it, shut the kit, and snapped both latches.

"Then I'll see you March first," he said.

* * *

At twenty to six the store was shut and dark down the aisles. Only the lamp over the register was on. Up in the rafters the roof rakes and shovels hung in rows with the light gone off them.

Danny had the drawer out and was counting twenties onto the counter in stacks of five. Ray sat on the stool behind the register. The other stool, the one with the cracked green cushion, stood beside it where it always stood.

On the counter in front of Ray lay the Thursday league schedule, folded open to this week. Lane 7 had a match on it. The Highway Department and the Lakeview Diner.

"You going to fix it?" Danny said, without looking up from the twenties.

"Fella I knew in Tilton," Ray said. "Kept his neighbor's furnace going all one winter. Neighbor was a miserable old so-and-so. Wouldn't wave. Fella went over there every week and bled the lines and cleaned the nozzle and never got a thank-you."

"Okay."

"Their houses shared a wall. If the neighbor's furnace went, the fella's pipes froze."

Danny wrote a number on the bank bag. "I asked a yes-or-no question."

"You got a story."

"About pipes." Danny started on the tens. "Part's on the nail. You're the only one who can put it on." He squared the tens. "I'll put you down as a yes."

Ray pulled the tablet over, found the Kettle Lanes account, and copied her cell number into his phone. He typed, I can fix lane 7 if you want. He looked at it and deleted it. He typed, That clutch is a two hour job. He deleted that too.

What he sent was: Thursday league needs lane 7. I can do it tonight if you'll let me in.

He put the phone face down on the schedule. Danny counted fives.

The phone buzzed before Danny got to the ones. Ray turned it over.

2100. Back door. I'll unlock it.

Danny shut the drawer. "Take the good flashlight," he said. "Not yours."

* * *

At nine the back door opened from inside. Ellen stood there in her coat with her car keys already in her hand.

"Counter lights are on," she said. "The breaker on seven is off, and it's yours to reset. The office door is open. Leave the old part on the desk. This door locks when you pull it shut."

"All right."

"How long?"

"Two hours. About."

She checked her watch and stepped past him into the lot. She hadn't offered him a key, and he hadn't asked for one. He stood in the doorway until her engine turned over on the second try and her tires found the plowed track down to Kettle Road.

The building was cold already, the baseboard ticking along the wall.

He went up through the dark lanes toward the light at the counter.

The rooster box sat at the end of the counter where Arlene had always kept it. He flipped the lid out of twenty years' habit and walked his fingers back through the cards. L. M. Nothing under M at all.

He shut the lid.

Her tape was on the light switches now, where Arlene's had been, five columns of block capitals. On the pegboard the LOCKSMITH / 0800 card was still up, though the job was done. She kept what still had a use and changed what she had a reason to change. Out of a whole tin box of Arlene's cards she had kept the one that said she'd need him. He couldn't decide if that made the room better or worse.

On his way past the trophy case he touched the glass with one knuckle.

The steel door by lane 10 stuck, as it always had. He went through it into the pit and turned on the good flashlight.

The clutch was on its nail, third from the left over the bench, under his own tape. He took it down and turned it in the light. Then he lay down on the catwalk behind lane 7 and took the old one off. It came away with the facing glazed and cracked in two places, and it smelled like the inside of a toaster. He set it on the bench. The new one went on slower than it should have, because his hands were cold and the catwalk was colder.

He went out front, reset the PINSETTER 7 breaker, and came back and ran the machine empty. The sweep came down. The table lowered and rose. Seven seconds by his watch, and seven twice more.

He carried the old clutch up to the counter, took an index card off her stack, and found one of her Sharpies in the cigar box. He bent to her tape on the lane 4 switch until he had the spacing, then printed his own in her five columns, as square as he could make them.

07 / 2340 / MX / 1 / CLUTCH — SLIPPING. REPLACED.

He punched a hole in the corner with the tip of his pocketknife, ran a twist tie through it, and tied the card to the old clutch. In the office he set the clutch and its card in the middle of her desk, square to the edge, and left everything else on the desk exactly where it was.

On the way out he turned off the counter lights.

At the back door he pulled it shut behind him and tried the handle twice. Out at the road the sign buzzed on its pole. He walked to the truck with his hands in his jacket pockets, and in the right one, among the others, was Arlene's key, which opened nothing.

* * *

At five past six the plow truck at the curb was still idling, and the Bench was up on its stools. Ray had the drawer in and the coffee on when his phone buzzed on the shelf under the register.

A photograph: his card, on her desk, still tied to the clutch, the twist tie neat. Under his line she had added one more, in block capitals, smaller than his and perfectly level.

REMARKS: INVOICE TO E. DALY.

He looked at it for a while. Then he typed with one thumb.

I didn't fix it for you. Thursday league's got a match.

He sent it.

"That her?" said Normie, who had come up to the counter for a refill and was reading upside down.

From down the plumbing aisle, where he was restocking elbows out of a carton, Danny said, "You going to bill her?"

Ray didn't answer either one.

He looked up at the pool sheet behind the register. The squares were nearly all full, across and down. The first was empty, and so was the one in the corner. MARCH 1. The pen stood in its cup where he had left it, uncapped end down. A man who meant to buy a building didn't put five dollars on the day it came up for sale.

The bell went. A plow driver came stamping in with a key in his hand and snow on his shoulders. Ray turned from the sheet, took a blank off the board, and started the cutter.
