# Chapter 9 — Car Deck, No Lights

**Day 8 to Day 9 — Friday night to Saturday**

*Day 8, twenty to five*

Ned brought Russ over the gangway and didn't follow him aboard.

He stood at the pier end of the brow with his duffel on his good side and looked up once at the wheelhouse glass. Then he turned and went up the stone pier at his hip's pace, past the wreck of the slip, and on into the dark of the Harbor Road. Anyone on the hill with a pair of binoculars would see the captain walk off his own boat with his bag. Ned's boat looked like Ned's boat. The floods were on. The two trucks stood chained in the orange light, the fire company's propane truck and the store's box truck, and nobody was on deck.

In the crew mess Russ leaned on the doorframe. The heater rail ticked, and the room smelled of burnt coffee and everybody's wool.

Dane sat with his back to the bulkhead. He didn't take long.

"The shot at the landing yesterday came off the *Margaret Ann*'s skiff. The shooter's Shane Doucette. Sternman. Quist ran the crew list. He's been ashore since. Somebody talks him around on a radio."

Russ looked at Beth.

"Tess," she said. "The back door. The Ashby place. The landing. All of it went through the store."

Russ took off his cap. He held it in both hands by the brim and looked into it a while, and then he put it back on.

"Jolene's cousin's boat," he said. "That's family, if you squint." He shifted his weight on the frame. "Last time there was trouble in my family I was the last man on Cutter to hear about it. Found out from the mail boat." He looked at Dane, not her. "Somebody ought to arrest somebody on this island before your people get here and do it for us. I figure it might as well be the constable."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't meant them to.

"What do you carry," Dane said, "and who trained you?"

"M&P. Part-time officer course at the academy, three summers back. I can arrest on Cutter. Never had to do it with the lights out." Russ touched his belt. "Cuffs."

Dane looked at the hand on the belt, then at Russ's face, and nodded once.

Beth stood. "Up top."

On deck the wind came at them out of the northwest, dry and cold, with the whole clear sky behind it. The harbor was already going flat in long dark streaks.

"Russ. Inside the gate, in the shadow of that door." She pointed at the deckhouse door, the black square beside the gangway. "The pier's the only way in from land. You're the pier."

He went and stood in it. The shadow took everything but his cap brim.

"Dane. Between the trucks." She walked him into the alley. Propane tank on one side, the aluminum box on the other, a yard and a half of black between. "From the aft end you have the stern gate. From the side you have the door and the foot of the ladder. I'm in the wheelhouse with every light on."

"And the signal."

"If the floods go dark, it's the water. That means the stern. The ramp's up, and this side's on the stone. There's nowhere else."

He looked aft at the gate and its chain, then up at the lit wheelhouse.

"Walk me through it," he said.

His left hand was tucked into the front of his soft-shell, the fingers just showing at the zip. He held out the right.

She took it. She laid it on the box truck's rear binder, and he closed his fingers over the cold handle and its pin. She moved it down to the D-ring flush in the plate, and he had to crouch, and she crouched with him. Then the lip of the chain bin against the bulwark, rough with rust, at the height of his knee.

"That's where your feet are," she said.

She left her hand over his on the lip of the bin. He didn't take his away. Neither did she, for as long as it took the wind to drop and come back.

Then she stood and went to the outside ladder. The floods threw her shadow up the deckhouse ahead of her, rung by rung, long and broken at the wheelhouse rail. Below her Dane stepped back between the trucks, and the dark took him.

---

*Day 8, half past five*

Her thumb was on the fixed set's channel knob.

She turned it down from sixteen, click by click, and stopped on nine.

Not the Icom. The fixed set carried, and it sounded like a boat. She lifted the mic off its hook.

Her own voice. Low. No hurry in it.

She keyed. "Store, store. *Ruthie C*."

Her father's call. The bank had the boat. The words were still hers.

The set opened almost before she'd let go.

"*Ruthie C*, go ahead." Fast and bright, and then the rest of it came in a rush. "Bethie. Christ on a bike. I've been ticking you every morning on faith, hon. Where are you?"

"Count me for six, Tess. Keep it off the air."

*Keep it off the air.* The Day 1 words. Tess had carried them once already. She'd carry them again.

"Course I will. Where are you?"

"On the boat. By myself tonight."

"Where's Ned?"

"Gone up to Russ's with his hip. You know him. Won't sleep on that settee another night."

Tess laughed. It came a half second late.

"And your marshal?"

Beth kept her eyes on the dark glass. "Down with the arm. Running a fever. I left him where he's warm."

Not where. Never where.

"Oh, hon."

"I'm up here till the pumps cycle at midnight. Ned left the deck lights on. You know Ned."

Alone. The gun down. The captain ashore. The deck lit. A window till twelve. She'd left out two things, and she ran them once to be sure: where Dane really was, and the man in the shadow by the gate.

She had one more thing to say, and it wasn't for whoever stood behind Tess.

"*Ruthie C*, clear."

She let go of the key. Her thumb stayed on it.

The speaker hissed. Somebody at the store had keyed and was holding it. The carrier ran open, a dry empty rush, the sound of a woman in a back office with the mic up at her mouth and nothing coming out.

Beth wiped the grille of the mic on her sleeve.

"Okay, Bethie."

---

*Day 8, six o'clock to eleven*

At six she turned the set to sixty-eight and listened.

"Cutter Island storm net, this is net control." Tess's net voice, warm and steady, a woman reading a list she loved. "Good evening, island. Dyer, Ida, by phone, counted. Grindle, Walter—"

Tess went through the island house by house. She came to the C's and went past them without a pause.

Beth turned the knob back to nine.

Then the wait.

She did what a woman alone on watch would do. She wiped the forward windows with Ned's rag and left them smeared, gray arcs the width of her arm. She opened the bilge pump panel and read the run lights and shut it. She sat in Ned's chair, which had his shape and not hers, and put her boots on the rail.

Outside it had gone clear and bitter. The harbor lay flat. The *Margaret Ann* sat on her mooring off the fish pier with her deck lights burning white, and up the hill the store had one window lit, the generator's. The stars came out over the northwest wind, so many and so sharp that the sky looked crowded.

From the chair she could see a sliver of Russ's cap brim at the gate. Of Dane she could see nothing at all. That was right.

She didn't go down. The set hissed on nine and gave her nothing.

At eleven it opened.

"Bethie." Tess, low and fast, close to the mic. "Water side—"

A clatter. Something knocked against the desk at the store end. Then the carrier, open and dead, and then not even that.

Beth's palm was flat on the console. The steel hummed with the generator below her.

*Water side.* Not the pier. The stern, and the port quarter ladder, the only way aboard from the water.

And the cut. Somebody had taken that radio out of Tess's hand in the middle of the word. He was in the store. He'd heard her.

She was out the wheelhouse door before the set went quiet. She leaned over the wing rail and spoke straight down into the dark by the gate, not loud.

"Russ."

The cap brim tipped up.

"Tess warned us. He's in the store with her. Go now. The water's ours. Call on sixteen when it's done."

He didn't ask. He was off the brow and running up the stone pier, his boots slapping, one hand on his hip to keep the holster still.

What Tess had just done would get her killed tonight. And two could hold a boat whose only open door was now the stern.

She went back in and opened the lighting panel on the aft bulkhead. She put her hand on the car-deck flood breakers.

She threw them. Below her the deck went black. With the same hand she killed the wheelhouse lights, and the glass went black too, and the room she'd lit like a lantern for six hours wasn't there anymore.

---

*Day 8, eleven o'clock*

The box truck's aluminum was cold through two layers at his back, and Dane let it be cold.

She had put him here, and here was good. The alley ran fore and aft between the two trucks, and from its after mouth he had the stern gate as a lit rectangle of steel and chain, with the black water beyond it. To his left, past the propane truck's tank, the deckhouse door and the foot of the outside ladder. A man coming over the port quarter would have to come forward past that mouth to reach either one, close enough to touch.

Hiking boots on steel plate. Wrong for a deck, and he'd known it all week.

The left arm lay inside his soft-shell where he had zipped it, useless above the shoulder, the hand at the end of it good for nothing that needed a grip. The Glock was in his right hand, low along his thigh, his finger laid straight along the frame.

He heard Russ go. Boots on the brow, then on stone, at a run, going away up the pier. Russ was not a man who ran for nothing. Dane didn't know why, and stayed where he was.

Out on the flat water an outboard was running, well off, a small engine pushing a light boat. Over water this still, a man could hear a cough a mile away. The note came steadily nearer and then cut.

Quiet, and wind.

Then a stroke. A paddle going in clean and coming out, and after it the drip off the blade, a small sound and then a smaller one. Another stroke, nearer. He stopped trying to see and listened to the sound change: the splash going sharper and closer, the drip arriving before the next stroke instead of after it, the hull beginning to talk back as it came under the stern's shadow. Whoever it was knew boats and was in no hurry.

The floods went out.

The lit frame at the end of the alley was simply gone, and so was everything else, the trucks, his own hand. She'd been warned, then, by someone, and she had done what she'd said she would do. She was coming down.

He did not move. A man who moves blind on a deck he doesn't know makes noise, and noise was hers to use tonight, not his. Whatever came, he would not fire into a dark she was somewhere in.

Four years ago a woman had asked him for ten minutes, and he had given them to her because he decided he knew better than the plan. That had been a rule he bent. This was not that. Beth had walked him through it and put his hand on the steel and told him where his feet were. This was a position she had given him, and holding it was the whole of his job.

He thought he would have liked very much to go up that ladder and find her. He kept his back on the cold aluminum.

Shane Doucette. Twenty-nine. Sternman on the *Margaret Ann*, a mainland address that went nowhere, a bad left leg nobody had written down the cause of. A man who had fired off a moving skiff at sixty yards and opened Dane's arm, and who had been talked along the Harbor Road by radio in the small hours because he didn't know the island.

He didn't know this boat either.

His eyes began to give him the sky over the stern, pale and thick with stars, and nothing below it.

Aluminum touched steel at the port quarter. A soft knock, and the scrape of a hull being held against the plate.

A boot found the quarter-ladder rung. Then the deck plate, heavy on one side, and the heel rang on the steel.

Dane didn't move.

---

*Day 8, ten past eleven*

She came down the inside companionway with her hand on the rail and her boots in her other hand. She set them inside the deckhouse door and stepped out in her wool socks onto the plates. Her gloves went in her float coat pocket, against the Icom.

Steel under her soles. Cold enough to ache.

The foot of the outside ladder was under her left hand. From here to the stern gate was thirty-four steps. Eleven years of them.

She listened.

A heel rang on the plates aft. Then nothing, and a soft drag, and the heel again. Heavy on the right. The left leg coming after it, careful, never trusted.

She knew the walk. She had known it since the night freight.

He was coming forward along the port bulwark. Toward the trucks. Toward the ladder. Toward where she had told the whole island she would be.

She went down on one knee by the ladder foot and put her palm on the plate. The plate told her each step.

A binder rang, struck by a passing shin. He stopped. He came on.

He was at the alley mouth.

Dane came out of the dark. She heard it: a body against a body, a grunt, both of them going down hard. Chain jumped and rang in the box truck's tie-downs, and kept ringing.

Somebody was fighting the chain and losing. One-handed. She knew the sound of a sleeve fouled in a binder run, and Dane's left arm was the one in it.

The ringing changed. A man was getting up off the plates. Only one.

Beth was already moving aft along the port bulwark, her right hand on the cap. Bin lip. D-ring. The seam in the plate where they'd welded a patch the winter her father died. She stopped well aft of the trucks.

"Shane."

She moved the moment it was out of her mouth.

The shot was huge in the steel box of the deck. The flash showed her everything for a blink: the bulwark, the plates wet with dew, the box truck's tail, Dane down in the chains with the Glock under him, and a hooded shape turned toward the place she'd been.

Then black, and the ringing in her ears.

"*Fuck*—Beth!"

Dane, from the chains. She didn't answer him.

The heel came aft after her, fast and uneven. Right, and the left dragging, slipping on the dew. She backed along the bulwark cap with her palm on it and her feet reading the plates. The cold curve of the ring buoy bracket under her fingers. She went past it.

The gatepost hit her hip. She stopped against it, and turned her shoulder to the sound.

He was close. She could hear his breath. The right heel landed, and then his weight came over onto the bad left leg to make the last step.

She drove her shoulder into his hip.

He went back against the gate chain at the height of his thighs. It held and he didn't. He went over it.

Something hard hit the deck plates and skidded, a long metal scrape forward into the dark. The gun. Still aboard.

The water took him.

Out of the black below the transom came a sound she had heard twice in her life, a huge, sucking whoop, a man's whole chest pulling in at once. Then silence while he was under.

She had the ring buoy out of its bracket. She didn't decide to. Her hands knew the bracket.

He came up with the second gasp, choking, off the port quarter. She threw at it. Ninety feet of line ran out through her bare palms, burning. She heard the buoy slap the water.

"It's at your head. Arm through it."

Thrashing.

"Arm through it. Both arms. Now."

The line went taut and jerked.

"Kick. Keep your face up. Kick."

She sat back on the line and took it in hand over hand, the wet rope stiffening in the cold, her scarred palm under it. Forty-eight degrees. He had a minute of breath, and ten of hands. She meant to use none of the ten.

She worked him aft along the hull to the recessed quarter ladder and held him there against the plate.

"Rung's at your right hand. Hold it."

Behind her, a ratchet clicked.

Dane had found the binder's release in the dark with his good hand, the thumb on the pawl and the handle back. He had watched her do it every morning for a week. The chain fell slack with a long rattle.

He came aft along the bulwark by feel, and his hand found her shoulder, and then the line.

Doucette couldn't climb. They lay flat on the plates, took him under the arms and brought him up the rungs between them, both his legs gone to nothing. They tipped him over the gate chain onto the deck. He lay there shaking so hard his teeth clattered against the steel.

Dane knelt on his back and cuffed him one-handed, the ratchet of the cuffs loud and small.

Beth went forward to the crew cabin in the dark and came back with the gray wool blanket off the lower bunk and put it over him.

The Icom woke in her float coat.

"*North Reach*, Whitcomb." Russ's voice, grim and formal. "One in custody at the store. Tess Marr is unhurt. Over."

She took the radio out and keyed it. "Whitcomb, *North Reach*. Copy one in custody, Tess Marr unhurt. One in custody aboard. Standing by one-six."

Dane's good hand came up to her jaw, cold, and he kissed her once and let her go, and went back to the man on the plates.

She walked forward to the deckhouse door, found the ramp-light switch by touch and pushed it up.

The sodium lamps over the ramp warmed slowly, first a dull pink and then orange, and the light came back down the car deck over everything she'd been holding blind. Doucette shaking under the gray blanket at the stern gate. The gun on the plates where it had stopped, against a chock. And the chain in the box truck's tie-down, slack between the binder and the D-ring, still ringing faintly where Dane had torn it loose.

---

*Day 9, one o'clock, and first light*

Russ brought Lyle Arsenault down the stone pier in cuffs.

Clean-shaven. The good Musto jacket, zipped to the chin. He walked like a man going to look at a boat he might buy, and he said nothing at all.

Tess walked beside them in her red vest. Ned walked on her other side, his duffel gone, his hands in his pockets. He didn't touch her. There was a foot of cold air between his sleeve and hers, all the way down the pier.

"Town office has no power," Russ said at the foot of the gangway. "You've got heat, light, steel, a lock and a federal officer."

"How'd it go?" Dane said.

"He had her by the wrist and the radio by its cord." Russ shifted his weight. "I asked him for both."

He looked across the harbor at the *Margaret Ann*, white-lit on her mooring. "Other hand's still aboard her with the lights on. He's not going anywhere. Marine Patrol can have him."

Dane took Arsenault down to the crew mess, where Doucette sat by the heater under the blanket, and stayed with them both.

"She isn't under arrest," Russ said, low, to Beth. "I'm not sure what she is."

Tess stood at the foot of the gangway. There was a red band around her right wrist, darker at the bone. Beth stood at the top. Twelve feet of aluminum brow lay between them.

Tess's hand came up toward Beth's braid, the old reach, and stopped in the air between them, and came down.

Beth didn't step back. She didn't step closer.

"Russ, put her in the passenger cabin. The stove's lit."

Toward three, with Russ in the mess doorway, Dane stood on the open deck under the stars with the Iridium and gave Pike the night in order. Relief would come the first hour the sea allowed. The sea already allowed it.

At first gray Ned climbed the ladder to the wheelhouse. He hadn't brought coffee.

The harbor lay flat as a floor, the northwest wind dropping to nothing. Through the smeared glass Beth could see down into the passenger cabin window. Tess sat upright on the bench by the stove with her hands in her lap, looking out at the water.

Ned took the mic. He turned the knob on the fixed set, up from nine, click by click, to sixteen. Beth let him.

"All stations, all stations, this is the Cutter Island ferry *North Reach*." His radio voice, clipped and correct. "First departure Sunday at first light. Passengers and light freight. *North Reach* standing by one-six and one-three."
