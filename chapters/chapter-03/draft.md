# Chapter Three: Deadwood

Judy Ostrowski wrote two numbers on a clean sheet of paper, uncapped a blue pen, and drew a circle around each one.

"Two numbers," she said. "I'll circle them so neither of us can pretend later."

"You've circled them."

"I like to say it while I do it. Sit down, Ellen."

Ellen stayed on her feet. Judy's office had glass on three sides, and through the glass the lobby was all marble and brass and a calendar of loons. Out at the drive-up the pneumatic tube landed with a thunk. Judy waited with a butterscotch in her hand. When the waiting got her nowhere, she turned the sheet around so it faced the right way.

"Here's how the underwriter sees you. Your other nine months net out to about nothing. Spring leagues pay for summer, summer people pay for fall, and it all washes. So the winter is the year." She tapped the paper. "The new loan costs about twenty-four thousand a year in payments. The committee wants a quarter more than that coming in, so there's room for one bad thing. That's thirty thousand, net operating income, December first through February twenty-eighth. And twenty-five thousand in the bank on the twenty-eighth, so one bad month doesn't sink you."

"And if I come in at twenty-nine?"

"Then the committee says no, and I say I'm sorry, and I mean it." Judy started unwrapping the butterscotch. "I like you. The underwriter has never met you. He's met Arlene's last three winters."

Ellen looked at the circles. Nobody had handed her anything this clear since she turned in her headset: a number to make or miss, in ink, with a date beside it. She had missed being measured. A bank was an awkward place to find that out.

"Net operating income of thirty thousand dollars," she said, "for the period December first through February twenty-eighth, inclusive. Cash on deposit of twenty-five thousand dollars as of the close of business February twenty-eighth. Failure on either one is a decline."

The butterscotch stopped halfway out of its wrapper.

"Nineteen years," Judy said. "Nobody has ever read my criteria back to me. Mostly they ask if I can round."

"Can you?"

"No."

Behind Judy's head hung a framed photograph of Judy in a snowmobile suit, on ice, holding a fish nearly as long as her leg.

"How much did it weigh?"

Judy didn't turn around. "Eleven pounds, four ounces. The underwriter didn't believe that either."

"Which item has the longest lead time?"

"The appraisal. Then an environmental review, because you've got a 1958 building with an oil tank in the basement and the committee likes to know what's in the ground." Judy finally ate the butterscotch. "If you want to close on March first, order both now. Then the only thing still open on March first is your winter."

"Order them today."

Judy wrote APPRAISAL / ENVIRO on a yellow pad and underlined TODAY twice. "I'll call the appraiser before lunch. He'll want to see the boiler."

"Everybody does."

Ellen sat down then, for the four minutes it took to sign the engagement letters.

On her way out she stopped at the teller window and asked for a second business account. The teller wore a tie clip shaped like a loon. He slid the form under the glass.

"It's for money I'm only holding," Ellen said. "Meals tax from the snack bar. The league's prize fund."

"Would you like a nickname on the statement? A lot of people put 'Rainy Day.'"

She took the pen on its little chain and printed in block capitals: HELD. NOT MINE.

Then she slid the form back under the glass, and the teller turned it around to read it.

* * *

The steel door by lane 10 gave on the second shove, and Ray came through it sideways with a carton under each arm.

"Place up past Plymouth closed in October," he said. "Fella's daughter was selling the machines for scrap. I got there Monday before the truck did."

He set the cartons on the workbench beside the coffee can of bolts. Half the parts in them already wore masking tape in his square capitals.

"How much?"

He told her. She wrote the check against the bench without looking up from it, tore it out, and held it out to him.

"Now the calls," she said. "You keep the machines running. I pay a fair rate per call. On an invoice."

"Never charged Arlene."

"That's the problem. Arlene's books look better than they were by exactly what you're worth. I'm not carrying a number I know is wrong." She waited. "Lane 7. Last Wednesday. Invoice me."

He looked at her for a while. Then he took a pad of Gagnon & Son sales slips out of his jacket, licked the carpenter's pencil from behind his ear, and wrote on the bench. LABOR / LANE 7 CLUTCH. He wrote a number under it and turned the pad toward her.

"That's what you charge to cut a key," Ellen said.

"About what it took."

"It took two hours and forty minutes on a cold catwalk. I have your card." She took the pencil out of his hand, crossed out his number, and wrote one above it.

"That's too much."

She crossed out her own number and wrote a higher one.

"Now you're going the wrong way, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms. And I'm going the right way. You're the one going the wrong way."

"That's not how anybody does this."

"Object again and it goes up again."

He studied the slip, three numbers stacked like rungs. "You're bad at this."

"I'm accurate at this."

He tore off the white copy and handed it to her, and she folded it into her vest pocket.

Then she took her own key ring out, worked one back-door key off it with her thumbnail, and set it on the bench. Not in his hand. On the bench, beside the coffee can.

From the same pocket she produced an index card and a pushpin and pinned the card to the pegboard beside the pit door. She had ruled it the night before. R.G. — AFTER HOURS. Under the heading, five columns: DATE / TIME IN / TIME OUT / LANE / REASON.

"Back door," she said. "Any night after close. I don't need to be here and you don't need to call first. You write it down. Every visit, in your own hand."

Ray looked at the card. He looked at the key. He didn't pick it up.

"Readback," Ellen said.

He took his time with it. "Back door. After close. Write it on the card." He picked up the key. "Every time."

It was complete and in order. She nodded once.

He took out his ring and threaded the new key onto it, beside the brass one with Arlene's orange tag. The ring jingled once against the bench. Then he closed his fist around it.

* * *

On Thursday at two-thirty an electrician stood on a ladder over lane 6 with three screws in his mouth and a black-light fixture held up against the ceiling tile. Denise had the crossword folded on the counter and did not look up from it.

"He says six o'clock, which means eight, which means tomorrow at four you're up that ladder yourself in your good shirt, and don't look at me, I don't do ladders, I did a ladder for Arlene in 2009 and I've been five foot one ever since. Eleven letters, 'mutual agreement.' Roger had a ladder. Roger had a lot of things, and now he's got a dental hygienist in Laconia, and she does his teeth now, which, fine, somebody should." She wrote. "Concordance. Here's the thing, though. You can't bowl."

"I'm aware."

"The league's aware. The league has eyes. They watched your first ball go into lane 6, and they will not respect an owner who can't bowl. They'll be polite to her, which is worse."

"Who's going to teach me?"

"Honey, who do you think?"

Denise reached under the counter without looking and slid a form across with the pen on top. LEAGUE SANCTION CARD. "Fill it out. So you're legal if they're ever short."

"Short of what?"

"Bowlers, honey. Not money." She glanced at the register. "Well."

Ellen filled it in, in block capitals. Denise turned it with one finger and inspected it the way she inspected rental shoes after the spray.

"You'll live."

"Testing," the electrician said, through the screws, and one fixture came on over lanes 5 and 6.

The light ran violet down the pair and out across the counter. On Denise's red polo a handprint appeared on the left shoulder, bright as paint.

"That's Roger," Denise said, still writing. "Fourth of July, 2011, mustard, I've washed that shirt forty times. Fourteen Across. Look at your menu board."

Between HOT DOG and FRIE, where the S had fallen out years ago, a clean ghost of an S glowed on the felt. Out in the vestibule every bean-supper flyer on the corkboard had lit up like a pharmacy sign.

"And the Grange prints on the brightest paper in New Hampshire," Denise said. "Seven letters, 'exposed.' Revealed. It's a theme."

Ellen turned to the strip board. Six party bookings for the week, each on its card in its slot. Two of them overlapped on lanes 1 through 4 on Wednesday, and one, a dentist's office with eleven employees, claimed a head count of 140. She knew exactly why she had bought this building. It filled up with people who all arrived at the same minute and all wanted lanes, and somebody had to put them in order, and nobody else in Ambrose wanted the job.

"Office parties want five to seven," Denise said, to 22 Down. "So the husbands and wives can come after work and the boss can leave before anybody gets loud. Arlene put them at seven and wondered why they never came back."

Ellen took down the two overlapping strips, changed them to 1700, and printed PER D. LAVOIE in the REMARKS column of each. She pinned them back where Denise could read them.

Denise looked at the strips longer than she had looked at anything all afternoon.

"Your sevens look like ones," she said.

Ellen's phone was in her vest. She typed with both thumbs. LESSONS 0700 MONDAY. RATE PER OUR AGREEMENT.

The reply came while the electrician was climbing down. I don't charge for bowling. Tell you my price Friday.

"Stand back," the electrician said, and threw the switch for the whole bank.

Down the length of the building the trophy case went violet. Fifty years of tarnish disappeared into the light, and in the center of the case the card in its frame glowed white. WOMEN'S HOUSE HIGH SINGLE. 187. LINDA GAGNON.

Denise stopped writing.

* * *

By ten to nine on Friday all ten lanes were running under black light, and the waiting list was on its own strip with four names on it. Denise worked the register and corrected it from memory twice without looking down.

"Snack bar's done a league night's business and it's not nine," she said. "Don't get excited. And the popcorn machine and the space heater can't both be on, so somebody's going to be cold or somebody's going to be hungry, and I've made my choice."

Ellen walked the floor from lane 1 to lane 10 and back. Fifty-two, under her breath. Fifty-two people bowling in a building that had refunded three lineages ten days ago.

At 9:40 lane 3's table started coming down late. She watched it twice to be sure, then switched it off, apologized to the birthday party on it, and moved them to the top of the waiting list with a free basket of fries.

"You calling Ray?" Denise said.

"No."

"Good. I texted him."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You didn't ask me not to. And I didn't call him, I texted him, which is different. A call is a favor. A text is just information."

At 11:20, after close, the back door opened. Ray stood at the pegboard with his pencil and wrote on the strip before he did anything else. 12/11. 2320. TIME OUT left empty. 03. TABLE LATE. Then he went through the steel door into the pit.

Ellen gave him five minutes and followed.

The electrician had run one fixture over the masking at the lane ends, and it lit the catwalk violet through the machines. Ray's canvas jacket glowed. So did the pencil behind his ear. Every strip of tape he had ever written on, twenty years of PIN ELEVATOR 4 and BELT, SPARE and DON'T, hung glowing on its nail along the wall.

He stood looking at them.

"Nobody told me the tape did that."

"It's been doing it the whole time," Ellen said. "You never had the lights for it."

"Those staying?"

"Fridays and Saturdays."

He thought about that with his hands in lane 3's table. He loosened something, listened to it, and tightened it a quarter turn.

"My price," he said, to the table. "I'll teach you. Mornings, before the store. And the championship stays the way it's always been. Last Saturday in February. All ten lanes. The Linda Gagnon Memorial. Nothing glowing."

He reached for the motor switch.

Ellen spoke from the pit doorway. "You have my word. The championship stays as it is, the last Saturday in February, all ten lanes, unless the numbers say otherwise."

He threw the switch as she got to all ten lanes. The table dropped, and the sweep came down with its thunk across the end of the sentence. He was watching the table, lips moving, counting the cycle.

"That's all I'm asking," he said to the machine.

He wiped his hand on a rag from his back pocket and held it out. She shook it.

He had not said it back to her. She noticed that he hadn't, and his hand was already out, and she took it.

"Monday, then," Ray said. "Seven."

"Affirmative."

The table rose on its seven-second cycle. Ray watched it come up level. Ellen went out front and turned off the black lights, one bank at a time.

* * *

At seven on Monday morning only lanes 5 and 6 had their lights on. The rest of the building ran off into the dark, and the baseboard ticked along the wall as the heat came up. Somebody's glow-night confetti still lay in the gutter of lane 6.

Ellen had a clipboard. On it was a diagram she had drawn the night before with a protractor, three lines of approach converging on the gap between the 1 pin and the 3. Her reading glasses hung on their cord.

Ray, in his own shoes, looked at the clipboard for some time.

"Stop doing math at the pins."

"I'm not. I'm doing it at the clipboard."

He handed her a house ball. It fit in her palm like a grapefruit, no holes, and weighed nothing she could feel through her wrist.

Her first throw sailed, touched down well past the lob line, and skipped twice like a flat stone on a pond. The foul light came on and the pins didn't count. So did the second. So did the third, which also cleared the head pin and three others, none of which counted either.

She wrote each one down.

"You writing down the misses?"

"The misses are the data."

He took that in. Behind her the ball return rumbled the ball back.

"What's the maximum?" she said.

"Nobody's ever bowled a 300. Best anybody's done is 245."

"So the system is designed so that no one ever completes it."

"That's the part people like."

He picked a ball off the rack and held it out. "You want me to show you or tell you?"

"Negative," Ellen said. Then she heard it. "Show me."

He came and stood at her right. He took her wrist, turned her hand until the palm faced the pins, and lowered it six inches.

"Low," he said. "Let the lane have it."

He let go first.

In her whole working life nobody had watched her be bad at anything. Her calm had been that good. The mistakes reached people afterward, typed up, with times on them. This was new: being bad in plain sight, in the cold, with a man standing three feet away who knew what good looked like. She kept her face level, and discovered she didn't much want to.

The next ball stayed on the lane. It took the front pins off in a clean chop and left a cluster standing at the back of the deck, with two fallen pins lying across the boards in front of them.

"Can we re-rack that?"

"The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."

He pointed at the fallen pin nearest the cluster. "Hit that. Let it do the work." He glanced at the leave. "That's a spread eagle. Don't let the name scare you."

She missed it. She wrote that down too.

Wednesday she left the clipboard in the car.

On Friday she brought it in and laid it face down on the orange settee. Her second ball went low and long, struck the fallen pin, and sent it skidding into the cluster, and three pins went down with it. One stayed up, at the far left corner of the deck. She took her third ball, let it go low, and took that one down too.

Ray didn't say anything. He stepped to the ball return and waited for her ball to come back.

The clipboard stayed where it was, face down.

* * *

At twenty to midnight on Saturday the Highway Department had gone home and the fryer was ticking as it cooled. Denise had her coat on and her hat with the earflaps in her hand, and she was not leaving. She had taken the R.G. strip down off the pegboard by the pit door and was reading it aloud from the office doorway.

"Twelve-eleven, lane three, table late. Twelve-fifteen, lane nine, belt. Twelve-seventeen, lane three, table late again, and he underlined again, which is a man with feelings about lane three. Twelve-eighteen, lane six." She held the card up to the light. "'Glow light bracket. Not mine. Checked anyway.' Not mine, checked anyway. Linda used to say his handwriting looked like a fence somebody built to keep the letters in, and look at that, twenty-some years, still a fence, every one of them square as a box of nails."

"Put it back, please."

"I'm putting it back." She did not move. "Honey, somebody added a new row to the sheet at the store this week. They're betting later now." She put her hat on and pulled the flaps down. "Don't let it go to your head."

The vestibule door banged, and then her car went down the drive.

Ellen pinned the strip back by the pit door and sat at the steel desk with the adding machine. The appraiser had come Wednesday, walked the basement with a flashlight, and looked at the boiler for a long time without writing anything. She had paid him. Two glow nights went on the tape, then six parties, then the scavenged parts and the electrician, then Ray's invoices in his square capitals. She took no salary; her pension covered her. The machine printed and advanced.

Net operating income to date: $5,600. Cash: $34,300.

Ninety percent of where a straight line to thirty thousand said she ought to be. Behind, but less behind than last week. Ninety percent was a number she knew well. At ninety percent you either got ahead of the work or the work got ahead of you, and the room stayed perfectly quiet either way, right up until it didn't.

She ran the weeks forward. The league nights were the thinnest columns on the tape, Tuesdays and Thursdays both, thinner than a slow Wednesday afternoon. She didn't know why.

On the two shelves above the desk, Arlene's green ledgers ran from 1971 to this year without a gap. Ellen took a blank strip and printed JAN / RECONCILE / ARLENE / LEAGUE and pinned it to the board.

Then she wrote one more. 02/27 / 1800 / LGM / 10. She left REMARKS blank and pinned it at the far end of the board, past everything else.

She left the pen uncapped beside it and turned off the counter lights.
