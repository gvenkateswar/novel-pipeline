# Reader Notes — Chapter 4 (with cross-chapter notes, Ch 1–4)

**Verdict:** This is the best chapter so far. It's the first one where I read for the people as much as the plot, and the shed kiss earned its place. I'm buying Chapter 5.

---

## Where I was gripped

- **The binocular sequence.** The rower lifts a hand to Ned, Ned turns his back, and the walk matches: "Left leg favored. The right heel coming down hard every step, as if the planks owed him something." Then he turns up *her* driveway. Her counting to ninety-one while he walks round the back of her empty house was the scariest thing in the book so far, and it's scary because it's seen from six hundred yards and she can't do anything.
- **The phone held to the binocular eyepiece.** Cheek an inch from hers, "His breath was the warmest thing on the hill." The tension and the attraction land in the same beat, which is what I bought this genre for.
- **The money conversation inside the bleed job.** She answers one injector nut at a time. The *Ruthie C*, the master's license in a drawer, "Asking Ned for the wheel is asking him to be old." That's her whole life in five lines, and the work keeps it from being a speech.
- **"Anything come in lately? Insurance. A settlement."** That hit me hard. He thinks somebody is putting money on her, and she laughs. That's when I started worrying about Tess and the deposit bag.

## Where I got bored or skimmed

- **The opening mooring count.** "Twenty-two... Twenty-two boats where there should be twenty-four. Then she had the other two." It's a cold open on a number, and then a correction. Four chapters in, I've had a lot of Beth counting things: rungs, cartons, steps, boats, primer strokes, furnace minutes. It's characterization, but by now my eyes slide over the numbers.
- **Mrs. Dyer's check-in.** The chimney, the west wind, Harold and the big ice. I skimmed it. I get that it shows Tess's warmth and that she's leaving Calloway off the air, but it ran long for something I understood in one line.
- **Russ, round two.** He's almost the same beat as Chapter 3. Cap off, looks into it, cap back on. "As if standing were optional" echoes "as if sitting up straight were optional" from Ch 3 nearly word for word. A recurring gesture is fine. A recycled simile reads like the writer forgot.

## Where I got confused

- "She followed him down the ladder and into the skiff, then passed the glasses up." I had to read it twice to see that she followed him *with the binoculars*, and that "passed the glasses up" means to Dane, who is lying above her. Physically muddy.
- **"Sternman."** Dane calls him that on the phone. How do they know he's the sternman and not some other crew? It's a guess presented as a fact.
- **Russ's short ring finger.** Dane looks at it on purpose, so it must mean something, but nothing earlier connects to it. Either it's a plant I can't place or it's noise.
- **The furnace "ticked on at ten past eleven."** The Ashby place has a furnace now? Fine, the generator's running. But in a house where every detail gets counted, it showed up out of nowhere.
- **Calls.** Pike at 11:20 nightly, a 10 a.m. call in Ch 3, Quist on the granite in Ch 4. I'm keeping track, but the "She doesn't know" call in Ch 3 is still floating, and I don't know who was on the other end.
- **"I'll bring Thursday's paper, so you'll know how it all came out."** I couldn't tell if Tess was joking or if this meant something. I read it three times.

## Did the choices make sense?

- **Beth sitting on the heel ID for a day.** Mostly believable. She's someone who waits to be sure. But it also works conveniently for the author, saving the reveal for a binocular set piece. Dane noticing and *not* asking was the right note.
- **Dane not going after the sternman.** Fully believable, and well argued. "If I go after him, I've left you." That's a professional making a choice that costs him, and I respected it. "He isn't my job. You are." tipped over into a romance-cover line, though.
- **Dane letting the day tank run nearly dry.** This is the one I didn't believe. Here's a man who sits with the window on his left, keeps his right side clear and reads every door, and he sucks air into the only generator on the point? It feels like it happened so the shed scene could. A city man not knowing diesel is plausible. This man being careless isn't.
- **Taking her up on open granite in daylight to glass the harbor.** Borderline, but Beth's logic (watchers watch the road) made it work for me.
- **Beth walking past Russ instead of ducking.** "Duck into the trees and he sees the tracks after and wonders." Good island logic. She's the expert on this ground and he defers to her, which I like.

## The kiss

- **Did it land?** Yes. The scar, "A door. It won," and then "She lifted her face, and he was already coming down to it" is a clean handoff. "His mouth was cold and then it wasn't" is simple and physical. Coffee, cold air, diesel on both of them. This is what I paid for.
- **Did I want it?** Yes, from the eyepiece scene on. The Ch 3 shed near-miss set it up, and doing it in the same shed paid that off.
- **"She wanted him on the concrete with the engine going, and she'd have told him so."** Good. This is the first time the heat level of the book shows up on the page. It's also the only line like it in four chapters, and since I bought open-door, I noticed how alone it is.
- **Did his stopping make sense?** Yes, completely, and it's set up physically, not just argued. She turns him so "the door was behind him," and he'd "had his back to it the whole time." That's the best piece of craft in the chapter. His job is in his body, and she accidentally made him break it.
- **Did it cost him?** Partly. Not letting go of the rag is the real tell, and it's good. "He said it slower than he'd said anything all day" helps. But "That's not a no. It's a not yet I can't afford" is too polished. It's a line made to be screenshotted, not something a man says with his pulse up and a driveway at his back. "As if she'd handed him something he'd have to carry in both arms" is the narrator explaining the cost instead of showing it. In Beth's POV I mostly see the cost through her burning face, not through him. "Okay" and then "Dane," the first time she uses his name, is the stronger beat, and it does more than his speech does.

## Does Dane feel like a person now?

Yes, and this is the chapter that did it. The boots running joke ("That's a narrow definition of waterproof"), Lowell, the father from Ponce who introduces you to potholes, the horse at Christmas, "On the phone I sound like a tourist." Until now he was a set of protocols, so these were overdue. The "Once" / "She asked for ten minutes. I gave them to her." exchange gives him a wound without a flashback dump, which I appreciated.

The weak spot is "Somebody has to stand between the person who saw it and the people who'd rather she hadn't. My mother did it with a radio. I do it in other people's sheds." That's a mission statement. It ties up his backstory too neatly, and nobody talks like that while wiping a primer lever.

## Do the two leads sound different?

Better than before, but not enough. Both still talk in short declaratives with no wasted words. In Ch 1–3 I could swap most of their lines. Now Dane has humor (boots, horse) and a longer, warmer register when he talks about family, and Beth stays clipped and procedural. That's the start of a difference. But when they're working they still sound identical: "Close it." / "Crank it." / "Lace those or go in." Russ and Tess have more distinct voices than the leads do.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **The not-asking tic.** Every character not asking is now a formula. Ch 1: "He didn't ask why." Ch 2: "He didn't ask if she was sure." "He nodded. He didn't ask how." Ch 3: "He didn't ask why" (Ned again). Ch 4: "He didn't ask why she'd sat on it. She heard him not ask." "She waited... she didn't ask." "She set the two side by side and didn't ask." It started as island reticence and now it's a verbal habit.
- **"She put it where she put things"** and its variants run through every chapter (Ch 1, 2, 3, and in Ch 4 "she knew he'd put it somewhere," "She marked where it lived"). Once was character. By now it's a refrain.
- **Neat closing lines.** Every chapter and most scenes end on a loaded, quiet image or a mic-drop. Ch 4: "Out here that's the same thing." / "His pen was still moving." The Russ scene, the shed scene and the porch scene all click shut. I'd like one scene to end messily.
- **Too-quotable lines:** "That's not a no. It's a not yet I can't afford." "I knew how to do this work before I knew it was work." "He isn't my job. You are." Each is fine alone. Three in one chapter feels engineered.
- **Repeated similes across characters:** the "as if [posture] were optional" for Russ, twice.

## Who I suspect of leaking her whereabouts, and why

1. **Tess (main suspect).** She has the only cell signal on the island, through the booster, and she stepped into the back office to take a call the moment Beth was reading her log. Her laugh "came late" on the Friday call, right after Beth told her she was home, and the door got tried that night. She's net control and emergency director, so she knows who's where. She has an $1,800/month tank loan. The fuel float was lit after hours for the *Margaret Ann*. She handles everyone's paychecks in the deposit bag, and Dane's "Anything come in lately?" makes me think money is being put in Beth's name, which runs through Tess's bag. Her fingers went slow on the drawer with Arsenault standing over it. And the book keeps making her lovable (the hot chocolate, the braid tug), which is exactly what a writer does to the traitor. The Ch 1 cut-off "Not in front of—" nags at me too.
2. **Dane (second).** "She doesn't know." The nightly calls nobody hears. The notebook that logs "Where you were, and when." The money questions. Ch 2 ended on her suspecting him and the book hasn't cleared him. I half-trust him now, which may be the point. At minimum the notebook is a liability: if someone takes it, it's a map to her.
3. **Ned.** He lied about the reefer, the hearing aid goes off whenever things get uncomfortable, he turned his back on the rower (refusal or signal?), and he holds the only Ashby key. Dane's argument that "Nobody tries a door he has a key to" clears him of the latch but not of talking.
4. **Russ.** He knows about the Ashby generator, and the finger detail gets pointed at. But he's so openly nosy that he reads as a red herring.

One thing I noticed: the sternman checks her *empty Cape* on Sunday morning, so whoever is feeding him doesn't know about Ashby yet. Whoever leaks it next is my traitor. And the four people who could know are Ned, Russ, the four houses on the north road, and anyone Dane's notebook reaches.

## What I want to know next

- Who was "she" in "She doesn't know," and what doesn't Beth know?
- Why is Dane asking about money? Has something been deposited in her name?
- What was the sternman looking for at her house, and what did he see through the kitchen window?
- Was Ned's turned back a refusal or a signal?
- What did Dane write in that last line, after she went in?
- Does the sternman find Ashby, and who tells him?
- When does the "not yet" become yes, and does it happen with the threat closer instead of further away?

I have plenty of questions pulling me forward.

## Would I keep reading?

Yes. The suspense is a steady low burn, the island feels real, and the kiss finally lets the romance in. My worries for the rest of the book: the stylistic tics (counting, not-asking, putting things away) are going to wear on me over ten chapters, and the spice so far is one line. If the open-door promise doesn't start paying off soon, a reader who bought this for heat will start to feel short-changed.

## Cross-chapter observations (Ch 1–4)

- Ch 1 is a strong hook. Her pressing the lid down and cinching the strap in front of the smugglers is a great character introduction.
- Ch 2's ending (the latch, "Who knew you were here?" "You.") is the best chapter ending so far.
- Ch 3 drags in the middle store scene. There are too many people to track, though Tess's booster reveal pays off.
- Through Ch 3, the romance was all near-misses and looks. Ch 4 is where it finally moves.
- The heroine's competence is the book's engine and it never wobbles. I'd follow her anywhere.
