# Chapter 5 — Half Tide

**Day 4 — Monday**

The gravel crunched dry under her XtraTufs for the first forty steps.

Beth walked the bar with her hood down so she could hear the water. The wind was still northeast, but it had stopped leaning on everything. The swell ran long into the cove, lifting the rockweed off the ledge and laying it back down.

At forty-one the stones went soft. Her heel sank, and the gravel sucked at it when she pulled free. A skin of water lay in the hollows, the same gray as the sky. Forty-one to sixty-three was the low spot. Ninety steps to the far side. Low water at ten past seven. A thread across the low spot by half past, ankles by half past eight, knees an hour after that.

She turned and walked back.

Dane stood at the top of the ledge where the shore path came out of the alders. He'd put himself where he could see the alders and the house at once.

"Walk me through it," he said when she reached the ledge foot. "Like I've never seen water."

"You've seen it. You just don't believe it."

"Then like that."

"High gravel dries about two hours either side of low. The low spot doesn't wait that long." She pointed with her chin. "Couple of hours into the flood, it's a knee-deep wade there. Ninety seconds. Longer if a sea's running. By half flood a man from away would call it the ocean."

"So there's a stretch of the flood when it's a cold wade and a short one, and after that it stops being a crossing at all."

"Then ocean."

She climbed to the rock below him and looked up at the house. The drive ran from the dooryard through the spruce, three hundred and forty steps to the north road.

"Only road out," she said. "If I wanted someone in this house, I'd wait at the end of that."

He looked down the drive with the same flat attention he'd given Russ's hands on the tailgate. "Your constable walked this Saturday. Whitcomb."

"At low water, in daylight, with a constable's reason. Nobody sets a watch on the bar, and nobody tries it on a flood." She turned him by the sleeve toward the head of the cove. A shingled shed sat up on granite blocks above the wrack line, buoys hung under the eave, the paint gone pale. "That's the fish house. The one I pay taxes on."

He gave it as long as he'd given the drive.

The ledge pitched steep for the last ten feet, slick where the spray reached. He was already at the top. He turned and put his hand down. She took it. His palm was dry and warm, with a ridge of callus at the base of the fingers she hadn't expected on a man who carried a notebook.

At the top neither of them let go when they could have. The alders hissed. His thumb moved once across the backs of her fingers.

He let go first, carefully, the way she'd set down a line she meant to come back for.

Halfway up the path to the house she looked back. A first thread of flood had found the low spot and was crossing it, thin as monofilament, and the gravel where she'd stood went dark.

---

By half past three the light had gone out of the window sheets.

Beth packed the bag on the bench in the seaward mudroom. Dry socks in a bread bag, twisted shut. Strike-anywhere matches in an amber pill bottle with the label scraped off. A headlamp with the red lens clipped on. The Icom, switched off, in a freezer bag. Her wool watch cap. The granola bar she'd carried since Friday and not eaten. She zipped it and stood it by the door, and set her boots beside it with the tops pulled open so her feet could find them in the dark.

The propane furnace ticked on under the floor. Across the yard the generator held its steady hum, the one they'd bled and brought back yesterday with four hands.

Dane watched from the mudroom doorway without a word. He went away and came back with the spare magazine, the trauma kit, and the sat phone sealed in a zip bag, and set them on the floor against her bag.

Then he went back to the kitchen.

On Friday he'd have kept his things on his own side of any room. She looked at the two piles, then at the door.

They ate canned chowder heated on the propane range, standing at the counter until he carried his bowl to the sheeted dining table. He sat with his back to the wall. She took the other chair without being asked.

"This is mostly potato," he said.

"It's from Tess's shelf. It's mostly potato on purpose."

He ate it anyway, all of it, and scraped the bowl.

At dusk he went out on the porch with the notebook. She watched through the gap in the window sheet. He wrote standing, facing the road, and the pen moved for a long time. When he turned toward the door, he closed it.

Later she went back to the mudroom to check the bag one last time. He was already there, crouched, with his hand on the strap. Hers went to the same place and landed on his. He didn't move it. Neither of them pretended it was the strap they'd come for. Then he stood and went past her sideways in the narrow room, his breath on her temple, and didn't stop.

She lifted the seaward latch and dropped it. Lifted it again. Then she left it on the hook instead of shooting the bolt, so it would open with one hand.

---

The generator quit at ten.

It didn't die the way it had on Sunday. It choked, stuttered, ran a second at the wrong speed, and stopped. Beth was on the couch in the dark front room, dressed, and the quiet filled the house.

Then the smell. She knew it from the *North Reach*'s engine room the winter an injector line split: diesel burning where diesel shouldn't burn. Under it something drier. Shingles.

Orange light grew at the edge of the window sheet.

Dane was up before she spoke, his hand at his hip. He went to the front corner where the sheet didn't meet the frame and stood back from it. She came in behind his shoulder.

The shed was going. Fire ran up the seaward corner and along the eave, and the light of it reached down the drive. At the far end, where the drive met the north road, it caught a windshield. No lights. No engine. A shape behind the glass, or only the glass's own dark.

"Not the drive," she said. "That's what the fire's for. We run for the road, and he's at the end of it." She was already moving. "The bar. Now, while it's knees."

Short of half flood, and rising. Nobody who didn't know that bar would put a foot on it in the dark.

He looked at the windshield once more, the way a man looks at the one thing in the night he knows how to handle. Then he turned his back on it and came.

Boots. Bags. The latch came off the hook under one hand. No light. They went out the seaward side into rain driving off the water.

She went down the ledge by feel and by the line of the rock against the glow behind them. Dane came after her with the gun out and low, his face turned uphill.

At the ledge foot the gravel was already under. She stepped in.

Forty-eight degrees. It went into her shins first, a strap cinched tight on bone. Then the knees. Then nothing below the knee at all, which was worse, because she couldn't feel what she was standing on.

She put herself on the seaward side and took a fist of his belt at the small of his back.

"Left of me. Small steps."

He took small steps.

"Feet flat. Don't lift them."

She counted under her breath. Thirty, and the gravel went soft. Forty-four, and she heard the sea before she felt it.

"Sea coming. Stand still."

It came in surging out of the dark and lifted the water to mid-thigh, cold enough to take the air out of her. The gravel slid out from under him. His weight swung away downstream, and her fist on the belt held, and her arm took all of him and didn't open. If he went under, the cold would make him breathe in before he could tell it not to. That was what killed you here. Not the cold. The gasp.

"Shit," she told the water.

The sea drew off, and he got his feet back.

"Go."

Ninety came with the far gravel still a body-length away. At a hundred and six it was under her boots and crunching, and she let go of the belt, and her hand stayed curled.

They came out soaked to the thighs and wet everywhere else from the rain. Behind them, over the point, the shed roof went up in a column of sparks. The bulb that had come on over the two of them was in there. The bleed screw she'd watched him close.

From the north road came a tanker horn, held down the whole way. Walt Grindle. Nobody else leaned on it like that.

At the head of the cove her hands shook too hard for the first try. She found her father's key on the nail under the eave, where he'd always left it, and got it into the lock on the second. The door had swollen in its frame. She put her shoulder to it.

---

Kindling spat in the oil drum and took. Beth fed it split cedar off her father's pile until the iron creaked with heat, then looked at her hands. Soot and pitch on both. They were still going.

Dane had set the Glock on the workbench before anything else. Now he stood at the water-side window, the only one, shoulder to the wall and face out of the lantern. His jaw was shaking, and he was holding it shut so it wouldn't show.

She went to him and ran his zipper down. The soft-shell came off heavy as a wet net. She hung it on the nail by the stove, notebook side to the wall, and it began to steam.

He did the fleece himself, arms crossed, over his head. Under it he was white to the collarbones and rough with gooseflesh. She laid her hand on his breastbone. His heart ran fast under it. Cold did that. So did other things.

She went back to the landward door and dropped the iron bar into its brackets.

"Door's shut behind you," she said.

He came away from the window and stood in front of her, close enough that the cold came off him onto her skin.

"When this is over, I report this. I want you to hear that before. Not after."

"I know what you are." She hooked two fingers through his belt, where her fist had been in the water. "I'm choosing anyway. Your call."

He looked at her long enough for the stove to tick.

"Yes."

His mouth tasted of rain and chowder and shook once before it steadied. Her back found the warm flank of the drum while the draft under the door ran cold along her legs. His hands went to the hem of her shirt and stopped.

"I don't have anything," he said against her mouth. "With me."

"Neither do I." She looked past him at the shelves. "My father kept everything in this room. Not that."

"I've got a trauma kit."

"Does it—"

"It's a very disappointing kit."

She laughed into his shoulder, and he laughed too, low, and it shook them both.

"Hands, then," she said.

"Hands."

They got to the cot with her shirt on the floor and her canvas pants half off, one leg still around her ankle, heavy with the bar. The cot side of the room was cold, the stove side hot. He put his hands on her ribs, cold too, and she held them there until they weren't.

He put his mouth on her breast: stubble, then heat, then cold air on the other nipple until his thumb came to it. Somewhere behind the point the shed was still burning. Someone had known exactly where to put a match.

She pulled his head up and kissed him so she'd stop thinking it. A gust came down the stovepipe, and the flame in the drum ducked. His hand went down her belly and waited at the waistband of her long johns.

"Beth."

"Yes." She covered his hand with her own and pushed it down. "That's the answer. Don't make me give it twice."

He didn't. His fingers found her clit, and she was wet, and his calluses caught on her and she said his name, once, like a complaint. Wind worked in the shingles. He watched her face the whole time, the way he watched people's hands.

Once he looked past her at the workbench. At the gun. Then back at her, and he stayed.

She came with one fist in the blanket and her eyes open on the dark loft hatch. What went through her wasn't his name. It was that he'd turned his back on that windshield, that he'd walked into the water holding nothing but her word, and she'd been holding his belt.

Then she put her hand on his belt buckle, cold brass, and opened it.

"Your turn," she said. "Unless you want to argue about it."

"No."

She took his cock in her hand. Her palm was rough as his, the hawser scar across it. Out on the north road the tanker horn went again, two short blasts. He said her name into her hair as though it cost him, and she put her mouth on his throat, on the pulse there, and he came against her hip, forehead on her shoulder, then heavy and quiet and warm.

The stove ticked. She pulled the wool blanket up over both of them.

He spoke first, to the hatch overhead. "They knew the Ashby place."

"Only Ned knew."

"And me."

She lay with her palm flat on his chest. She had kept believing it was him alone. Since the bread rack she had worked at it, because one man was a man she could watch.

She took her hand off his chest.

"Friday. Twenty to six. I called Tess on the landline. Told her I was home. Don't send anybody, keep it off the air." The cove slapped the ledge. "She wrote it in the check-in log. The spiral one. It sits open on the counter by the register. Anybody buying cigarettes can read it."

The stove ticked twice.

"Tess knew I was home."

She pulled the blanket to her chin.

"Tess has known where I sleep since I was nine," she said. "She walked me home from every dance and every wake on this island. That's not a thing she found out."

He didn't move for a long time. His eyes stayed on the hatch. He let her have both halves. He didn't hand either one back.

"Then the circle was always wider than the two of us," he said, "and you've been carrying the whole of it on me."

---

Past midnight the lantern was down to a bead. Their clothes steamed on the coils of pot warp and on the nail. The rain had eased in the shingles. Through the water-side window the glow over the point had gone dull red, and above it, on the north point, hung the tanker's work lights.

Dane pulled on his damp jeans and the fleece and took the sat phone out of its bag. He lifted the bar.

"Sky," he said, and went out.

She went to the door, not the window, and stood at the gap so she could hear.

"We moved. Secondary location." A gap. "Not on this phone." Another. "Not where."

He was in again in under two minutes. He set the bar in its brackets.

"Pike knows we moved. She doesn't know where."

She sat on the cot with the blanket around her and said nothing.

He took the end of the workbench, his back to the wall. "Who was at the counter?"

"Friday it's three," she said. "You. Ned, because I wasn't on his boat. Tess, because I called her." She drew her knees up. "And whoever read the log between twenty to six and the power going. I can't tell you who. I was home with you. The landlines still worked Friday. Read it, go home, make a call."

"Then it's the people you told, and after them a counter that nobody can put a name to."

"From Saturday it's anybody who stood there since. Phones were dead by then. You'd make your call in the store, on the booster, or walk it down to the float."

"Arsenault stood there Saturday," he said. "He looked at me once and never at my hands, which is a man who'd already decided what I was. He goes on the list."

"His crew, if they came ashore for anything. The heel. And six at the stove."

"You didn't give me names."

"You never asked. They're island."

"Whitcomb asks where you're sleeping, and then he turns up on the camp road."

"Put him in," she said, to the stove. "He's in. He knows the shore path to the cove road. He's never had a reason to open this door."

Dane looked at the stove. "And the woman who wrote it down."

"If Tess were selling me, she'd have kept it in her head. You don't write a thing in a book anybody can read if you're the one using it. She writes everything down. Fuel, credit, who's home. She's net control. That's what she's for."

He'd met Tess once, across a counter. Whatever he thought of that, he kept.

"Ashby's smaller," she said. "Ned and you."

"Or someone Ned told."

"Ned tells Tess. Everybody knows that." She shrugged under the blanket. "That's not talking."

She looked at him in the low lantern light. He'd picked the Ashby place out of Ned's three. His calls went out every night, to people she'd never met. Her palm on his chest an hour ago had told her nothing she could check.

His jacket hung on the nail by the stove, and the square of the notebook sat in the inside pocket, left side. What was in it stayed his.

She set it down beside the other thing. He'd walked into the water on her word, with a gun in his hand and his back to the man he wanted.

"We're done hiding," she said.

He waited.

"Hiding's been found twice in four days. And I know every boat in that harbor by its exhaust, which does me no good behind a sheet."

"And?"

"Somebody on my island is passing on what they hear. The man in that drive expected us on the road. He doesn't know this island. I do."

He was quiet long enough that she thought it was no.

"Pike's going to tell me to sit tight," he said, slow. "A witness who goes looking is a witness who gets seen. What Pike doesn't have is you. So if you're seen, you're seen with me beside you, in daylight, on ground you pick." He folded his arms. "Walk me through it."

"Store at opening. In daylight, in the open, with you. Being seen is normal out here. Hiding gets noticed."

"What's your reason for being in the store?"

"I'll think of one."

He kept his eyes on her after she'd stopped talking.

She took her father's carpenter pencil from the bench, pried the lid off the nearest paint can and turned it over. On top, in her father's square capitals: *RUTHIE C — TRIM, 2 COATS*. The underside was dull with old paint.

By lantern light she started writing. The ones from away first. *Arsenault. Crew (2).* She drew a line off the 2 and wrote *the heel* at the end of it. Then the island. *Russ.* Under him, one line: *Saturday — six at the store.* Under that she ruled a line and left the space blank, for Friday's counter. Then *Ned*.

She stopped, because that was everybody.

Dane leaned on the bench beside her and read it upside down.
