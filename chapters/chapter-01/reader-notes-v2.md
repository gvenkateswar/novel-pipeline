# Reader Notes: Chapter 01 (v2)

Read as a Kindle buyer who knows only the premise: a retired air-traffic controller buys a failing small-town bowling alley.

## Where I got bored

- **The first page.** "A row of ten lane-light toggles ran along the inside lip of the counter, each wearing a scrap of masking tape gone the color of weak tea..." The book opens with her relabeling switches, then hanging a pegboard, then putting away a calendar. I got the idea (she's an orderly person taking over someone else's mess) about halfway down the page, and it kept going. A light romcom asked me to wait for a person to walk in. Things picked up at "somebody stamped salt off a pair of boots, four times, hard."
- **"04 / 1830 / LG / 4 / —."** This is a code I can't read yet, and the next line ("By lane 7 she had the spacing right") made me feel I'd missed something. I skimmed past it.
- **The lane 7 breakdown, for a paragraph.** "Seven seconds a cycle, never more than seven and a half. Lane 7 took nine. Its next cycle took eleven, and the sweep paused at the bottom of its stroke before the table moved at all." Sweep, table, deck: machine words I can't picture. Her competence comes through, but my eyes went over the mechanics without seeing anything until the burnt-iron smell.

## Where I got confused

- **What kind of bowling this is.** For the first two-thirds of the chapter I pictured regular ten-pin. "Three strings," "foul row," "lob line," and "a ninety-four" (is that a score? an age? an average?) all came before any explanation. Candlepin finally gets explained when Ellen throws her ball ("A ball four and a half inches across with no holes, three throws to a frame..."). That paragraph was clear and helpful, and it made the earlier details make sense afterward. But I had already built the wrong room in my head. "Bev Dubois. She's a ninety-four" and "Paulie Roy... he's a ninety-one" still aren't clear to me. I'm guessing averages.
- **"Lineage."** "And seven gets its lineage back." I don't know this word in a bowling sense. Context tells me it's the lane fee, but I had to work it out.
- **"Two," Ellen said, under her breath.** I didn't know what she was counting. I only got it at the end with "One," to the building, and it's a nice bookend. When I first read it, it seemed like a typo or a missing beat.
- **The locksmith call.** Did Ellen know Gagnon & Son was Ray's locksmith business when she dialed? "Ellen looked at the stitching on his jacket and dialed" suggests she did, and that it was a deliberate move. Then "She didn't answer" when Danny says the locksmith is his father reads like she was caught off guard. I couldn't tell if she was being clever or got surprised. The scene is funny either way, but I didn't know whose joke it was.
- **The interference ruling.** "Verna, is that interference?" and then the *Auxiliary captain* answers "Hollis, it's a ball in Ray's lane," and then the woman in lavender rules. For a moment I thought the Auxiliary captain was Verna. Three new people in one paragraph, and the names went to the wrong faces. Later "V. TIBBETTS, SEC." sorted it out, mostly.
- **The big man.** He gets a whole description (blaze-orange cap, square beard, taped glasses) and a key role, and I only learn his name is Hollis from someone else's line of dialogue. That's fine, but I spent a scene calling him "the beard guy."
- **"Opening night" on December 1.** Hollis says Arlene threw the first ball "every opening night since 1971. Tonight's yours." Is this the season opener? In December? Or is he calling Ellen's first night "opening night"? I wasn't sure what was being celebrated.
- **Why Ray didn't buy it himself.** He has the key, he was the mechanic, his wife's trophy is in the case, and he gets "first crack" if she fails. So why is Ellen the owner? I can't tell if that's deliberate mystery or something I was supposed to pick up on.

## Where I stopped believing

- **The ball.** Her first-ever throw jumps the gutter and wipes out exactly Ray's three standing pins plus his one fallen pin, in front of forty people, on the lane Hollis steered her next to. It's the meet-cute set piece, and I went along with it, but I could feel the author's hand putting the ball there.
- **Hollis matchmaking on night one.** "You want him to hear about you, or see you?" It's a great line, but why is this man pairing her up with Ray within hours of her owning the place? Either he knows something about the two of them that I don't, or the town's romance machinery started too early.
- **The bowlers calling her "tower."** So the whole league already knows her old job, before she's said a word? The pool explains that the town has been talking about her, so I accepted it, but it was a little quick.
- Small one: Ray stood in the vestibule refusing to come any farther, then went straight to bowling on lane 6 all night. When did that thaw happen? He went from not stepping off the mat to bowling in her building without any beat between.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Explaining the subtext right after showing it.** "He had put all of it into a pencil and none of it into his voice... She knew something about keeping a voice level." The pencil groove and the trophy-glass touch had already told me everything. The last sentence underlines it. Same with "Her face would be showing nothing... her face would look exactly like this up to the morning it happened," which states the theme outright.
- **The homey similes come in a steady stream.** "A wall with a cardigan on and a tissue up the sleeve," "like she's waiting on a bus," "a rattle like a silverware drawer pulled out too far," "a crack like a dropped plate," "like an iron left face down on a shirt," "as lightly as a man checking a stove he already knew was off." Each one is good. Together they feel like a writing-workshop rhythm: an image every hundred words, all from the same kitchen drawer.
- **Too many terse people.** Ellen talks in clipped radio-speak. Ray: "Some." "Not my alley." Danny: "Three keys a door. More?" Hollis talks "the way he might give a wind speed." Verna talks in minutes. Five of the six speaking characters are economical and dry. Only Denise really sounds different, and she carries all the warmth and chatter in the chapter. On the page, Ray and Danny are close to the same voice, which might be the point (father and son), but Ellen sounds like them too.
- **The neat closer.** "'He's punctual,' he said." It's a good line and I smiled, but it felt like a scene-ending button.
- **ATC jokes.** "Negative." / "Normal people say no." "Lane seven is unable." "Lane six, stand by." I enjoyed most of them, but I can see the bit being rationed. If it keeps up at this rate, I'll tire of it by chapter 4.

## Why Ellen bought the alley

Partly. The book tells me: "For thirty-one years the FAA had picked her shifts, her airspace, and finally the month she stopped. This building was the first thing she had ever chosen on nobody's schedule but her own." Plus the image of forty people needing to go somewhere in order. So she was retired against her will at 56 (the sheet-cake line is great) and wants a room to run. I understand that emotionally. What I don't know is why *this* alley, in *this* town. Does she have a connection to the place? Did she just answer an ad? "The worst night of her career" is hinted at but kept back, and I suspect it's the real reason. Right now the motive reads more as a theme than a decision a person made.

## What I want to know next

- What happened on "the worst night of her career"?
- Who was Linda Gagnon, how did she die, and why does Ray touch the glass?
- Why didn't Ray buy the alley, and does he want it?
- Lane 7 is dead and the only mechanic said "Not my alley." Who fixes it? Does Ellen try it herself with the manual?
- The 8 a.m. rekey: Ray's son is changing the locks on Ray's own key. Does Ray show up?
- $250,000 due March 1, the first night closed in the red, and the whole town is betting against her. How does she make the money?
- Why does Ray "not buy a square" in the pool on his own wall?
- Why did Ray's pickup wait until her engine started? That last image did most of the work of pulling me forward.

Those are real questions, and I'd turn the page.

## Best moment

The ending:

> Ray had gone out with the last of the league at ten-thirty, but his pickup still sat beside the pole, engine running, a thread of exhaust going up past the cab. She brushed the snow off her windshield. The pickup stayed where it was. Her engine caught on the second try, and she switched on her headlights.
>
> Across the lot, the pickup's lights came on. It rolled down to Kettle Road and turned toward town, and its taillights got smaller until the trees took them.

Nobody explains it, and it's the most romantic thing in the chapter. It changed how I read the whole of Ray. Runner-up: "Let the minutes reflect... The secretary is aware of no precedent since 1979." That was the biggest laugh I got.
