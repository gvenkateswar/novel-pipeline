# Chapter One: Cleared to Bowl

At 3:40 Ellen had owned Kettle Lanes for six hours, and Arlene's tape was coming off the switches in curls.

The lane-light toggles ran in a row of ten along the inside lip of the counter. Each wore a scrap of masking tape gone the color of weak tea, with a number in green ink and nothing else. Ellen peeled them left to right. Under number 6 she found an older scrap, and under that a third, in a hand that wasn't Arlene's.

She tore fresh tape against the edge of the roll and wrote in block capitals, five columns, the way she had written flight strips at twenty-five: lane, time, league or open, count, remarks. The Sharpie squeaked on every downstroke. 04 / 1830 / LG / 8 / —. By lane 7 she had the spacing right. By lane 10 she wanted to redo the first three.

Below them, the foul-light switches had their own row. She gave each one a red stripe and a smaller label, FOUL 1 through FOUL 10, so that nobody reaching under the counter in a hurry would put a lane in the dark when she meant to watch its line.

Beside the shoe cubbies she screwed a square of pegboard to the paneling, checked it with a level, and pushed one index card onto a peg at eye height. TUES LEAGUE / 1830 / 40 / ALL LANES.

The baseboard heat ticked as it came up. The building smelled of lane dressing and last night's fryer oil. Low sun came through the glass-block band and laid pale squares on the approaches to 1 and 2.

In the office she lifted a fuel-oil company calendar off its nail. JANUARY 2019. A covered bridge, and a delivery number printed in red along the bottom. She looked at the number for a while. Then she put the calendar in the desk drawer and shut the drawer with her hip.

At 4:30 the vestibule door opened, and somebody stamped salt off a pair of boots, four times, hard.

"Two," Ellen said, under her breath.

Denise Lavoie came around the end of the counter in a parka over a red polo faded pink at the shoulders. She slid a folded newspaper and a ballpoint pen onto the shelf under the register, crossword up, without looking where they went. Then she put on a pair of cat-eye readers and bent to the switches.

She read all ten. She read the foul row. She straightened.

"What's 'remarks'?"

"Anything that doesn't fit in the first four columns."

"Three's got a dash."

"Lane three has no remarks."

"Lane three's got a dead spot on the approach and a settee with a crack that pinches you through your slacks." Denise folded the readers. "But sure. Dash."

"Noted."

"Uh-huh." Denise looked at the pegboard. "That's new."

"Since three-fifteen."

"Forty." She tapped the card with one finger. "You count the subs?"

"Forty is the number Arlene gave me."

"Arlene gave you forty because it's round. Arlene know you're doing all this?"

"Arlene signed at nine-forty this morning. She's at the pharmacy. You could ask her."

"I've been asking Arlene things for twenty-two years, honey. She's a wall with a cardigan on." Denise hung her parka on the hook behind the cubbies and looked Ellen over the long way, from shoes to haircut. "Who's working the fryer while you label things?"

"I am. From six-fifteen."

"You ever run a fryer?"

"I've read the manual. Twice."

Denise didn't answer that. She reached past Ellen's elbow, found the lane 4 toggle by feel without lowering her eyes, and flipped it. Down the building, the lights over lane 4 hummed and came on.

"Twenty-two years," Denise said. "They've had numbers on them."

Three knocks on the door glass at 5:15, a second apart.

Ellen came around the counter. Through the glass she could see the lot, plowed down the middle and not at the edges, and a pickup idling by the sign pole with its parking lights on. A tall man stood on the step in a brown canvas jacket with snow on both shoulders. GAGNON & SON was stitched over the pocket. His right hand was closed at his side, and an orange paper tag showed between two knuckles.

She opened the door. Cold came in low across the linoleum and went for her ankles.

"Mr. Gagnon."

"Evening." He didn't move off the step. "I open on league nights."

"Arlene mentioned it."

"Lights and coffee. Tuesdays and Thursdays." He looked past her, down the length of the lanes, and back. "Since ninety-six."

Ellen put out her hand.

He considered it. The fist stayed where it was. "Arlene gave me this."

"Arlene gave me the building."

"Sold you."

"Yes. Sold me. The key comes with it."

"If Arlene wants it back, she can ask. She knows where the store is."

"Arlene holds a note on this building for a quarter of a million dollars, Mr. Gagnon. That is the only thing of Arlene's I intend to keep."

He looked at her for a moment, and then at the snow starting to collect on the sill between them. "That one she'll ask for."

"You can wait inside," Ellen said. "You can't open anything."

He wiped his boots on the outer mat, left and then right, stepped over the sill onto the rubber mat in the vestibule, and stopped. The door eased shut behind him. Between his boots, the 1974 cigarette burn showed through a split in the rubber. He went no farther.

At the counter, Denise wrote something into her crossword. "Ray."

"Denise."

"Cold out?"

"Some."

Ellen went behind the counter and took the Ambrose directory off the shelf under the register, forty stapled pages with a Rotary wheel on the cover. She ran a finger down the yellow section. LANDSCAPING. LIQUIDATORS. LOCKSMITH. One listing. She read the number, looked at the stitching on the jacket by the door, and dialed the rotary phone. The dial whirred back after every digit. Seven digits, seven whirs.

"Gagnon and Son." A younger voice, level.

"This is Ellen Daly at Kettle Lanes. I need a locksmith. Rekey all exterior doors. Front, rear, and the bulkhead."

On the other end, a fingernail tapped glass. "Ms. Daly, the locksmith's my father."

She didn't answer.

"It's Tuesday," the voice went on, "so he's probably standing in your alley."

Ellen looked at the vestibule. Ray Gagnon was standing on her mat with his hands at his sides and his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, examining the corkboard of lost mittens.

"Say again."

"The locksmith," the voice said, at exactly the same speed, "is my father. Ray. Six-one. Brown jacket."

"He's here."

"I figured. This is Danny." More tapping. "Okay. Kettle Lanes, rekey, all exterior. Front, rear, bulkhead. Eight a.m. tomorrow. Three new keys per door unless you want more. Do you want more?"

"No. Three."

"Three per door. Eight a.m. Invoice to Kettle Lanes, attention E. Daly." He said it the way he would type it, punctuation included. "Anything else?"

"Nothing."

"Good luck tonight." Danny hung up first.

Behind her, Denise's pen made one short stroke.

Ellen took a fresh index card and wrote in block capitals. LOCKSMITH / 0800 / GAGNON / EXT DOORS. She pushed it onto the pegboard under the league card, at the height of a tall man's eyes, squarely in line with the mat.

Ray read it from where he stood. He didn't need to lower his glasses.

"He's punctual," he said.

<p align="center">* * *</p>

The PA hummed for two full seconds before it would carry a voice. Ellen let it hum, bent the gooseneck down, and counted the room. Forty bowlers on the orange settees and at the ball racks, coats heaped at the ends of the benches. Two boys at the snack bar waiting on a parent. Denise. Herself. Forty-four.

"Good evening. Tuesday league." She read the pairings off Arlene's list in order, lanes one and two through nine and ten, with the same two seconds of air between each pair.

When she finished, the leadoff on lane 4, a man in a Bruins jacket, was standing at the line with a ball in his hand, looking back at the counter as though he expected something.

"Lane four, cleared to bowl."

He bowled. So did everyone else, on all ten lanes, without waiting for her, and the wood went down the length of the building in a rattle like a silverware drawer pulled out too far.

"Roger, tower," somebody called from lane 2.

From the far end, through cupped hands: "Kettle Tower, requesting a hot dog."

Ellen leaned to the microphone. "Hot dogs are on the roller at the snack bar. Relish is in the squeeze bottle. You'll need to walk over."

"Five out of ten," Denise said, without looking up from her crossword. "He bowls a ninety-one. He's done better."

A big man in a blaze-orange watch cap came up to the counter and waited until Ellen looked at him. White beard, trimmed square. Felt-lined boots. Reading glasses mended at one hinge with electrical tape. He took the cap off and held it in both hands.

"You fly?"

"No. For thirty-one years I told the people who do where to go. Then I turned fifty-six and the government gave me a sheet cake."

He considered this with the cap in his hands. Then he put it back on. "Arlene threw the first ball every opening night since 1971. Tonight's yours. You going to throw one?"

"I've never bowled."

"Think Arlene ever hit anything?"

"I wouldn't know."

"No," he said. "Lane five?"

Lanes 5 and 6 belonged tonight to Gagnon and Son and the Ambrose Fire Auxiliary. Ray was leading off on 6. He had thrown two balls and was standing at the line with his third, studying what was left: three pins standing toward the back and one lying across the deck at an angle he appeared to have arranged. On lane 5 the Auxiliary's leadoff had just finished his frame, and the machine had set him a fresh rack.

The big man waved the Auxiliary bowler back to his seat and put a house ball in Ellen's hands.

It was no bigger than a grapefruit, with no finger holes and a weight that settled into her palm and stayed there. Sixty feet of honey-colored maple ran away from her shoes, dented near the line. The pins at the far end were as thin as their name. She had watched forty people do this for twenty minutes. She swung her arm back and let go late and high.

The ball came down well past the painted line ten feet out. A red light came on over the lane. The ball struck the boards once, jumped the gutter cap with a crack like a dropped plate, and ran into lane 6, where it went through everything Ray had standing and everything he had lying down. All of it went into the pit.

Nobody made a sound for one second by her watch. Then forty bowlers did.

Ray stood at the line on 6 with his third ball still in his hand, looking at an empty deck.

Ellen walked back up to the counter and bent the gooseneck down. "Lane six, stand by."

By the time she came back down the building, three people stood over the scorer's table between 5 and 6: the big man with his cap off, the Auxiliary's captain, and a tiny woman in a lavender parka zipped to the chin, holding a three-ring binder against her chest.

"Who invited her?" the big man said. "I did."

"Nobody's saying she wasn't invited, Hollis." The Auxiliary captain pointed down lane 6. "I'm asking where the ball went."

The woman in lavender looked at the empty deck, then at Ray, then at Ellen. "Re-rack," she said. "Lane six re-bowls the frame." She wrote one line in her binder and shut it.

On 6, the machine set a full rack. Ray bowled the frame again from the beginning: three balls, four pins, nothing left lying where it could help him. He walked to the scorer's table, took the pencil, and wrote the 4 into the box, pressing hard enough that Ellen could see the groove from where she stood. He didn't look at Hollis or at the Auxiliary captain. He didn't say anything.

Ellen opened her mouth. Forty-three people were listening. She closed it.

Ray came back up the approach and passed her on his way to the settee. "Lob line's ten feet out, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms.," Ellen said.

He was already sitting down.

<p align="center">* * *</p>

At 8:10, in the second string, Ellen was carrying a fresh coffee filter toward the snack bar when lane 7 lost its rhythm.

She had been timing the machines since six-thirty without deciding to. Ball, crash, the thunk of the sweep, the table coming down to set the pins and going back up. Seven seconds, give or take half of one. Lane 7 took nine. Its next cycle took eleven, and the sweep paused at the bottom of its stroke before the table moved at all.

Ellen set the filter on a scorer's table, took out her phone, and started recording.

The next ball took out the headpin and two others. The sweep came down. The table followed it, slower than it should have, and stopped about a foot above the deck, canted to one side, with pins scattered underneath. The motor hummed. Then it quit. A smell drifted up the approach like an iron left face down on a shirt.

One by one, the four bowlers on 7 turned around on the settee and looked at the counter.

She stopped recording, walked up the aisle to the PA without hurrying, and bent the gooseneck down. When she spoke, she used the voice. Thirty-one years of it: low, level, every word given the same room as the one before.

"Lane seven is unable. Lane seven, you'll finish on lane eight with your opponents. Bring your sheets. Nobody's in the air."

They brought their sheets. A man on 8 slid down the settee to make room. Somebody on 3 laughed, and then didn't.

Ellen took the flashlight from under the counter and went through the steel door by lane 10. The catwalk ran behind all ten machines, low enough that she kept her head down, and it smelled of grease and warm rubber. At lane 7 she put the light on the machine. The table hung where it had stopped. The belt was still. One pin lay against the curtain at the back of the pit. She looked for a full minute and touched nothing. Then she walked back and pulled the steel door shut behind her.

Arlene's card file sat at the end of the counter, a tin recipe box with a rooster on the lid. Under M there was one card. MECHANIC, in green ink, and below it R.G. and a phone number.

Ray was on the 5–6 settee with a pencil behind his ear. Ellen stood in front of him and held out the card.

"Lane seven. Second string, eight-eleven. The table stopped about twelve inches above the deck on its way down. The sweep is down. The motor ran four seconds after the table stopped, then quit. There's a burned smell at the back. I touched nothing."

He read the card. He didn't take it. "Arlene's mechanic was me, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms."

He took his key ring out of his jacket pocket and turned it over once in his palm. The keys jingled against each other, the orange tag among them. Then he closed his fist on the ring and put it away.

"Not my alley," he said.

Beside him, Hollis took off his cap and studied the ceiling tiles.

"Understood," Ellen said.

Ray stood up, lifted the coffee filter off the scorer's table where she had left it, and carried it to the snack bar. He set it beside the urn. On his way back he passed the trophy case and, without turning his head, touched the glass with one knuckle. Ellen saw where. The center shelf. A trophy with a brass plate that read LINDA GAGNON MEMORIAL, and beside it a framed card: WOMEN'S HOUSE HIGH SINGLE, 187, LINDA GAGNON, FEB. 1998.

He sat down and took the pencil from behind his ear.

Ellen stayed on her feet at the counter. Denise watched her over the top of the crossword.

"Nine lanes," Ellen said. "And seven gets its lineage back."

"Honey, they'd have finished on eight and said thank you."

"They paid for a lane. They got half of one."

She sat down on the stool, opened the register, and counted it out into four small piles.

Ninety days until March 1, when Arlene's note came due. The first of them was going to close below zero.

She took the piles down to lane 8 herself and put each one into a hand.

"You don't have to do that," said a man in a plaid hunting jacket.

"It's done."

The last pile went to the tiny woman in the lavender parka, whose name was printed at the top of the score sheet: V. TIBBETTS, SEC. She counted the bills, folded them into an envelope, and opened her binder.

"Arlene never refunded anyone, Mrs. Daly," she said, writing. "I'll note it in the minutes."

At 10:55 the lanes were dark and only the counter lights were on. Denise worked down the last row of rental shoes with the spray can, toe and heel, two counts each, and passed sentence on every pair.

"You'll live. You'll live. These, I'd burn."

Ellen squared the score sheets, tapped the edges even, and fed the night into the adding machine: register, snack bar, the refunds for lane 7. The machine chattered. The last line came out in red. She tore off the tape and folded it once.

"How bad?" Denise said.

"Below zero."

"How far below?"

"Far enough that the machine changed colors."

Denise set the spray can under the counter, zipped her parka, and took out her phone. She thumbed through it and turned the screen toward Ellen.

It was a photograph of a sheet of paper tacked to a wall behind a cash register, with a board of key blanks hanging beside it. The sheet was hand-ruled into squares, one date to a square, from the first week of December to the last of February. Most of the squares had initials or a name in them, in blue pen and black pen and one in pencil. Across the top, in marker: KETTLE LANES. NEW OWNER. HOW LONG?

"Honey," Denise said, "this town's had a pool on you since the closing. I'm just the one holding the sheet."

"That's the hardware store."

"Best foot traffic in town."

"How much is a square?"

"Five dollars."

"Who has tonight?"

"Nobody had tonight. Most people figured you'd make it to New Year's." Denise scrolled with her thumb. "You want to know who's got Christmas Eve?"

"Negative."

Denise looked at her over the cat-eye readers. "Normal people say no."

"I said no."

"If you miss," Denise said, "you have to sell, and Ray gets first crack."

"I'm aware. I signed it."

Ellen took the phone out of Denise's hand, put two fingers on the screen, and spread them. She walked the photo down to the bottom right corner. The last square said MARCH 1. Nothing was written in it.

"Ray won't buy one," Denise said.

"Why not?"

"Ask him. It's his wall." Denise took the phone back and put it in her pocket. "Back door first. Arlene always did the back door first."

"I have a list."

"Course you do." The vestibule door shut behind her, and the cold came across the floor and went away again.

Ellen did the building in order. Ten lane-light switches, each one checked against its label. The foul row. The fryer, off and cooling. The coffee urn, drained and unplugged. The back door, then the front, with the lock Ray's key would open until eight in the morning. She tried the handle twice.

At the road the sign buzzed on its pole. It said ETTLE LANES in red over the snowbank.

Ray's pickup sat beside the pole with its engine running and a thread of exhaust going up past the cab. She brushed the snow off her windshield, then the side mirrors, then the rear window. The pickup stayed where it was. She got in and turned the key. The engine caught on the second try, and she switched on her headlights.

Across the lot, the pickup's lights came on. It rolled down to the road and turned onto Kettle Road toward town, and its taillights got smaller until the trees took them.
