# Chapter 6 — The Window

**Days 5–7 — Tuesday to Thursday**

*Day 5 — Tuesday*

The green ledger lay open by the register, and Beth read it upside down.

Tess was ringing up two five-gallon cans of generator gas for Ned. Out back the generator held its flat roar. The woodstove kept the room at fifty-eight and not a degree over, and the floor gave its creak under Beth's boots where the sill had gone soft by the counter.

"On the book, Captain?"

"On the book. Lindqvist house and the Pratts. They'll square it in June."

The page was Thursday, November 12. The last line was in Tess's round hand: *Island Girl — diesel — 38 gal — TM — 3:52*. Under it, nothing. Blank lines to the bottom of the page.

At nine that night the float had been lit, with the red hull alongside.

Cash after hours wasn't a crime. And Tess needed the money. Eighteen hundred a month on the tank loan, and November was no month for gas.

Tess glanced up from the register and saw where Beth was looking. She drew the ledger and the spiral check-in log to the counter's edge and lined them up with it, two square corners.

Ned kept his eyes on the cans. "You somewhere warm?"

"Warm enough."

He nodded, as if that answered something. Then: "Ashby shed. Told them three years running that transfer switch was no good. Wrote it on the invoice, three years. Those Generacs, you get a bad switch, it backfeeds, it cooks the whole panel. And the shed's on a sill that's half sawdust. I said so. Walt'll tell you I said so."

He reached up and turned his hearing aid off with one finger. He took his cans and went out, and the bell knocked on the door behind him.

Nobody had asked him about the shed.

Across the store Dane stood reading the propane price card as if it might change. They'd come in by the Harbor Road from the church end, so nobody on the cove road had watched them come.

Beth set her things on the counter. Kerosene for the lantern. Strike-anywheres. A can of coffee. Eight D-cells. Saltines. Then she went to the pharmacy shelf, took down a box of condoms and set it on top of the saltines.

Tess looked at the box. Then at Beth.

"Christ on a bike, Bethie."

She picked it up and turned it over to the date before Beth could reach for it, then held it out across the counter. Beth took it and read the date anyway.

"Good till next August," Tess said. "Which is more than I can say for the chowder base." She punched the keys. "Four-ten, those cost me. Wholesale. You're paying eleven ninety-nine. I'm happy for you. I'm not a charity."

"Wouldn't want you to be."

Tess bagged the kerosene and the batteries and the box. Then she reached under the counter and dropped a Hershey bar on top. "That one's free. That's from me." Her eyes went once to Dane's back at the propane card and came home to the register.

Dane came up beside Beth as she lifted the bag. He watched the box go into it.

"I'd have asked for a receipt," he said.

Tess laughed, the big one, the one you could hear from the float.

Outside on the store dock Beth looked back through the window. Tess stood at the counter with the green ledger squared to its edge, smoothing the cover flat with both palms, and then again. Behind Beth, across the dock, the *Margaret Ann*'s radio finished one song and started the next.

---

*Day 6 — Wednesday*

At noon the ramp to the town landing pitched so steep that Beth went down it sideways, one hand on the rail.

Near low water. The float boards were green with weed and gave under her boots. Outside the harbor mouth the ledges still threw white water, but in sets now, with flat gray between. The float lights stood dark on their timer pole. Daylight. That was right.

Russ was on the float bailing the skiff he rowed out to *Second Wind*, with a bleach jug cut down for a scoop. Dane stayed at the head of the ramp, where he had the shore lane and the water both.

"Russ."

"Calloway." He kept bailing. "Somebody said you'd turned into a rumor."

"The *Margaret Ann*. You know her skipper."

He stopped. Took his cap off, looked into it, put it back on.

"Arsenault's Jolene's cousin. Makes him family the way the town dump's family. You didn't pick it, it's there, and you try not to visit." He tipped the jug over the side. "Woman called me Friday. Homeland Security, Portland, before the lines went. Said stay clear of that boat. So I stayed off that boat. I'm not a complicated man."

She watched his hands. They went back to the jug and didn't hurry.

"When are they taking you off?"

"When the sea lets them."

He looked past her at the harbor mouth, at the sets on the ledges and the gaps between them, and his lips moved. Timing the gap. She'd timed it herself at breakfast. They'd have come up with the same number.

At the top she didn't take the lane straight off the ramp. She ducked under the bait shed's roll-up door, half raised, into herring reek thick enough to coat her tongue. Between the totes. Through the dark. Out the back door onto the shore lane.

From the back door to where the lane dropped behind the seawall: fourteen steps.

Dane stood in the back doorway and watched her pace it. He didn't ask.

The frame was cut low, for men who'd been built shorter. She ducked it coming back in, and in the shed's dark his hand came to the small of her back and stayed a breath after she'd cleared it.

He rapped the wall beside the door with one knuckle. It boomed thin.

"Corrugated," he said. "Stops weather. Not rounds."

She filed that beside the fourteen.

Then she went to the back door again and put her palm flat on the wall by the frame. The cold came through the metal into her hand. She looked down the shore lane toward the cove road, with Dane behind her in the dark of the shed, and she pulled the door to and the latch dropped.

---

*Day 6 — Wednesday*

By half past three the light had gone thin over the cove, and the wind had come round west and was dropping.

Beth split kindling on the block by the fish house door. Cedar, off her father's pile. Twenty feet above her, on the bare ledge with the spruce at his back, Dane stood with the Iridium's stub antenna pointed at open sky.

The wind took half of what he said. The hatchet took more.

"Six feet by midnight." A gap. "Motor lifeboat." Another. "Zero six hundred. Town landing."

She set a round on the block and split it.

"Your office coordinated with local emergency management for the landing. Understood." Then: "Quist. Go ahead."

Some county office on the mainland, then, with a desk and a radio. She split the halves into quarters.

He came down the ledge with one hand on the rock and stopped by the block.

"Zero six hundred tomorrow. Coast Guard motor lifeboat at the town landing. Seas offshore under six feet by then. Next system's in by tomorrow evening." He watched her hands, not her face. "Miss this one and there isn't another before the ferry. A deputy meets us at the station. Safe site till Monday."

Someone else's boat. Someone else's wheel, someone else's room with someone else's lock. She gathered the kindling into the crook of her arm.

"Shore lane," she said. "Back of the bait shed. We look from the front door before anybody puts a foot on that ramp."

"Why the back?"

"The lane's below the seawall. Nobody on the water sees it. The ramp, you see from every mooring in the harbor."

"Lane. Back door. Look first." He nodded once. "You were right about the water. I'm not arguing about the landing."

He climbed back up to the ledge. He stood facing the cove path, took out the notebook and wrote. The pen moved a long time.

She went inside and opened the stove and laid the kindling on the coals.

When he came in, the fire had caught and her hand was out.

"Your watch," she said. "There's no clock in here. I'm setting it for four-thirty."

---

*Day 6 — Wednesday*

At twenty past eleven Dane went out under the sky for his call. At twenty-six past he came back in with the notebook in his hand.

He slid it into the inside pocket of the soft-shell on the nail by the door, ran the pocket zipper shut, and set the bar in its brackets.

Beth watched the pocket close. She could ask. He'd tell her, or tell her why not, with a reason attached, because that was how his no's came. She found she didn't need either one. She lifted the jacket off the door nail and hung it on the one in the post beside the stove, so the damp of the ledge would be out of it by four-thirty.

The stovepipe hummed. Wind worked at the shingles, less than at supper. Falling off.

The store bag sat on the bench by the paint-can lid with the names on it. She took out the box and set it on the bench between them.

He picked it up and read the end. "Tess check the date?"

"Tess checked the date. Then I did."

"Better kit than mine."

"Lower bar."

He put it down. He didn't reach for her. He stood with the lantern low at his back and his hands open at his sides, and she watched them stay open.

"You can stop it anywhere," he said. "Halfway. After. Anywhere."

"I know."

"Then say it."

"Yes." She took him by the front of the fleece. "Yes. Come here."

He kissed her slowly, a man with all night, which was the one thing they didn't have. She got the fleece off over his head, then the T-shirt. His watch lay on the bench beside the box, face up, the alarm where she'd set it. She turned it face down.

The cot's canvas was cold through the blanket, and then it wasn't. The drum threw its heat across her right side, hip and shoulder and the side of her face, and off the water-side window the cold came steady on her left. July on one side of her, November on the other, and Dane between.

His mouth went along her collarbone and down to her breast. By noon she'd be in a car on the mainland behind a deputy she'd never met, inside someone else's plan. She put her hands in his hair and held his head where it was, so the car would have to wait.

"You're still reporting this," she said.

He lifted his head. "Yes."

"Good. Don't change it for me."

"I won't."

She reached over the edge of the cot, found the box on the bench by feel and tore one loose. She put it in his hand, and he dealt with it without fumbling, and she liked him for that more than she'd say.

He came into her slowly. She was wet and he was hard, and it still took her a breath to make room, and she said "there," and he stopped and waited, and she moved first.

His palm was under her hip, the callus at the base of his fingers rough on her skin. Her hands were on his back, where the muscle worked.

She turned her head on the blanket to find the door.

She didn't know where it was. Behind her head, or past the stove, or somewhere off her feet. Since the night on the freight deck she had known the door in every room she stood in, how many steps, which hand for the latch. She reached for it now and didn't have it, and she let that be true, and turned her face back to his.

"Beth."

"Don't stop."

He didn't. He kept it slow when she didn't want slow, until she did, and the canvas creaked under them, and the heat on her right side and the cold on her left met somewhere in the middle of her. She came holding on to him with both arms, hard, her mouth open on his shoulder and no sound in it at all. He followed a little after, his breath stopped against her ear, and then let go of everything at once.

They lay as they were. He saw to the condom and came back under the blanket.

She laid her hand flat on his chest.

"Who are you?"

"You've seen the credentials."

"Not those."

He looked at the lantern. The wind went on falling.

"Four years ago," he said. "Boston. I had a witness."

She waited.

"Marisol Duarte. Twenty-four."

The lantern hissed on its low wick.

"She wanted to see her sister. On the way to the site. Ten minutes." He breathed out. "I said yes."

Beth kept her hand where it was.

"Parking lot," he said. "Before I had the engine off."

His heart went on under her palm, slow and level, the same at the end as at the start. Nothing in it jumped. It was the truth, all of it, and she stopped reading him and left her hand there because it was warm.

He turned his head on the blanket and looked at her. He opened his mouth as if there was more. Then he closed it, and leaned in, and kissed her instead, at the corner of her mouth first and then properly. She took it for the rest of Marisol, the part he couldn't say yet, and let him keep it.

She slept like that, her hand still on his chest and his arm across her because the cot was too narrow for anything else, and there was no door anywhere in her head. The wind went out of the shingles. The cove kept on at the ledge. The fire in the drum burned down to coals and the coals went red and quiet, and the lantern held its bead, and his watch lay face down on the bench, running toward four-thirty, and on its nail by the stove his jacket hung in the last of the heat and slowly gave up the damp of the ledge.

---

*Day 7 — Thursday*

At ten to six the bait shed smelled the way it had at noon, only colder.

Beth stood inside the roll-up door, raised to her waist, with her back to the wall. Dane had the Icom low at his ear on 22A. Outside, the harbor was the gray that comes before color. Three hours and more off low water and falling, and the ramp ran steep to the float.

At 5:52 the lifeboat's lights showed off the harbor mouth, white over red, lifting and settling on what was left of the swell.

She went under the door in a crouch to the head of the ramp and stopped where the shed's corner still covered her.

The float lights were dark.

Not their hour. In November the timer held them till seven. Ned set that timer. Ned never let those lights go dark.

She looked for the *Margaret Ann* at the store dock. The dock was empty. She found the red hull on a mooring off the fish pier, one Beth had never seen her use, with a straight line across open water to this float. Her deck lights were out. Every night for a week she'd burned end to end. This morning she was black.

On the float, tied short on the outside, lay a skiff. Gray fiberglass, high-sided, no name, no patches. Not island. Not the dragger's aluminum sixteen. Its outboard was down, the lower unit in the water. Nobody tied up to a float in a swell with the leg down to grind on the weed. Not unless he meant to leave fast.

"No," she said. "Not this. Back."

Dane was at her shoulder. He didn't ask.

"Float lights are out. Dragger's moved to where she can see the float, with her lights off. That skiff's from away with its leg down."

He keyed the Icom. "Coast Guard motor lifeboat, this is Deputy Reyes on two-two alpha. Abort, abort. Landing is not secure. Do not approach. Over."

"Reyes, Coast Guard. Copy abort. Standing off. Out."

Down on the float the skiff's outboard coughed and caught.

They went back under the door. Between the totes, through the herring and the dark. Her hand found the back door's latch where she'd left it. Behind them the outboard rose to a howl as the skiff ran off the float and turned.

She got the door open.

The shot came through the wall. Corrugated metal banged a foot behind her head and a coin of gray light opened in it. Dane was already at her back, between her and the water side, and he jerked against her and drove her through the door.

"Fuck," he said. One word, through his teeth.

Gravel. The lane. Fourteen steps to the seawall.

A second shot clanged into the shed somewhere behind them and went nowhere.

Off the harbor mouth the lifeboat's lights swung, showed red only, and went away.

The Icom in Dane's fist came alive. "Any station, any station, this is Cutter Fire, Chief Grindle, at the firehouse. Was that gunfire at the town landing? Does anyone require the EMT? Over."

Nobody answered him.

They ran down the shore lane under the seawall, Dane's right hand clamped over his left arm and blood coming dark between his fingers, while out across the harbor the outboard went small, and smaller.

---

*Day 7 — Thursday*

She took them home by the grown-over path off the cove road, not the road, through alders that slapped wet across their faces. At the bend where the path looked back down on the lane she stopped and looked once. Empty lane. Gray water. Nothing coming.

Inside the fish house she sat him on the bench, put his own right hand back over the wound and hers on top of it, and pressed.

"Hold it. Harder than that."

He held it. The blood had run to his wrist and was drying there.

"I have to report."

"You're bleeding."

"It only works outside." He stood, and took the sat phone out onto the ledge under the low sky. The wind had already backed.

Through the open door she heard his side of it.

"They had the time." A long gap. "No. Nobody." Another. "Only us."

She stood at the stove and laid it out in a row.

She had heard him set the time. Zero six hundred, town landing. She had heard him say it back to Portland. The only people she knew of who had the hour and the place were the man on the ledge and the voices he gave his day to every night.

Three days and a storm until the ferry. No other way off.

Out on the ledge the first fine rain came over the spruce and settled on his shoulders, darkening the fleece. He kept the phone at his ear and his bloody hand clamped on his arm.

She had moved. She stood on the stove side of the room with the door in front of her, and she knew it was six steps to it without counting them.

She was keeping an exit again.
