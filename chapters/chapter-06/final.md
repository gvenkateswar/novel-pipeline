# Chapter 6 — The Window

**Days 5–7 — Tuesday to Thursday**

*Day 5 — Tuesday*

The green ledger lay open by the register, and Beth read it upside down.

Tess was ringing up two five-gallon cans of generator gas for Ned. The woodstove held the room at fifty-eight, and the floor creaked where the sill had gone soft by the counter.

"On the book, Captain?"

"On the book. Lindqvist house and the Pratts. They'll square it in June."

The page was Thursday, November 12. The last line was in Tess's round hand: *Island Girl — diesel — 38 gal — TM — 3:52*. Under it, nothing. Blank lines to the bottom of the page.

At nine that night the float had been lit, the red hull alongside.

Cash after hours wasn't a crime, and Tess needed the money. Eighteen hundred a month on the tank loan, and November was no month for gas.

Tess glanced up from the register and saw where Beth was looking. She drew the ledger and the spiral check-in log to the counter's edge and lined them up with it, two square corners.

Ned kept his eyes on the cans. "You somewhere warm?"

"Warm enough."

He nodded, as if that answered something. Then: "Ashby shed. Told them three years running that transfer switch was no good. Wrote it on the invoice. That shed's on a sill that's half sawdust. Walt'll tell you I said so."

He reached up and turned his hearing aid off with one finger, took his cans and went out.

Nobody had asked him about the shed.

Across the store Dane stood reading the propane price card as if it might change. They'd come in by the Harbor Road from the church end, so nobody on the cove road had watched them come.

Beth set her things on the counter. Kerosene for the lantern. Strike-anywheres. A can of coffee. Eight D-cells. Saltines. Then she went to the pharmacy shelf, took down a box of condoms and set it on top of the saltines.

Tess looked at the box. Then at Beth.

"Christ on a bike, Bethie."

She picked it up and turned it over to the date before Beth could reach for it, then held it out across the counter. Beth took it and read the date anyway.

"Good till next August," Tess said. "Which is more than I can say for the chowder base." She punched the keys. "Four-ten, those cost me. Wholesale. You're paying eleven ninety-nine. I'm happy for you. I'm not a charity."

"Wouldn't want you to be."

Tess bagged the kerosene and the batteries and the box. Then she reached under the counter and dropped a Hershey bar on top. "That one's free. That's from me." Her eyes went once to Dane's back at the propane card and came home to the register.

Dane came up beside Beth as she lifted the bag. He watched the box go into it.

"I'd have asked for a receipt," he said.

Tess laughed, the big one, the one you could hear from the float.

Outside on the store dock Beth looked back through the window. Tess stood at the counter with the green ledger squared to its edge, smoothing the cover flat with both palms, and then again. Behind Beth, across the dock, the *Margaret Ann*'s radio finished one song and started the next.

---

*Day 6 — Wednesday*

At noon the ramp to the town landing ran at an easy pitch on the half flood, and Beth went down it with one hand on the rail.

The float boards were green with weed and gave under her boots. Outside the harbor mouth the ledges still threw white water, but in sets now, with flat gray between. The float lights stood dark on their timer pole. Daylight. That was right.

Russ was on the float bailing the skiff he rowed out to *Second Wind*, with a dented coffee can. Dane stayed at the head of the ramp, where he had the shore lane and the water both.

"Russ."

"Calloway." He kept bailing. "Somebody said you'd turned into a rumor."

"The *Margaret Ann*. You know her skipper."

He stopped. Took his cap off, looked into it, put it back on.

"Arsenault's Jolene's cousin. Makes him family the way the town dump's family. You didn't pick it, it's there, and you try not to visit." He tipped the can over the side. "Woman called me Friday. Homeland Security, Portland, before the lines went. Said stay clear of that boat. So I stayed off that boat. I'm not a complicated man."

She watched his hands. They went back to the can and didn't hurry.

"How long you got him for?"

"Till the sea says otherwise."

He looked past her at the harbor mouth, at the sets on the ledges and the gaps between them, and his lips moved. Timing the gaps, the way anybody on a mooring would.

At the top she didn't take the lane straight off the ramp. She walked every way off a float before she needed it. Her father had, and the habit had outlived his boat. She ducked under the bait shed's roll-up door, half raised, into herring reek thick enough to coat her tongue. Between the totes. Through the dark. Out the back door onto the shore lane.

From the back door to where the lane dropped behind the seawall: fourteen steps.

Dane stood in the back doorway and watched her pace it. He didn't ask.

The frame was cut low, for men who'd been built shorter. She ducked it coming back in, and in the shed's dark his hand came to the small of her back and stayed a breath after she'd cleared it.

He rapped the wall beside the door with one knuckle. It boomed thin.

"Corrugated," he said. "Stops weather. Not rounds."

She filed that beside the fourteen.

Then she went to the back door again and put her palm flat on the wall by the frame. The cold came through the metal into her hand. She looked down the shore lane toward the cove road, with Dane behind her in the dark of the shed. Then she pulled the door to.

They came out under the roll-up door into the noon light. The cold of the wall stayed in her palm, and she kept that hand shut in her coat pocket all the way to the cove road, with the harbor gray and flat beyond the seawall.

---

*Day 6, afternoon*

By half past three the light had gone thin over the cove, and the wind had come round west and was dropping.

Beth split kindling on the block by the fish house door. Cedar, off her father's pile. Twenty feet above her, on the bare ledge with the spruce at his back, Dane stood with the Iridium's stub antenna pointed at open sky.

The wind took half of what he said. The hatchet took more.

"Six feet by midnight." A gap. "Motor lifeboat." Another. "Zero six hundred. Town landing."

She set a round on the block and split it.

"Your office coordinated with local emergency management for the landing. Understood." Then: "Quist. Go ahead."

Local to him meant the mainland. Some county office in Pruett with a desk and a radio, the people who'd have to clear the ramp on that side. The island's version of that was Tess with a clipboard, and nobody in Portland phoned Tess. She split the halves into quarters.

He came down the ledge with one hand on the rock and stopped by the block.

"Zero six hundred tomorrow. Coast Guard motor lifeboat at the town landing. Seas offshore under six feet by then. Next system's in by tomorrow evening." He watched her hands, not her face. "Miss this one and there isn't another before the ferry. A deputy meets us at the station. Safe site till Monday."

Someone else's boat. Someone else's wheel, someone else's room with someone else's lock. She gathered the kindling into the crook of her arm.

"Shore lane," she said. "Back of the bait shed. We look from the front door before anybody puts a foot on that ramp."

"Why the back?"

"The lane's below the seawall. Nobody on the water sees it. The ramp, you see from every mooring in the harbor."

"Lane. Back door. Look first." He nodded once. "You were right about the water. I'm not arguing about the landing."

He climbed back to the ledge, faced the cove path, took out the notebook and wrote until the page ran out of light.

She went inside and opened the stove and laid the kindling on the coals.

When he came in, the fire had caught and her hand was out.

"Your watch," she said. "There's no clock in here. I'm setting it for four-thirty."

---

*Day 6, night*

At twenty past eleven Dane went out under the sky for his call. At twenty-six past he came back in, slid the notebook into the inside pocket of the soft-shell on the door nail, ran the zipper shut and set the bar in its brackets.

Beth watched the pocket close. Then she lifted the jacket off the door nail and hung it on the one in the post beside the stove, so the damp of the ledge would be out of it by four-thirty.

She took the box out of the store bag and set it on the bench beside his watch. Tess's Hershey bar stayed in the bag. He read the end of the box, the way he read everything, and put it down.

He didn't reach for her. He stood with the lantern low at his back and his hands open at his sides, and she watched them stay open.

"You can stop it anywhere," he said. "Halfway. After. Anywhere."

"I know."

"Then say it."

"Yes." She took him by the front of the fleece. "Come here."

He kissed her slowly, a man with all night, which was the one thing they didn't have. She got the fleece off over his head, then the T-shirt, and turned his watch face down on the bench. He laughed once, through his nose, at the watch.

The cot frame complained when it took them both. Her father had built it for one man and a nap between hauls. The canvas under the blanket was salt-stiff and smelled of old oilskins and hot iron.

His mouth went along her collarbone and down to her breast, and she arched to meet it. By noon she'd be in a car on the mainland behind a deputy she'd never met, inside someone else's plan. She put her hands in his hair and held his head where it was, so the car would have to wait.

"You're still reporting this," she said.

He lifted his head. "Yes."

"Good. Don't change it for me."

"I won't."

She found the box by feel on the cot rail, tore one loose and put it in his hand.

He came into her slowly, and though she was wet and he was hard it still took her a breath to make room. She said "there," and he stopped and waited, and she moved first.

Above them a hank of her father's net twine hung from a rafter nail and swung each time the frame took their weight. She watched it swing, then shut her eyes.

She turned her head on the blanket to find the door.

She didn't know where it was. Behind her head, or past the stove, or off her feet somewhere. Since the night on the freight deck she had known the door in every room she stood in, and which hand for the latch. She reached for it now and didn't have it, and she let that be true, and turned her face back to his.

"Beth."

"Don't stop."

He didn't. He kept it slow when she didn't want slow, until she did, and the cot frame knocked the wall planks, slow, then not. The lantern hissed on its low wick. She came with both arms locked around him and her heels dug into the canvas, no sound in her at all. He followed, breath stopped against her ear, and let go of everything at once.

He saw to the condom and came back under the blanket. Her fingers found the inside of his wrist and stayed there, two of them, where she'd take a man's pulse on deck.

"Who are you?"

"You've seen the credentials."

"Not those."

He looked at the lantern for a while.

"Four years ago," he said. "Boston. I had a witness."

Ten minutes. He'd said that much in the Ashby shed, with diesel in his knuckles, and wiped his hands twice and given her nothing after. She'd let him keep the rest then.

"Marisol Duarte. Twenty-four. She had a sister in Dorchester she hadn't seen in a year. It wasn't on the route." He breathed out. "I said yes."

Beth kept her fingers where they were.

"Parking lot," he said. "Before I had the engine off."

Under her fingers his pulse went on slow and level, the same at the end as at the start. Nothing in it jumped. It was the truth, all of it, and she stopped reading him and left her hand there because his wrist was warm.

He turned his head on the blanket and looked at her. He opened his mouth as if there was more. Then he closed it, and leaned in, and kissed her instead, at the corner of her mouth first and then properly. She took it for the rest of Marisol, the part he couldn't say yet, and let him keep it.

She slept with his arm across her because the cot was too narrow for anything else. By six there would be the lane, the bait shed, the ramp, in that order. The drum burned down to coals and smelled of hot oil and old rust, and the lantern burned on. His watch lay face down on the bench, running toward four-thirty, and on its nail by the stove his jacket gave up the damp of the ledge.

---

*Day 7 — Thursday*

At ten to six the bait shed smelled the way it had at noon, only colder.

Beth stood inside the roll-up door, raised to her waist, with her back to the wall. Dane had the Icom low at his ear on 22A. Outside, the harbor was the gray that comes before color. Two hours past high water and going out, and the ramp ran steep to the float.

At 5:52 the lifeboat's lights showed off the harbor mouth, white over red, lifting and settling on what was left of the swell.

She went under the door in a crouch to the head of the ramp and stopped where the shed's corner still covered her.

The float lights were dark.

Not their hour. In November the timer held them till seven. Ned set that timer. Ned never let those lights go dark.

She looked for the *Margaret Ann* at the store dock. The dock was empty. She found the red hull on a mooring off the fish pier, one she'd never seen her use, with a straight line across open water to this float. Her deck lights were out. Every night for a week she'd burned end to end. This morning she was black.

On the float, tied short on the outside, lay a skiff. Gray fiberglass, high-sided, no name, no patches. Not island. Not the dragger's aluminum sixteen. Its outboard was down, the lower unit in the water. Nobody tied up to a float in a swell with the leg down to grind on the weed. Not unless he meant to leave fast.

"No," she said. "Not this. Back."

Dane was at her shoulder. He tipped the Icom to read its channel.

"Float lights are out. Dragger's moved to where she can see the float, with her lights off. That skiff's from away with its leg down."

He keyed the Icom. "Coast Guard motor lifeboat, this is Deputy Reyes on two-two alpha. Abort, abort. Landing is not secure. Do not approach. Over."

"Reyes, Coast Guard. Copy abort. Standing off. Out."

Down on the float the skiff's outboard coughed and caught.

They went back under the door. Between the totes, through the herring and the dark. Her hand found the back door's latch where she'd left it. Behind them the outboard rose to a howl as the skiff ran off the float and turned.

She got the door open.

The crack came off the water, flat under the outboard's howl. Sixty yards out, by the sound. The skiff had turned broadside to the float, the shed's front square in its line.

Corrugated metal banged at the harbor end. The round came through. It went by a foot behind her head, where Dane was, between her and the harbor, and he jerked against her. Beside the frame a coin of gray light opened in the back wall. He drove her through the door.

"Fuck," he said. One word, through his teeth.

Gravel. The lane. Fourteen steps to the seawall.

A second shot clanged into the shed somewhere behind them and went nowhere.

Off the harbor mouth the lifeboat's lights swung, showed red only, and went away.

The Icom in Dane's fist came alive. "Any station, any station, this is Cutter Fire, Chief Grindle, at the firehouse. Was that gunfire at the town landing? Does anyone require the EMT? Over."

Nobody answered him.

They ran down the shore lane under the seawall. Dane's right hand was clamped over his left arm above the elbow, where the round had grazed him through the sleeve. Blood came dark between his fingers. Out across the harbor the outboard went small, and smaller.

---

*Day 7, after*

She took them home by the grown-over path off the cove road, not the road, through alders that slapped wet across their faces. At the bend where the path looked back down on the lane she stopped and looked once. Empty lane. Gray water. Nothing coming.

Inside the fish house she sat him on the bench, put his own right hand back over the wound and hers on top of it, and pressed.

"Hold it. Harder than that."

He held it. The blood had run to his wrist and was drying there.

"I have to report."

"You're bleeding."

"It only works outside." He stood, and took the sat phone out onto the ledge under the low sky. The wind had already backed.

She heard enough through the wall. The lifeboat had stood off until the shot went out on the air, and then its rules had taken it home: no landing under fire, no second try with a system coming in by dark. The window was shut.

Through the open door she heard his side of it.

"They had the time." A long gap. "No. Nobody." Another. "Only us."

She stood at the stove and laid it out in a row.

She had heard him set the time. Zero six hundred, town landing. She had heard him say it back to Portland. The only people she knew of who had the hour and the place were the man on the ledge and the voices he gave his day to every night.

Three days and a storm until the ferry. No other way off.

Out on the ledge the first fine rain came over the spruce and settled on his shoulders, darkening the fleece. He kept the phone at his ear and his bloody hand clamped on his arm.

She had moved. She stood on the stove side of the room with the door in front of her, and she knew exactly where it was.

She was keeping an exit again.
