# Chapter Ten: Ice-Out

The check mark went in beside the first circle, small and square, in blue ballpoint.

Judy Ostrowski had her glasses down off her head and Ellen's books squared in front of her: register reports, bank statements, the tape from Saturday night. On top lay the sheet from December with its two circled numbers. The butterscotch bowl sat where it had sat in December. So did the lake trout on the wall, eleven pounds four ounces.

Judy set the pen beside the second circle and left it there.

"I'm a banker today," she said.

Out in the lobby the drive-up tube thunked. Heels crossed the marble and stopped.

Judy turned the February statement around on the desk and laid one finger under the last line. Twenty-two thousand one hundred forty dollars, on the twenty-eighth.

Ellen had not taken the chair. "Saturday's receipts went into the floor safe. Three thousand four hundred sixty dollars. The roads closed at four, and Sunday I was closed. They came in through your drive-up at eight this morning."

"I have the slip." Judy didn't look at it. "Here's the rule, so we're reading the same one. The commitment says cash on hand on February twenty-eighth. The underwriter reads 'on hand' as money he can see. On Sunday he couldn't see your safe."

"I could."

"I know you could."

Ellen looked at the statement. Every figure on it was correct. She had made that number herself, at two-fifteen on Saturday, with a red marker.

"I do not have an answer for that," she said.

"I didn't think you would." Judy picked up the phone. "I'm going to call him."

She pointed at the door with the receiver. Ellen went out, and the door shut behind her.

Through the glass Judy listened and wrote and listened. The pen went back to the second circle and stayed. For thirteen weeks every number had been Ellen's to move, and she had moved them. She knew this one to the dollar, and she could not sequence a phone call she was not on. Her face would look exactly like this right up until Judy shook her head, and nobody in the lobby would know the difference. There were fourteen butterscotch candies in the bowl. She counted them again, under her breath, and got fourteen.

Judy opened the door with the receiver held against her cardigan.

"One question. Did any of what was in your safe belong to somebody else?"

"Negative. None of it." Ellen kept her hands at her sides. "The derby's half of the raffle went into HELD on its own slip, same window, same minute. The meals tax has been in HELD since December. You have both slips."

Judy had both slips. She had the HELD statement too, three months of it, and she ran her pen down the deposits one line at a time.

"Not one dollar crossed over," she said. "I'll read him that part."

She went back in and shut the door.

It took six minutes. Then Judy came out without the phone and stood across the desk from her.

"Approved. With a condition. You keep twenty-five thousand minimum on deposit, reported to me monthly, through next February. He wants to see the safe from now on." She didn't soften it. "Closing's at one, at Leclerc's."

"Accepted," Ellen said.

Judy held out her hand, and Ellen shook it.

"Congratulations. Don't celebrate until it funds."

* * *

The radiator under the window knocked twice and quit. Judy's phone buzzed on the table.

"Funded," she said. "Two-eleven."

The lawyer printed the wire confirmation and handed it across to Arlene. Arlene licked her thumb and counted it.

"One," she said. "That's all of it."

She took the 1996 ledger out of her tote and set it on the table in front of Ellen, green cloth, the loan strip still standing up out of the pages. DUE 03/01. Ellen uncapped her pen and printed RETD 03/01 underneath it in block capitals.

On the wall the 1950s map of the lake showed the narrows twice as wide as they were. The lawyer brought the embosser down on the last page. Its hinge went down and came up without a sound.

Ellen had carried the question since December, and she had not asked it, because she had two guesses and liked neither. Arlene might have picked her for the size of the check. She might have picked her for not being Ray. Either way the whole winter would turn out to be something that had been done to her.

"Arlene. Why me?"

Arlene was buttoning her cardigan. She did not stop.

"You asked me the name of my boiler man before you asked my price. I wrote it on the back of your offer."

That was all she gave it. She stood and got into her coat, and her keys came out of the cardigan pocket with the tissues. A new key on a white paper tag read THE PINES 2B. Beside it hung a brass key with an orange tag, R.G. — 1996, in her own green hand.

Ellen looked at it.

"Raymond brought it back yesterday, with more tape than I'll ever use," Arlene said. "He said it was a loan."

Down on Main Street the van from The Pines tapped its horn.

"Voyons donc. He's paid by the hour." Arlene went to the door.

The stairs to the street were steep and narrow. Ellen offered her arm at the top. Arlene went down one step at a time with the keys in her fist and her other hand on the rail.

* * *

Denise had the pool sheet out from under the crossword and her pen uncapped, and she didn't look up.

"December first, nobody. February second." She tapped the line drawn through it. "'They split.' Whoever wrote that crossed it out himself Saturday, which I'll give him. March first, nobody. All winter, nobody." She wrote across the bottom of the sheet in pen. NO WINNER. POT TO DERBY KIDS DIV. "There."

"It goes in HELD."

"Honey, I know where it goes. You want to know what the dates tell you? December's all men with snowblowers that won't start, they figure everything quits early. January's the Rotary, they wanted to be right in time for the banquet. February was the hopeful ones. They gave you long enough to break something expensive, and you did, and you still didn't fold, and they're sore about it." She capped the pen. "Gerry's sorest."

She folded the sheet in thirds so the bottom row stayed on the outside. WEDDING DATE. She slid it back under the crossword and filled in seventeen across.

Ellen went to the pegboard by the pit door. Ray's card had a new line at the bottom in his square capitals.

03/01 / 1540 / LANE 07 / WAITING

Lane 7 was the first machine that had died on her, at eight-eleven on the first night.

She was already walking toward the steel door by lane 10.

"He hasn't run a machine back there," Denise said, still in seventeen across. "I'd have heard it."

* * *

The drop light hung on its hook over lane 7. Under the raised table the ten pins stood on the deck, each on its spot, nothing wrong with any of them. Out front the baseboard ticked. Ray sat on the kickback down in the pit with his forearms on his knees.

"Fourteen minutes, Mrs. Daly."

She looked at her watch. "Fifteen and a half."

"Arlene count it?"

"One page. With her thumb."

He nodded as though that were news.

The settee bolts on lane 7 had been tightened. So had the hook the drop light hung from, and the hinge on the machine's cover, and the screw on the catwalk rail beside her hand.

"The settee," Ellen said. "The hook. The cover hinge. This rail."

"Rail's been loose since 1990."

"It was loose this morning."

He looked at his hands. "I'll still invoice you."

"I'd expect it."

"Tuesdays I want out front. Bowling, with the team. Somebody else can listen to the tables. I'm sixty-one. I eat supper at a quarter past five, and I'm going to fix the hinges on anything you own whether you ask or not. I love you."

He didn't get up.

Ellen came to the end of the catwalk and crouched on the step above him, and for once she was the one looking down. She had said this once already, on the second of February, to her own windshield, somewhere inside six minutes and twenty-eight seconds. She had got every word of it right, and he had been forty yards away in a dark truck.

"At your truck I had it backward," she said. "It was you first. I wanted you before I knew whether I could carry this building. If I had taken your money, you would have spent the rest of your life wondering which of the two I wanted, and I would have let you. I love you."

He sat there. The drop light hummed on its hook.

She put her hand flat against the side of his face, where the morning's shave had missed a patch along the jaw, and leaned down and kissed him. Behind him the ten pins stood under the table in their triangle. Her shoulder knocked the drop light, and it swung, and the shadows of the pins went one way across the deck and came back.

"Ellen," Ray said.

"Yes."

He stood up out of the pit and held out his hand to help her up off the step. She took it. Her left knee went off like a cap pistol.

"That's the knee," she said. "It's from 2009. We don't discuss it."

Ray laughed. It came up out of him all at once, loud enough to carry past the pit door, and he kept on, with her hand still in his.

* * *

By the seventeenth of April the lake had gone black along the edges, and what ice was left in the middle was the color of old pewter. The bob-houses had been off since the first. The town ran an ice-out pool now, and Hollis called it: ice-out was the morning he could row from the town dock to the public access without touching ice. He had called it Thursday at 6:10, from the boat. Ellen had bought Monday the twelfth at nine a.m. and missed by two days, twenty-one hours, and ten minutes. She had told him the margin twice.

He came in with the transformer box under his arm. The sign shop in Laconia had wired a test tag to the handle.

On the strip board behind the counter, between a ninth birthday and the Rotary, hung a strip in Ellen's capitals that hadn't been there Thursday.

MX / R.G.

There was no end date on it. He looked at it and set the box on the counter.

Linda's 187 card was still in the case across the room. He had asked Ellen once, in March, whether it stayed. She had said a record was a record, and gone back to the schedule.

"Is that transformer certified?" Ellen said.

He turned the box so the tag faced her. She put her glasses on and read it, every line: the shop, the date, the man's initials, BENCH TESTED, the voltage, OK.

"Thank you," she said, and let the glasses down on their cord. "Denise. I'd like a square on the wedding row."

Denise slid the sheet out from under the crossword. It was still folded with that row on the outside. NORMIE, JUNE 12. D. LAVOIE, NEVER, PAID IN FULL. DANNY, OCT.

"Honey, you don't get to bet on your own flight." She put the sheet back. "Danny's had October since January. Says it's his slow month after roof rakes, and he can spare the help."

Ray leaned on the counter. "You figure on asking me first, then?"

"I'd like to get the order right this time."

He didn't say anything to that. He picked up the ladder from where it leaned by the vestibule and went out.

The commitment letter had run out on March 31 in his coat pocket. On April 1 a woman from the lender called to ask whether he'd like another extension, and he told her no, thank you. She asked if there was a reason, for her form. He told her he was spoken for, and heard her type it.

At dusk the ladder stood against the pole at the road. Ellen held it at the foot with both hands. She hired out everything she wasn't qualified for, and she let him. He opened the disconnect, took the old transformer out, set the new one in its place, and made up the connections the way he had done the store's sign for thirty years. The town had called the place Ettle's for seven of them.

He keyed the radio on his collar. "Counter, Gagnon. Throw the sign."

"Thrown," Denise said.

The sign buzzed and caught. Up against it, all Ray could see was red glass and his own hands.

"How's it read?"

Ellen looked up at it from the road.

"Both words."
