# Chapter Eight: Position Relief

Ray set the two rolls of packing tape on the kitchen table, between a stack of face-down photographs and a box marked THE PINES, UNIT 2B, KITCHEN in green ink. Arlene moved into The Pines on the first of March, and he had offered to come up and tape.

"By the ledger," Arlene said. "Off it."

He moved them off it. The 1996 book lay open on the oilcloth, its green cloth gone gray at the corners. A strip of masking tape ran down the spine in block capitals: 1996 — LENT A. FORTIER 02/05 — DUE 03/01.

He knew those capitals from every strip on Ellen's board. The ledgers had come with the building, every one from 1971 on, so the book was Ellen's now. She had lent Arlene her own 1996 back, with a due date on it.

Arlene turned the ledger toward him and laid the rim of her magnifier under a line halfway down September. *Key to R.G.* Below it, in the same ink, *no charge.*

"That's the only line I ever gave you in the body of the book," she said. "After that you're in the margins."

The scanner on the counter clicked and spoke, low. A plow truck off the road at the bend on Dock Road, driver out and walking.

"That's the Ledoux boy." Arlene didn't turn her head. "His father took that bend the same way in 1988 and took the town's mailbox with him." She tapped the page. "Where was I."

"The margins."

"I know where the margins are, Raymond. I kept them."

Wintergreen came up through the floorboards from the pharmacy, and somewhere below a door chime went twice.

"You told me first crack," he said.

Arlene looked up.

"In this kitchen. At the counter with the drawer open. And at the Legion hall after Linda's funeral, with your hand on my arm, while Danny was still shaking hands at the door." His voice stayed low. "First crack. You said it that day."

"I said it," Arlene said. "Twice. I meant it both times."

The building on Kettle Road was the last room Linda had been in that nobody had rearranged. Since December he had practiced what he would say to an eighty-four-year-old woman in a cardigan. None of it had made it up her stairs with the tape.

Arlene took a tissue from her sleeve, looked at it, and put it back.

"Everybody on Main Street knew your bank was coming. I knew before you did. I sold to her anyway." She folded the magnifier shut on its chain. "Voyons donc. You'd have kept the lineage at seven-fifty and Linda's ball in the locker, and lost money every league night polishing that trophy case, the same as Normand and I did for thirty years. She asked me what the league paid before she asked me the price of the building. I knew she'd raise it, and she did, her first winter, and lost two teams for it. I'll take my money from somebody who does that."

"Does she know why you picked her?" Ray said.

"She hasn't asked."

The scanner reported a dog loose on the ice off the town beach. Arlene gave it no opinion. She pulled the tape rolls across the oilcloth, licked her thumb, and touched each one.

"Two," she said. "I asked for three."

Ray was already on the stairs, going down for the third.

* * *

The text had come at 2:31.

*Lane 4 table hanging on the reset. 3x since noon. Lane closed. Work order. Rate per our agreement.*

Ray came in the back door at 2:40, past a lot full of Massachusetts plates. He wrote 02/13 / 1440 / LANE 04 / TABLE on the card by the pit door.

Ellen met him there with her reading glasses down on their cord.

"It hung at twelve-ten, one-forty, and two twenty-six," she said. "I closed it after the third."

"Belt." He had heard it from the catwalk. The table came down, caught half an inch above the deck, and dropped the rest, like a man missing the last stair in the dark.

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes. Thirty, if it's worn through."

"I have six parties on the waiting list."

"Then twenty."

In the pit he got the drop light on its hook and the cover off. The belt had a worn spot the size of a thumbnail. A spare hung on the third nail from the end, in his own tape. He had it off the nail when a voice came down the lane at him.

"Hey."

A boy of about eight was lying flat on the approach of lane 4 with his chin on the foul line, looking down sixty feet of maple at the light under the table.

"Do you live back there?"

Ray considered it. "Saturdays."

The boy scrambled up and ran for the settees. "Dad. Dad. He said *Saturdays*." It carried over nine lanes. A man in a Patriots cap looked down the lane at the pit and lifted one hand.

Eleven minutes later the new belt was on. Ray cycled the table four times, and it settled onto the deck clean each time. At 3:02 he wrote his time out on the card.

At the counter he laid a Gagnon & Son slip in front of her. She read it, wrote the check, and tore it out along the perforation.

"Thank you, Mr. Gagnon."

"Ms. Daly."

Seventeen days ago she had held a fistful of his jacket on her own cellar stairs.

The Open clipboard sat by the register, and he read it across the counter while she wrote. Nine lines left.

He put the check in his shirt pocket and went down the side of the snack bar toward lane 1, as far as the trophy case.

The photograph on the 187 card showed Linda mid-release, her right foot sliding and her left arm out. It was the only way he had ever seen that string.

Linda would have entered the Open the first day the clipboard went up. She'd have paid cash, written her name on line one in big loops, and then leaned on the counter and asked Ellen exactly where the entry fee went, to the dollar.

Locker 14 was beside the case. He lifted the latch. Her bag sat where she had set it, blue vinyl, the zipper pull a loop of his picture wire. He took it out by the handles and left the locker door standing open.

He walked back up past the snack bar and the counter. Denise sprayed a pair of size nines, toe, heel. Ellen had the phone at her ear and a pencil on the strip board.

"Lane four," she said. "Three-fifteen. Four bowlers."

* * *

The release lay on the desk, RELEASE OF RIGHT OF FIRST REFUSAL typed across the top and his name typed in it twice. LECLERC was lettered on the door glass in gold.

"I'll need a photo ID," the lawyer said.

Ray took out his wallet and slid the license from behind its window. The bank's commitment letter came out with it, folded in quarters and soft at the creases since December. The lawyer picked it up to move it, read the letterhead, and laid it beside the license.

The developer in Concord was a real man with a real phone number, and Ray still believed every word he had said about him in her parking lot. But as long as his name sat on that right at the registry, there was a jack post under her floor.

He signed at the first flag. The radiator under the window banged once. He signed at the second, and it banged again.

The lawyer brought out the embosser. It was cast iron and older than she was, and she had to stand to get her weight on it.

"There's a line here for the reason," she said. "It isn't required."

"No."

She stamped it, signed below the seal, and squared the pages on the blotter. Over her shoulder a 1950s map of the lake hung framed under glass, every camp on the north shore drawn in by hand.

"I'll record it this afternoon. After that it's public. Anybody at the registry can pull it."

Ray put the license back behind its window. The letter went into the inside pocket of his coat.

"Do you want a copy sent to Ellen Daly?"

"No."

The lawyer capped her pen. "When will you tell her?"

* * *

Tuesday, eleven-thirty at night. Taylor's Marina and the Legion had finished their league strings at Pine Ridge two hours ago, and nobody from either team had gone home.

Ray laced his own shoes on a molded blue settee at the far end of the house. Back at Kettle Lanes, Hollis had Gagnon & Son's sheet. The place smelled of pizza and of the carpet glue Gil had been promising since 2008 would wear off.

The two teams had walked over a price, and nobody on Main Street was going to talk them back to it. Every man on the Marina would drive forty minutes to watch the 112 beat Ray Gagnon, and Gil had the lanes.

He had called Gil on Sunday, and Gil had let him finish the whole sentence.

"The Marina has bowled one Tuesday for me and paid for it on the Friday," Gil had said. "And I've wanted you on my lanes for thirty years, Ray. Bring your shoes."

Denise sat at the scorer's table with a Kettle Lanes paper sheet and her own pencil. Linda's bag sat on the carpet by her feet.
Gil came down the approach in his overcoat with the wireless mic in his gloved hand.

"House rules, Ray. Three strings, total pins, my lane, my house ball. You'll forgive the ball." Gil's audience was eight bowlers, Denise, and Ray, and his voice came out of every speaker in the building.

Ray took a gray house ball off the rack, chipped at one edge.

"Mrs. Lavoie's sheet is official alongside my screen," Gil said. "Where the two disagree, we stop and count." He turned to the two teams on the snack-counter stools. "If Mr. Gagnon wins, Taylor's Marina bowls Tuesdays at Kettle Lanes at twelve-fifty, starting the twenty-third. If our friend from the Marina wins, Mr. Gagnon bowls Tuesdays here with the Marina through April."

The 112 was already standing, his ball in both hands. "I've asked him for this since 1996," he told the room. "He always had a table down somewhere."

The Legion captain raised a hand off his knee. "We came up in the one van. If the Marina's back at Kettle Lanes, so are we."

Ray's first ball left his hand high and came down a yard past where he meant it.

Nothing had ridden on a string of his in six years, not since Linda. Her ball sat four feet away in the bag he had carried in himself.

In the sixth box he lofted the first ball again and heard it land.

"Holy mackerel."

He heard himself say it. Denise wrote a zero in the box. Ray finished the string at 84. The 112 had 108.

Between strings the 112 went to Gil. "I want fifteen. Sixteen's pulling left."

"Fifteen and sixteen were resurfaced the same week in 2008 by the same man," Gil said. "You'll stay where you are."

He stopped at the scorer's table on his way back. "Mrs. Lavoie. I watched you pick up a four-horse with your third ball in 2015. I still think about it."

"Second ball," Denise said, and went back to her sheet. She wrote something in the margin, erased it, and wrote it again.

In the second string Ray stood on the approach with the gray ball and looked at the lane.

He brought his hand down six inches and let the ball go along the boards. It ran true. He finished the string at 117. The 112 had 115.

By the third string Ray was taking back a pin or two a box. The 112 bowled his tenth, took three, and sat down on the stools with his total. Ray stood on the approach for his own tenth while Denise ran the column twice with her pencil.

"Nine to win," she said.

His first ball chopped. It took four off the right side and left two pins lying flat on the deck. His second took three more. Three still stood at the left edge in a crooked line, and in front of them lay one fallen pin, crossways, its head pointed toward the far side of the deck.

He stood with the third ball against his hip and looked at that fallen pin until the 112 shifted on his stool.

He put the third ball low along the left boards, at the base of the fallen pin. The ball caught it square and the pin swung like a gate, slid across the deck on its side, and cut the back two out from under themselves. The ball went on through and took the last one.

Gil read the totals off Denise's paper. "Three hundred thirty-four. Three hundred thirty-two." He switched off the mic. "Mr. Gagnon."

The 112 looked from the screen to the paper and back. They matched.

"Well," the Legion captain said, and zipped his jacket. "That's the van, then."

The 112 was halfway to the door with his coat on, the Marina behind him.

"Tuesday. Twelve-fifty," the 112 said over his shoulder. "Don't expect me to like it." He stopped with a hand on the push bar. "Thirty years of us," he said to Ray, "and you picked the Daly woman over the league."

Ray sat down to unlace his shoes.

* * *

"Is this you giving me the store, or you leaving the store?" Danny had his tablet under his arm and his coat still on. "Because those are different invoices."

"Giving," Ray said. "Outright. Papers to follow."

The key cutter sat idle at the end of the counter.

Normie had his hand flat on his chest. The other two leaned on their stools by the coffee pot.

"And there it is, folks," Normie said. "Gagnon and Son, sixty-three years in the family, and the old man hands it over at six-twenty on a Wednesday with no warning and no cake."

"Is the coffee included?" The one on the end looked at Danny. "In the transfer."

"Coffee's a quarter," Danny said. "Nobody's paid it since 1990."

Ray had grown up above this floor. His father had handed the store down those stairs in one afternoon, with the keys and the coffee pot, and Ray had kept it the way Lucien left it.

Linda's stool was still beside his at the counter, with its cracked green cushion. For four years Danny had put every bolt in the building into a tablet, and for four years Ray had come in behind him and counted the bins again, one at a time, and never once found a count off.

A box of hinge screws sat open on the counter by his elbow. He left it there.

Danny set the tablet down on the counter and kept his hand on it.

"Four years I've been waiting for you to say that," he said. "You picked six-twenty on a Wednesday, in front of the Bench." He looked at the tablet. "You coming in tomorrow?"

"Some."

"Call first. I'm moving things. The bolt bins go to the back wall, and the plow parts come up front. I've had it drawn up since 2023."

Ray looked down the first aisle. His father had built those bins out of pine the year the store opened, and the lids had a dip worn in each one where sixty years of hands had reached in.

"Your grandfather built those."

"I know who built them, Dad. They're moving twelve feet."

Ray opened his mouth and shut it again. He reached for the box of hinge screws, and Danny slid it out of his reach along the counter.

"Twelve feet," Ray said, and put on his coat. Danny waited until he had both arms in the sleeves.

"Dad. Whatever you're shopping for now, we don't carry it." He tapped the tablet. "I checked."

Ray buttoned the coat.

"Normie. Sit there." He nodded at the green stool. "Bench needs a man up front for the broadcast."

Normie looked at the stool, and then at Danny, and sat on it. He picked up the broadcast without a hitch.

"Normie in the front seat now, a terrific seat, folks, and the old man's out the door at six twenty-five with his coat buttoned wrong, destination unknown to this booth."

Ray pulled the door shut.

* * *

At 7:15 on a Saturday the doors were still locked, and there were three of them in the building.

The whiteboard leaned against the burnt-orange settee at lanes 1 and 2, four plans in red marker, A through D, for ice, storm, closed roads, and no power. D was the shortest column. Everybody in the room had been here on the seventh of January.

Across the top row of the strip board hung five strips in her capitals, one for each weekday of school vacation week. Each read 1200–1700 / VAC WK / ALL LANES / SOLD. In December Judy had asked what she had booked against that week, and she had said nothing yet. She had built it herself since then: kids' afternoons, family strings by the hour, and a block for the derby families. She had sold it on the derby's phone tree, and every family had paid ahead.

Behind the register the pace sheet carried every dollar of it and still said what it had said all week. Eighteen hundred dollars short on Judy's first line, with the Open still to run. Twelve hundred and fifty short on her second.

"Short on both," Ellen said.

"After the week we had," Denise said. She had the crossword folded on the counter and the pen uncapped.

The Open had sold its last line Thursday at 4:40, and the sandwich board by the shoe cubbies said OPEN, FULL in her capitals. Both Tuesday teams had phoned in Wednesday and gone back on the sheet at twelve-fifty, and neither captain would say why. The twenty-seventh had to do the rest, in one day, on both lines.

Hollis had a printed forecast flat on the counter and his notebook beside it.

"Coastal low in the long-range models, Friday night into Saturday," he said. "Two of three models have it."

"How much?" Ellen said.

"Don't know yet. I'll know more Thursday. I'll know at four a.m. Saturday." He put a finger on the printout. "Over ten inches, my contract has me on the back roads from three. Private drives around the lake."

"Then Gagnon and Son's a man short in the final," Denise said. "And Ray—"

"Ray'll find somebody," Hollis said. He looked at the board. "What's Plan E?"

"Plan E is we all go home," Ellen said.

Hollis wrote it down in the notebook with the golf pencil.

"Well, while we're on Ray." Denise put the pen down, which meant she needed both hands. "The Bench has it he gave Danny the store Wednesday, outright, and that's him clearing the decks for March second, which I grant you is the Bench—"

"Stand by," Ellen said.

"—and I was at Pine Ridge Tuesday, so I'll tell you. He bowled an eighty-four, then a hundred and seventeen, then a hundred and thirty-three, on a house ball with a chip out of it. Gil in that overcoat called it on a microphone to eleven people, and the Legion came up in their van and went home in it, and that's your two teams."

Ellen looked at the Tuesday column on the pace sheet.

He had won her two teams back forty minutes up the road and hadn't told her. Eighteen days ago he had stood at her left in front of eighty-one people. If she missed Judy's lines she would have to sell, and he still held the first refusal and a bank letter on the building those teams had come back to. At his truck she had asked to find out what she could carry. Now she would never know how much of the twelve-fifty had come back on its own.

Hollis had come around the counter to read the board column by column.

"Who's on the machines?"

At the foot of every column, A through D, she had written MX: in the same red marker. Nothing followed it in any of them.

"A hundred and fifty people in boots," Hollis said. "All ten lanes, all day. How many tables act up? Two? Three? And D puts people in the pit." He looked at her. "Who?"

Ellen looked at the four colons. She had planned for the ice, the roads, the power, and the fish. The certified boiler technician was on a card in her vest pocket, and so were the roof raker and the fuel-oil company. One man in the county was qualified to stand in that pit, and she had run every plan around him.

"I don't have an answer," she said.

Denise capped the pen.

"Call him, honey. Or I will, and I'll enjoy it." She pushed the counter phone across to Ellen by its cord.
