# Chapter Eight: Position Relief

Ray set the two rolls of packing tape on the kitchen table, between a stack of face-down photographs and a box marked THE PINES, UNIT 2B, KITCHEN in green ink.

"By the ledger," Arlene said. "Off it."

He moved them off it. The 1996 book lay open on the oilcloth, its green cloth gone gray at the corners. A strip of masking tape ran down the spine in block capitals: 1996 — LENT A. FORTIER 02/05 — DUE 03/01.

He knew those capitals. They headed the card by the pit door, over every time he had written in and out since December. She had lent Arlene the book and written a date on it.

Arlene turned the ledger toward him and laid the rim of her magnifier under a line halfway down September. *Key to R.G.* Below it, in the same ink, *no charge.*

"That's the only line I ever gave you in the body of the book," she said. "After that you're in the margins."

The scanner on the counter clicked and spoke, low. A plow truck off the road at the bend on Dock Road, driver out and walking.

"That's the Ledoux boy." Arlene didn't turn her head. "He takes that bend like it owes him money. His father took it the same way in 1988 and took the town's mailbox with him." She tapped the page. "Where was I."

"The margins."

"I know where the margins are, Raymond. I kept them."

Wintergreen came up through the floorboards from the pharmacy, and somewhere below a door chime went twice.

"You told me first crack," he said.

Arlene looked up.

"In this kitchen. At the counter with the drawer open. And at the Legion hall after Linda's funeral, with your hand on my arm, while Danny was still shaking hands at the door." His voice stayed low. "First crack. You said it that day."

"I said it," Arlene said. "I meant it that day."

The building on Kettle Road was the last room Linda had been in that nobody had rearranged. Since December he had been angry at an eighty-four-year-old woman in a cardigan, and it had done him exactly as much good as being angry at the weather. He had come up her stairs with two rolls of tape, and he had used the anger anyway.

Arlene took a tissue from her sleeve, looked at it, and put it back.

"Everybody on Main Street knew your bank was coming. I knew before you did. I sold to her anyway." She folded the magnifier shut on its chain. "You'd have kept it a museum. I wanted it to live."

The radiator clanked twice under the window.

"Does she know why you picked her?" Ray said.

"She hasn't asked."

The scanner reported a dog loose on the ice off the town beach. Arlene gave it no opinion. She pulled the tape rolls across the oilcloth, licked her thumb, and touched each one.

"Two," she said. "I asked for three."

Ray was already on the stairs, going down for the third.

* * *

The text had come at 2:31.

*Lane 4 table hanging on the respot. 3x since noon. Lane closed. Work order. Rate per our agreement.*

Ray came in the back door at 2:40. The lot was full of Massachusetts plates, and a minivan had followed him around the building hoping he was leaving. He wrote 02/13 / 1440 / LANE 04 / TABLE on the card by the pit door.

Nine lanes were running, and over the clatter a child was singing the same line of a song about a shark.

Ellen met him at the pit door with her reading glasses down on their cord.

"It hung at twelve-ten, one-forty, and two twenty-six," she said. "I closed it after the third."

"Respot cam." He had heard it from the catwalk. The table came down, caught half an inch above the deck, and dropped the rest, like a man missing the last stair in the dark.

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes. Thirty, if the follower's worn through."

"I have six parties on the waiting list."

"Then twenty."

In the pit he got the drop light on its hook and the cover off. The follower had a flat spot worn in it the size of a thumbnail. There was a spare on the third nail from the end, in his own tape. He had it out of the paper when a voice came down the lane at him.

"Hey."

A boy of about eight was lying flat on the approach of lane 4 with his chin on the foul line, looking down sixty feet of maple at the light under the table.

"Do you live back there?"

Ray considered it. "Saturdays."

The boy scrambled up and ran for the settees. "Dad. Dad. He said *Saturdays*." It carried over nine lanes and the shark song. A man in a Patriots cap looked down the lane at the pit and lifted one hand.

The follower went on in eleven minutes. Ray cycled the table four times, and it settled onto the deck clean each time. At 3:02 he wrote his time out on the card.

At the counter he laid a Gagnon & Son slip in front of her. She read it with her glasses on, took her checkbook from under the register, wrote the check, and tore it out along the perforation.

"Thank you, Mr. Gagnon."

"Ms. Daly."

Effective now, she had said at his truck window, and he had kept to it. The card, the slip, the check. Seventeen days ago she had held a fistful of his jacket on her own cellar stairs. *Rate per our agreement*: it was the one agreement they had left, and both of them were keeping it to the penny.

The Open clipboard sat by the register, and he read it upside down while she wrote. Nine lines left.

He put the check in his shirt pocket and went down the side of the snack bar toward lane 1. He stopped by the trophy case.

The photograph on the 187 card showed Linda mid-release, her right foot sliding and her left arm out. It was the only way he had ever seen that string.

Linda would have entered the Open the first day the clipboard went up. She'd have paid cash, written her name on line one in big loops, and then leaned on the counter and asked Ellen exactly where the entry fee went, to the dollar.

Locker 14 was beside the case. He lifted the latch. Her bag sat where she had set it, blue vinyl, the zipper pull a loop of his picture wire. He took it out by the handles and left the locker door standing open.

He walked back up past the snack bar and the counter. Denise sprayed a pair of size nines, toe, heel. Ellen had the phone at her ear and a pencil on the strip board, and she didn't look up.

"Lane four," she said. "Three-fifteen. Four bowlers."

* * *

The release lay on the desk with his name typed in it twice. LECLERC was lettered on the door glass in gold.

"I'll need a photo ID," the lawyer said.

Ray took out his wallet and slid the license from behind its window. The bank's commitment letter came out with it, folded in thirds and soft at the creases since December. The lawyer picked it up to move it, read the letterhead, and laid it beside the license. She didn't say anything about it.

He signed at the first flag. The radiator under the window banged once.

If she missed now, nobody had first crack. The building would go to whoever bid, and the developer in Concord was a real man with a real phone number. There would be no March second, and no somebody keeping the lights on. He signed at the second flag, and the radiator banged again.

The lawyer brought out the embosser. It was cast iron and older than she was, and she had to stand to get her weight on it. The hinge rocked under her hand.

"Hang on."

Ray took a dime from his coat pocket, set it in the slot of the hinge screw, and gave it a turn and a quarter. She inked the date stamp while he did it. When she bore down again, the jaws met square.

"There's a line here for the reason," she said. "It isn't required."

"No."

She stamped it, signed below the seal, and squared the pages on the blotter. Over her shoulder a 1950s map of the lake hung framed under glass, every camp on the north shore drawn in by hand.

"I'll record it this afternoon. After that it's public. Anybody at the registry can pull it."

Ray put the license back behind its window. The letter went into the inside pocket of his coat.

"Do you want a copy sent to Ellen Daly?"

"No."

The lawyer capped her pen. "When will you tell her?"

* * *

Ray laced his own shoes on a molded blue settee at the far end of Pine Ridge. Fourteen lanes were dark under scoring screens that still showed the league's last frames. The carpet was purple with yellow comets in it, and the place smelled of pizza and of the carpet glue Gil had been promising since 2008 would wear off.

He had called Gil on Sunday and asked. It had cost him more than the forty-minute drive.

Denise sat at the scorer's table with a Kettle Lanes paper sheet and her own pencil. Linda's bag sat on the carpet by her feet. Out past the front glass the Legion van was parked across two spaces, AMBROSE POST, AMERICAN LEGION on its side.

Gil came down the approach in his overcoat with the wireless mic in his gloved hand, and his voice came out of every speaker in the building.

"Good evening, Ambrose, and welcome to Pine Ridge Lanes. Sixteen lanes, fully refurbished, two of them lit for you tonight past closing at my own expense." Eleven people, counting him, were in the building. "House rules, Ray. Three strings, total pins, my lane, my house ball. You'll forgive the ball."

Ray took a house ball off the rack. It was gray and chipped at one edge.

"Mrs. Lavoie's sheet is official alongside my screen," Gil said. "Where the two disagree, we stop and count." He turned to the snack counter, where the two teams sat on stools in their jackets. "If Mr. Gagnon wins, Taylor's Marina bowls Tuesdays at Kettle Lanes at twelve-fifty, starting the twenty-third. If our friend from the Marina wins, Mr. Gagnon bowls Tuesdays here with the Marina through April."

The Legion captain raised a hand off his knee. "We came up in the one van. I'd say we go home in the one van, whichever way it goes Tuesdays. That's about how I'd put it."

Ray's first ball left his hand high. It came down a yard past where he meant it, took the front pins off the right side, and left the rest standing behind a scatter of wood. His second went through the gap. His third clipped one pin and rolled into the pit.

He hadn't bowled with anything riding on it since Linda. League strings didn't count. League was just Tuesdays and Thursdays. Her ball was four feet away in a bag he had carried in himself and couldn't use under Gil's rules, and he had brought it anyway.

In the sixth frame he lofted the first ball again and heard it land.

"Holy mackerel."

He heard himself say it. Denise wrote down the six and didn't look up.

He finished the string at 84. The 112 had 108.

Between strings the 112 went to Gil. "I want fifteen. Sixteen's pulling left."

"Fifteen and sixteen were resurfaced the same week in 2008, by the same man, who charged me the same price for each," Gil said. "You'll stay where you are."

Gil stopped at the scorer's table on his way back.

"Mrs. Lavoie. I watched you pick up a four-horse with your third ball in 2015. I still think about it."

"Second ball," Denise said. "There's nothing to think about in a third ball." She went back to her sheet.

"Second ball," Gil said. "You convert spares better than anybody who comes through my door." He walked on. Denise wrote something in the margin, erased it, and wrote it again.

In the second string Ray stood on the approach with the gray ball and looked at the lane.

*Low. Let the lane have it.*

He brought his hand down six inches and let the ball go along the boards. It ran true. He finished the string at 117. The 112 had 115.

The third string went frame for frame. Ray waited on the approach in the tenth while Denise ran the column twice with her pencil.

"Nine," she said.

His first ball chopped. It took four off the right side and left two pins lying flat on the deck. His second took three more. Three still stood at the left edge in a crooked line, and in front of them lay one fallen pin, crossways, its head pointed toward the far side of the deck.

*The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left.*

He put the third ball low along the left boards, at the butt end of the fallen pin. The ball caught it square and the pin swung like a gate, slid across the deck on its side, and cut the back two out from under themselves. The ball went on through and took the last one.

Gil read the totals off Denise's paper. "Three hundred thirty-four. Three hundred thirty-two." He switched off the mic. "Mr. Gagnon."

The 112 looked at the screen as if it might disagree with the paper. It didn't.

"Well," the Legion captain said, and zipped his jacket. "That's the van, then. I'd say it's probably best."

"You picked her," the 112 said to Ray. "Over thirty years of us."

Ray sat down to unlace his shoes.

The 112 had his coat on and was halfway to the door, keys out, the rest of the Marina behind him.

"Tuesday. Twelve-fifty," he said. "Don't expect me to like it."

* * *

"Is this you giving me the store, or you leaving the store?" Danny had his tablet under his arm and his coat still on. "Because those are different invoices."

"Giving," Ray said. "Outright. Papers to follow."

The key cutter sat idle at the end of the counter. Out front a plow radio crackled in a truck with its engine running, and somebody's dispatcher said Route 3 was greasy.

Normie had his hand flat on his chest. The other two leaned on their stools by the coffee pot.

"And there it is, folks," Normie said. "Gagnon and Son, sixty-three years in the family, and the old man hands it over at six-twenty on a Wednesday with no warning and no cake."

"Who do we report to now?" the one on the end said.

"We report to the coffee," Normie said.

"Is the coffee included?" The middle one looked at Danny. "In the transfer."

"Coffee's a quarter," Danny said. "It's been a quarter since 1990. Nobody's paid it since 1990."

"Then it's included."

Ray had grown up above this floor. The store had come down the stairs to him with everything in it, and he had kept it the way his father left it. Linda's stool was still beside his at the counter, with its cracked green cushion. Four years ago Danny had put every bolt in the building into a tablet, and Ray had been checking the tablet ever since.

"Now hold on." The one on the end set his cup down. "He gives up the store. So he's got his days free. So March second he's got nothing to do but run a bowling alley."

"He's clearing his decks," the middle one said.

"My brother-in-law's cousin bowls for the Marina. Says Ray went up to Pine Ridge Tuesday night and bowled for something. You don't bowl at Pine Ridge in February unless you're up to something."

"There you go. That's proof."

Ray took a box of hinge screws off the counter and put it back on its shelf where it belonged. He didn't say anything.

Normie turned on his stool and pointed past the register at the wall, at a nail with nothing on it.

"I'll tell you what I miss. You can't bet on a thing in here anymore. Sheet's gone up the hill. A man comes in for salt, and there's nothing to put a dollar on."

"Bet on the coffee," Danny said.

Ray put on his coat. Danny waited until his father had both arms in the sleeves.

"Dad. You're allowed to want something that isn't on a shelf."

Ray buttoned the coat.

"Normie. Sit there." He nodded at the green stool. "Bench needs a man up front for the broadcast."

Normie looked at the stool and then at Ray, and sat on it. He picked up the broadcast without a hitch.

"Normie in the front seat now, a terrific seat, folks, and the old man's heading out, and nobody knows where he's going at this hour, and he's got that look he gets when he's about to—"

* * *

Ellen had counted the room twice. Three people, counting herself, at 7:15 on a Saturday with the doors still locked.

The whiteboard leaned against the burnt-orange settee at lanes 1 and 2, ruled into columns in red marker.

A: ICE HOLDS. DERBY OUTDOORS. OPEN AS PLANNED.
B: STORM. DERBY INDOOR EVENTS HERE. FRIDAY FISH WEIGHED SAT, LANE 10.
C: ROADS CLOSED. OPEN WITH WHOEVER ARRIVES. HOLLIS KEEPS KETTLE RD PASSABLE.
D: POWER OUT. HAND-SET FROM THE PIT.

D was the shortest column. Everybody in the room had been here on the seventh of January.

Behind the register the pace sheet was taped at eye level. Ellen read it to them because it was easier than pointing.

"Twenty-four thousand three hundred to date. Projected at the end of the month, twenty-nine thousand. Judy's line is thirty. Cash projected on the twenty-eighth, twenty-three thousand seven hundred fifty. Judy's line is twenty-five." She took off her glasses. "Short on both."

"After the week we had," Denise said. She had the crossword folded on the counter and the pen uncapped.

It had been a good week. The Open had sold its last line Thursday at 4:40, and the sandwich board stood drying by the shoe cubbies with OPEN, FULL on it in her capitals. Massachusetts had filled every lane from Saturday through yesterday. Both Tuesday teams were on the sheet again at twelve-fifty. All of that put her at twenty-nine and twenty-three seven-fifty. The twenty-seventh had to do the rest, in one day, on both lines.

Hollis had a printed forecast flat on the counter and his notebook beside it.

"Coastal low in the long-range models, Friday night into Saturday," he said. "Two of the three models have it. One has it out to sea."

"How much?" Ellen said.

"Don't know yet. I'll know more Thursday. I'll know at four a.m. Saturday." He put a finger on the printout. "Over ten inches, my contract has me on the back roads from three. Private drives around the lake, the narrows, the camps."

"Then Gagnon and Son's a man short in the final," Denise said. "You're their second. That's three bowlers against four, and a blind score, and Ray—"

"Ray'll find somebody," Hollis said.

He took his cap off and looked at the board. He put it back on.

"What's Plan E?"

"Plan E is we all go home," Ellen said.

Hollis wrote it down in the notebook with the golf pencil.

"Well, while we're on Ray." Denise put the pen down, which meant she needed both hands. "The Bench has it he gave Danny the store Wednesday, outright, Normie told the pharmacy and the pharmacy told me, and that's him clearing the decks for March second, which I grant you is the Bench—"

"Stand by," Ellen said.

"—and I was at Pine Ridge Tuesday, so I'll tell you. He bowled an eighty-four. Then he didn't. Gil in that overcoat called it on a microphone to eleven people, and the Legion came up in their van and went home in it, and that's your two teams."

Ellen looked at the Tuesday column on the pace sheet.

He had won her two teams back forty minutes up the road and hadn't told her. Eighteen days ago he had stood at her left in front of eighty-one people. He still held the first refusal, and if she missed by fifty dollars he would own the building those teams had come back to. She could not make those facts sit on one line of a tape, and she had been trying since Thursday.

Hollis had come around the counter and was reading the board from closer up, column by column, his lips moving a little.

"Who's on the machines?"

At the foot of every column, A through D, she had written MX: in the same red marker. Nothing followed it in any of them.

"A hundred and fifty people in boots," Hollis said. "All ten lanes, all day. Two tables act up. Maybe three. And D puts people in the pit." He looked at her. "Who?"

Ellen looked at the four colons. She had sequenced the ice, the roads, the power, and the fish. The certified boiler technician was on a card in her vest pocket, and so were the roof raker and the fuel-oil company. There was one man in the county qualified to stand in that pit, and she had run every plan around him.

"I don't have an answer," she said.

Denise capped her crossword pen.

"You calling him, honey, or am I?"
