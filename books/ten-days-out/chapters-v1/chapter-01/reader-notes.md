# Reader Notes: Chapter 1, "Night Freight"

**Verdict:** A cold, confident, tense opening. I'd turn the page. But I bought a romance and haven't met the man yet, and the only promise of him is "the Marshals Service is sending someone."

---

## Where I was gripped

- **"The seal wasn't cold."** This is where the book hooked me. The reefer unit is off and the box isn't cold, and I understood why that matters without anyone explaining it ("A dealer who let his box die with lobsters in it lost money by the pound").
- **The sprung lid.** Lobsters, rockweed, then "Blue showing through." Then Beth says "Lid's sprung. You'll lose your count," presses it shut, cinches the strap and raps the trailer twice. That's the best character beat in the chapter. She covers for herself by doing her job exactly as usual. I believed her completely, and I was scared for her.
- **"She didn't look back."** Then she counts eleven rungs going up the ladder. The steady pace and the control told me she was terrified without saying so.
- **The driver's head "turned up toward the wheelhouse windows... The angle was enough."** Good menace.
- **Brake lights at the head of the ramp: "One. Two. Three. Four. Then he went."** I don't know what it means, which is why it works. It reads like a signal, or a threat.
- **The ending.** The *Margaret Ann* moves to the guest mooring "square below the house," a figure stands on her afterdeck turned toward shore, and Beth switches off the kitchen light. Strong.

## Where I got bored or skimmed

- **Paragraph one.** Binders, D-rings, deck plate, ratchet, then XtraTufs and the diesel at nine hundred. That's a lot of gear vocabulary before anything happens. I liked the authority, but I skimmed the second half of the paragraph. A romance buyer browsing the sample could bounce here.
- **The lighting panel paragraph** ("FWD FLOODS. AFT FLOODS. RAMP SODIUMS. RUNNING."). I skimmed it. It reads like texture for its own sake.
- **The Tess flashback.** Tess is vivid ("Christ on a bike," the braid tug), but the tank loan and deposit-bag explanation runs long in the middle of a tense crossing. I wanted to get back to the boat.
- **The weather radio and barometer.** I got that a storm is coming. The second barometer reading made me skim. The storm is clearly being set up for later, and I could feel the setup.

## Where I got confused

- **"Day 0 to Day 1 — Thursday to Friday."** This header looks like a planning note. "Day 0" read like a countdown I'm supposed to track, but nothing told me what it counts toward.
- **The timeline doesn't add up.** "It was nine" as they clear the breakwater, and "Twenty-two minutes to Pruett Harbor," but they arrive at 9:44 ("quarter to ten"). I stopped and did the math, which a reader shouldn't have to do.
- **Marr's and Tess.** I had to work out that Marr's fuel float is Tess's business. It's implied (the pump sign, the loan, "every gallon she could sell") but never stated. For a second I thought these were two different places.
- **Ned turning off his hearing aid.** Is he shutting her out, signaling that he doesn't want to know, or just tired? I liked the gesture, but I wasn't sure what it meant. The same goes for Ned never looking at the lit float. Is he complicit, or protecting her? I think the ambiguity is on purpose, but I couldn't tell whether I'd missed a clue.
- **"Not in front of anyone. Not yet. Not in front of—"** In front of whom? Ned? The cutoff felt coy instead of tense, because I couldn't guess the name.
- **Who Beth is.** I don't know her age, what she looks like beyond a braid, or whether she lives alone. I know she's worked eleven years with Ned and her father died. That's fine for now, but it's thin for a romance heroine.
- **"A sound she knew in her sleep and would never make by accident."** I read it twice. I think it means she'd know if someone else lifted the latch, but the phrasing points the other way.

## Whether the heroine's choices made sense

Mostly yes, and that's the chapter's strength.

- Covering the sprung lid: smart and in character.
- Not using the VHF because every kitchen scanner hears it: believable island logic.
- Skipping 911 because county dispatch goes out over the scanners: believable.
- Waiting until 1:10 so the stop happens far from the ferry: clever. But the driver saw her face, so a stop anywhere points back to the deckhand. She half-acknowledges this. I'm not sure the delay actually protects her.
- Not telling Ned: it made me suspect Ned, which I think is intended.

**Where I stopped believing:** the morning call. One overnight traffic stop, and by 6:10 a.m. a federal agent is calling and the *U.S. Marshals* are putting someone on the first boat to her island. That's very fast and very convenient. It's obviously how the hero arrives, and I could see the plot machinery. Also, "Until then, ma'am, don't go to work" is a strange thing for a trooper to say to a witness.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Every section ends on a button line:** "Then the ramp started coming up." / "He reached up and turned off his hearing aid with one finger." / "The car deck behind her was bare steel and chain." / "She wedged the chair..." and "still in her bib pants." / "Beth reached over and switched off the kitchen light." Each one is good alone. Five in a row becomes a pattern I noticed.
- **The counting tic:** eleven rungs, fourteen cartons, four brake lights, nine boats, twenty-two minutes. It's meant as character (she counts under stress), but it's applied so evenly that it starts to feel like a device.
- **"The guest mooring lay square below this window, so this window sat square above it."** This over-explains, and it's clunky. The previous paragraph already landed it.
- **"Her hands did it every departure without her."** / **"her father's clock went on with what it did."** Slightly precious, like literary filler.
- **"Not yet. Not in front of—"** Stacked fragments as a tension shortcut.
- Otherwise the voice is restrained and specific, and it doesn't over-explain her feelings. That's to its credit.

## Romance, for an open-door buyer

There's no love interest on the page and no hint of desire, attraction or loneliness. Beth is all competence and fear. The Marshal on the eight o'clock boat is the implied hero, and that promise kept me reading. If Chapter 2 doesn't deliver him quickly, and with some charge, a romance reader will start to feel misled by the cover.

## What I want to know next

- Who gets off the eight o'clock boat, and is he the hero?
- Who is the man in the hood with the limp? Is he an islander? Would Beth recognize him?
- Is the *Margaret Ann* part of this, and is that figure on the afterdeck watching her house?
- Is Tess selling diesel to smugglers after hours to cover her tank loan? Is Tess in it?
- What does Ned know? Why the hearing aid, and why did he let her skip the round?
- What did the four brake taps mean, and who were they for?
- How will the storm trap everyone on the island tonight?

## Would I turn the page?

Yes, without hesitation. The suspense hook is strong and the ending image is good.

## Best moment

> "Lid's sprung," Beth said. "You'll lose your count."
>
> She reached past the driver and pressed the lid down with the flat of her hand until it seated. She pulled the load strap over the stack and cinched it, counting cartons under her hand. Fourteen.
