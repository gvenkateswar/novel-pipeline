# Reader Notes — Chapter 8: "What Ned Told"

**Verdict:** The reveal is fair, and the braid detail made me feel it, but I'd picked Tess by Chapter 6, so it confirmed what I thought more than it hit me. Ned breaks my heart, Dane's account holds up, the reconciliation is quick, and the bait plan is clever about the boat but weak on why a marshal would agree to it.

---

## The reveal: fair, but not a surprise

It's fair. Every clue was on the page: Tess's initials on the bank-bag tag (Ch 1), the $1,800 tank loan, the float lit after closing with the *Margaret Ann* alongside, the ledger with blank lines under 3:52, the laugh that "came late," Tess fumbling the Golds with Arsenault standing over her, "emergency management director. Since town meeting" (Ch 2), and Dane's "local emergency management" (Ch 6). Beth dismissing that last one ("nobody in Portland phoned Tess") was the moment I decided it was Tess. When a book makes its heroine explain away a clue, it's usually pointing at it.

So when Beth works it out in the wheelhouse, I wasn't shocked. I was waiting for her to catch up. The suspect list was always small (Ned, Tess, Russ, Dane, a counter "nobody can name"). Dane was never a real suspect for me, because the book gave him a POV in Ch 2 and this is a romance. Russ got about one scene of suspicion. That leaves Ned or Tess, and Ned's lie came too early and too openly to be the twist.

**Did I feel anything?** Yes, in one spot: the braid. Tess has tugged Beth's braid in nearly every scene since page 3, and finding out she stopped on Tuesday was a small, quiet punch. That's the hit. The hot-chocolate memory, used again almost word for word from Ch 4, felt like the book reaching for my feelings when it didn't need to. The braid had already done the job.

There's also a gap Beth doesn't close: in Ch 5 she argued, "If Tess were selling me, she'd have kept it in her head. You don't write a thing in a book anybody can read." That was a good argument. The reveal never answers it. Did Tess write it down because writing things down is who she is, or was the log the cover? I wanted Beth to look back at her own reasoning and see where it failed.

## Ned's confession

I believed it completely, and it's the best character work in the chapter. "Not a damn dime." "Night freight's the subsidy. No subsidy, no winter boat." That fits everything the book set up: he lies about the boat's business, not about people. Then the man who "had never once in her life filled a silence" fills one, and what pours out is Tess: supper, the pump after dark, "she's scared all the time," sleeping in the back office by the booster, ticking Beth's line every morning. He's telling on the woman he loves without knowing he's doing it. That's very good.

The hearing aid payoff ("He left it on.") is earned, though that line is one of several section endings that land too neatly (see the AI section below).

**Did I feel for Tess?** Some, and only through other people. Tess isn't in this chapter, so the sympathy comes through Ned ("she's scared"). She comes across as cornered, not evil, which is the right note. But I haven't seen her afraid on the page with my own eyes, so I'm taking Ned's word for it.

## Dane's account: did it clear him?

Mostly, yes. The pieces fit: Quist asked, Pike ordered, "coaching ends your testimony," "no indication" from Sunday, and he left the fish house out of his own book. Beth's reasoning before he even arrives ("If it had been him, the fish house would have burned first") is the real proof, and it's a good one. It's also something she can check, which matters for a heroine who trusts binder handles over explanations.

What I didn't fully buy:
- **The struck-through D6 line being "Subject and I."** It's sweet, but convenient. Of course the one line she couldn't read turns out to be the romantic one.
- **"The last rule I bent for a woman, she died in a parking lot."** It's the right reason, but it comes out as a polished line, as if he'd practised it. Same with "Both were true. One went in the book." Nice line, but too finished for a man sitting across a steel table from the woman who walked out on him eleven hours ago.
- **The costs.** Off the detail, off the case, referred to internal affairs, disclosed to the defense. They're real, and I respect that the book doesn't let him off. But they're all stated, not felt. He lists them "the way it would go in the record," and that's on purpose, but nobody in the room is visibly hurt by any of it. The cost I felt most was the idea of a defense lawyer asking Beth about this under oath. That one has teeth.

## Reconciliation: earned or quick?

Quick. It's earned by logic, not by anything emotional. Beth gets to believing him alone in the wheelhouse before he even walks up the pier, so by the crew-mess scene the case is already settled and the scene is just a hearing. She never gets angry at him on the page. She read "assess" written about her after she'd slept with him, ran in a storm, cried into her sleeve on Ned's bench, and twelve hours later the worst thing she says is "You could have told me Saturday." I wanted one ugly exchange, one line where she makes him feel what *Subj.* felt like. The hand stopping an inch short and her leaving hers there is a lovely bit of restraint, but it does the job a fight should have done. Then she sleeps two hours and leads him into the bunk. From fleeing to sex in about fourteen hours felt fast.

## The third sex scene

**Heat: 3.5/5.** A bit cooler than the fish-house scene in Ch 6, with less detail and more mood.

**Different from the first two?** Yes, clearly. The first was hands-only, with the fire still burning and no condom. The second was slow, she lost track of the door, and Marisol came after. This one is in daylight, she sets the pace, and the scene runs on control: "That arm, Dane, stays on the rail." The upper berth forcing them close and slow, and him taking the cold steel at his back, are good physical details. "Tell me when it hurts" / "I'll tell you after" / "After. I said after." is the best callback in the chapter.

**Did it change anything?** Yes. It's the first time they choose each other knowing it's on the record. "I'll tell them I chose it. In daylight." That's the real turn, and it's a good one.

**But** the daylight idea gets hammered. "Daylight. All week she'd had him by lantern," "In daylight," "this, in daylight, with her eyes open," "as if daylight were something he didn't mean to waste." I got it the first time. By the fourth it felt like a theme being pointed at.

Also: she "stepped out of her jeans." She's been in bibs and canvas pants all book. Where did jeans come from on the ferry? Small, but it pulled me out of the scene.

## Beth as bait: brave and smart, or foolish?

Both. She's smart about where: steel, a boat she knows blind, thirty-four steps to the stern gate, ground she picks. That fits who she's been all book, and I trust her on the water because the book trained me to ("I'm going to stop arguing with you about water"). The Tess argument is strong: if Arsenault sails, Tess is the loose end, and waiting gets her killed.

Foolish, or at least hard to swallow, on the plan itself:
- Relief lands by noon tomorrow. The grand jury is Monday. She's the only witness. Putting her up as a target the night before help arrives is the one thing a protection detail exists to prevent. Dane folds after a single "He didn't answer." For a man whose whole wound is "I bent a rule for a woman and she died," he gives in way too easily. I needed him to fight much harder, or to have a reason to agree beyond being quiet.
- "You get it from her mouth." How? I don't see how a radio call on 9 gets Tess to confess. That part of the plan is fuzzy.
- So far this is the book's plan, not quite Beth's. She hasn't said what happens when someone actually walks those thirty-four steps.

Right now it reads as brave, with the book leaning on the "heroine walks into danger" setup more than the logic supports.

## Where I got confused

- **Moreau.** "All of that goes to Moreau, Deputy." Who's Moreau? I'm guessing the prosecutor, but this is the first time the name appears and the book just assumes I know.
- **Channel 9 in the wheelhouse.** She turns to 9, counts to eleven, doesn't key, and turns back. Is she listening for Tess? Testing whether Tess is on? Saying goodbye? I read it three times. Probably meant as memory plus decision, but it's unclear.
- **"the first name the back door came for"**: an awkward phrase. I had to untangle it.
- **The heel on the radio at 1 a.m.** Beth reports that "somebody was talking him along on a radio," and nobody in the room says the obvious next thing: that was Tess, from the store, on the booster or a handheld. Maybe that's being saved, but the silence felt odd in a scene that's otherwise putting everything on the table.
- **Pike's seven o'clock call arrives exactly as Ned blocks the brow.** It was set up ("I'll have it for you at seven"), so it's not a cheat, but the timing is very neat.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Every section ends on a button.** "He left it on." / "Ned stepped out onto the aluminum brow and stood in his way." / "He went still under her hand." / "...and began to report." / the Icom "its knob still on sixteen." By Ch 8 I can predict the shape: a short, flat, physical last line. Seven chapters in, it's a habit.
- **Explaining what a gesture means.** "Rage would have fed it to the stove. Squared, it said she had read it with a level head, and decided." / "To anyone who didn't know what he knew, the pages proved that a man had slept beside her and written her down." Dane's POV explains things the reader already understood from Ch 7's ending.
- **Everyone talks the same way.** Dane, Beth, Ned, Russ, Pike: all clipped fragments, no wasted words, lines that sound like they've been edited. "Arm down. Hand soft." "Knees. Ninety seconds. Then ocean." "Lane. Back door. Look first." Ned is meant to be the man of few words, but nearly everyone is. Only Tess and Mrs. Dyer ever ramble, and that's why Tess stood out as a person.
- **Repeated tics across the book:** tapping the barometer (it's in this chapter too), counting everything, "put it where she put things," "her palm flat on [surface]," "he looked at X's hands." Each was good once. Eight chapters of them in rotation starts to feel mechanical.
- **Lines built to be quoted:** "Both were true. One went in the book." "I'd rather you get asked than have it found." Good lines. Too many good lines per page.

## Where I got bored

Not badly anywhere. The thinnest stretch is the half-past-three planning scene, from "They have to get to me before Sunday's boat" to "Then go call Pike." It's all argument and logistics, laid out like a briefing, and the outcome is never in doubt because Dane barely resists. My eyes moved faster there.

## Cross-chapter observations (Ch 1–8)

- **The setting is the book's real strength.** Binders, barometer, the bar at half tide, the generator bleed, the check-in net, Tess's store. I believe this island. Ch 1 and Ch 5 (the bar crossing) are the best set pieces.
- **The mystery is the weakest part.** The suspect list was always tiny, and Tess was pointed at from Ch 1's bank bag onward. The "is Dane the leak?" question never had real weight for me, because his POV in Ch 2 and the romance ruled it out. The book spent Ch 6–7 building suspicion that I as a reader never shared, so the Ch 7 midnight escape felt like the heroine being wrong rather than me being fooled.
- **Ch 7 is the best chapter.** Reading the notebook, killing the alarm, "Deputy" under her breath, the crying into her sleeve on Ned's bench. Ch 8 couldn't top it, partly because it resolves Ch 7's tension so quickly.
- **The heat curve works.** Hands, then a slow first time, then daylight with her in control. Each scene changes something. That meets the promise of an open-door book. None of them would work pulled out on their own, which is a good thing.
- **Tess is the character I'll remember**, and she's been off the page since Tuesday. That's a risk for the last two chapters.

## What I want to know next

- What happens when Beth and Tess face each other. I need that scene on the page, in person, not over a radio or secondhand.
- Why Tess did it. Is it the loan alone? Is Arsenault holding something over her? Is she being forced? And does she warn Beth at the last second, or not?
- Who walks the thirty-four steps tonight: the heel, Arsenault, or both? And what's Russ for? He's been circling all book.
- Whether Dane goes along with the plan or breaks it to protect her, and whether his career survives.
- What Beth does after: the hundred-ton license in the drawer, the *Ruthie C*'s trim paint, Ned getting old. I want the ending to put her at a wheel.
- The series hook. Who's the next book about?

**Would I keep reading?** Yes, without question. Two chapters left, a trap set on a boat I know as well as she does, and a confrontation with Tess I've been owed since Ch 3. I'd finish tonight.

## Best moment

> Her hand went to the end of her braid. On Tuesday Tess's hands had been on the register, the ledger, the box held out across the counter. Not once on Beth's arm. Tess had touched her every day of her life.

It's the only moment in the reveal that showed me something I hadn't already worked out, and it does it with a gesture instead of an explanation. Runner-up: Ned filling the silence. "Morning net, she ticks your line. Every day since the lines went."
