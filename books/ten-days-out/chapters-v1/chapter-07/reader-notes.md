# Reader Notes — Chapter 7 ("His Handwriting"), with cross-chapter notes on 1–7

**Verdict:** The best-built chapter so far and the one I'd read straight through, but I trust Dane more than Beth does, and I'm sure of the villain while she isn't. That makes her midnight exit feel half smart and half dumb.

---

## Where I was gripped

- From the notebook hitting the floor to the end of the chapter, I didn't look up once.
- The upside-down second book, *ASSESS — per Pike (Quist req.). Daily. Do not disclose to subject.*, is a great reveal. "She doesn't know" from Chapter 3 flips in her hands: "It turned over now and landed the right way up. The she was her." That pays off a line I'd been carrying for four chapters. Really satisfying.
- *Subj.* "She had been Subj. all week." It's short and it hurts.
- Killing his watch alarm is cold. "Her thumb didn't slip." That one line told me more about how hurt she is than a paragraph of feelings would.
- Hiding in the churchyard: the man with the limp carries a light that dips on the short stride, and his radio breaks squelch every twenty seconds "like somebody reading down a list." That's the scariest picture in the book so far.
- The crew-mess breakdown and Ned setting the coffee "by her hand, not in it."

## Where I got bored or skimmed

- **The wound dressing that opens the chapter.** "She had pressed the wound twice that morning and again at noon, and waited till it quit about two, because strips won't stick to a seeping cut." Then strip two, three, four, five, the dead glue, the expiry date. I skimmed it. The electrical-tape joke is nice, but it takes a page and a half to reach.
- **The double recap.** First she reads the D1–D7 log entries, each with her reaction. Then "She put it in order" and walks Day 1 through Day 6 again. Same events, back to back, so I skimmed the second pass. I already know the plot, and so does she.
- "The kettle he'd put on, the cup set by her hand. His hand stopping on the small of her back." This is a list of tender moments, and I don't remember the kettle. It reads as a montage built after the fact.

## Where I got confused

- **"Key via Haskell."** Who is Haskell? I think it's Ned's surname, but I don't remember it ever appearing. For about three seconds I thought a new suspect had shown up.
- **"between the two chained trucks"** on the *North Reach*. The ferry hasn't run since Friday, and when it came in on Day 1 the car deck had one pickup and a pallet. What trucks?
- **The man with the limp on the road at night.** Is he hunting for *her*? How would he know she'd left? Or is he checking every house? The radio suggests someone is directing him, which is great, but I couldn't tell whether her leaving set it off or whether it was already happening.
- "Low water had been at five past ten. The tide had turned an hour since..." I couldn't place the time. She left the fish house around eleven, the scene is headed "small hours," and she reaches Ned at two. That makes it a three-hour walk, and if the flood started at ten past ten the spit should be well under by then. I stopped trusting the tide math, which this book has trained me to check.

## Did she snoop, or did it fall open?

Mostly fair, and the book is honest that it becomes a choice. It falls out of the pocket, she picks it up, and her own name is staring at her. Then: "She carried it to the workbench, turned the lantern up a quarter turn and held the page to the light. On purpose." I respected that line. She owns it.

The setup creaks a bit. The zipper happens to be down, the jacket happens to be on the floor, and the book falls open to *exactly* the page with her name and the money on it. One coincidence too many, but I let it go because of "On purpose."

## Would what she read make ME leave him?

No. That's the problem at the centre of the chapter.

What she reads is a marshal doing his job. A suspicious $9,000 deposit means his bosses tell him to vet the witness. His own notes say *No indication. No change. No change.* He's clearing her every day. The one entry that protects her most, *"secondary location, B's family property, not disclosed,"* is in his handwriting, and the fish house is the one hideout nobody has come to. Beth reads that entry and doesn't notice what it means. I did, immediately. Everything he wrote *with* a location got hit. The one place he kept out of the book is still safe. To me that points away from him and toward whoever he reports to, or whoever *they* talk to.

The interview in the shed stings, and I understand why it feels like betrayal after two nights in bed with him. If she had confronted him, fought, or wept, I'd buy the hurt completely. Leaving is a different call.

## Was leaving without confronting him smart or frustrating?

Both. Her reasoning paragraph is good: "If he wasn't, he'd explain, and she couldn't check an explanation. She could check a binder by its handle." That's very her. Leaving the book open so he won't think she's been taken and come out shooting is smart too.

But:
- She runs to **Ned**, who is on her own suspect list. Ned knew about Ashby, gave her the key, and lied about the reefer. She says so herself on the last page. So she's leaving the one man who took a bullet for her, that same morning, for a man she's *more* suspicious of. That's not smart. That's a romance-plot separation wearing a smart coat.
- She leaves a wounded man asleep, alarm disabled, with the bar *off* the inside of the door, while a shooter is on the island. Nobody remarks on it. That bothered me more than the leaving.
- It is a "miscommunication" separation, the trope a lot of romance readers groan at. It's better motivated than most, but it is one.

## Did I believe Dane might be the leak?

For maybe one paragraph, the one where she lists Day 1 through Day 6. The book has seeded him well: the porch call twenty minutes before the latch, "She doesn't know," picking Ashby, setting the landing time.

What made me trust him anyway:
- He got shot standing between her and the water in Chapter 6. Leaks don't usually volunteer to catch the round.
- Every time she reads his pulse it stays steady. Marisol's story checked out.
- The notebook itself clears her day after day, and he kept the fish house out of it.
- D6 is struck through hard. I read that as him writing something about *them*, or refusing to write something, not as anything sinister.
- He says "Beth" in his sleep. A bit much (see below), but it's aimed straight at making me trust him, and it works.

So no, I never really believed it. The tension of the chapter is "will she figure it out," not "is he guilty."

## Was the walk in the dark tense?

Yes. Best suspense set piece since the latch in Chapter 2. On her knees behind the minister's cordwood, mouth in her sleeve so her breath won't steam, the bell rope knocking, the radio bursts. Then the shore and the barnacles, and up the iron ladder to the sodium lights. My only complaint is the time and tide confusion above, which pulled me out for a second after the scene.

## Does Ned read as safe?

Mostly safe, and probably compromised. Letting her in with no questions, the key off the ring, the coffee by her hand, switching his hearing aid back on to talk to her: that's a man who loves her. But the reefer lie, the shed alibi he volunteered in Chapter 6 ("Nobody had asked him about the shed"), and now sitting in the dark at the only way aboard all keep him grey. "Guarding it or waiting at it" is a good last-page doubt. My read is that he takes cash to look the other way at night freight and would die before he hurt her. If he turns out to be the leak, I'll feel cheated.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Repeated narrator tics across the book:** "She put it where she put things" / "She filed it" / "She set it down beside the other thing." It's a nice idea the first time. By the fourth or fifth it's a stamp.
- **Character tics on rotation:** Russ takes his cap off, looks into it, and puts it back on (three times). Tess says "Christ on a bike" and "Hell's bells." Ned switches his hearing aid off with one finger (four or five times). They read like flags stuck on characters so I can tell them apart.
- **Everyone counts.** Beth counting is her character, and I like it. But Dane counts the slip in Chapter 2 in the same rhythm, and the narration counts steps, seconds, rungs, nuts and primer strokes. Sometimes it's texture and sometimes it's a habit of the prose.
- **Every chapter ends on a crafted button:** "She didn't step out." / "She was keeping an exit again." / "and read his own handwriting." All good lines, but they come at the same cadence every time, and I can feel the chapter-end machine.
- **"Her father had believed the bank."** I liked it, but it's an aphorism set on its own line to land, the same move this book makes a lot.
- **"Beth," he said. … He'd said her name asleep.** Convenient and sentimental. It's there to make me ache, and it's the one beat in the chapter I didn't believe.
- The short declarative rhythm ("She didn't touch him." "Her thumb didn't slip." "None of it was hers.") is effective but relentless. Seven chapters of almost no sentence longer than a breath.

## Who I suspect now, and how sure

**Tess. About 85%.** The book has been pointing at her since Chapter 2, and Chapter 7 hands Beth the clue and has her read right past it: "$9,000 cash deposited subject's acct, D1 a.m., w/ paycheck, **via store bank bag**." Tess took Beth's paycheck envelope for the bag. Tess initials the bag. On top of that:
- Ch 2: Tess is "emergency management director." Her laugh "came late." She logged Beth as home.
- Ch 3: the only cell signal on the island is Tess's booster, and she takes a call in the back office. She fumbles the register with Arsenault standing over it.
- Ch 6: the ledger stops at 3:52, but the float was lit at nine with the dragger alongside. She squares the books, smooths the cover twice, and glances at Dane. Then Dane's office "coordinated with local emergency management for the landing," and Beth decides that means some mainland county office. The island's emergency management director is Tess.
- The tank loan, $1,800 a month.

The radio voice directing the man with the limp, reading down a list every twenty seconds, sounds a lot like net control.

The risk is that I've been ahead of Beth since Chapter 3, and for a heroine built on noticing everything, her missing "bank bag" and "emergency management" is hard to accept. It reads as the author keeping her in the dark. If the big reveal is Tess, it had better land as grief, not surprise, because it won't surprise me. If it's NOT Tess after all this, I'll feel cheated.

Others: Russ at about 5% (Arsenault's in-law, asks where she sleeps, missing fingertip that went nowhere). Ned at about 5% for the leak, much higher for "took money to not look." Dane at about 3%.

## What I want to know next

- What Dane does when he wakes to a cold stove and the open book. Does he come after her alone, wounded, with no idea where she went?
- Who is on the other end of the radio telling the man with the limp where to go?
- When Beth figures out the bank bag, and what she does to Tess.
- What the struck-out D6 line said.
- Whether Ned is guarding that gangway or holding it open.
- Pelletier's grand jury is Monday, so how does she get off the island now that the Coast Guard window is gone?

Strong pull. I want Chapter 8 now.

## Would I keep reading?

Yes, without hesitation. The suspense is the book's strength, and Beth is the character I'll remember: the binders, the barometer, the counting, the hands. My worry going into the last stretch is that I'm well ahead of her on the villain, and her best-reasoned decision in the book is also the one I most want to argue with.

## Best moment

> She couldn't get the float coat's zipper down. Her fingers wouldn't close on the tab. She sat on the bench, put her forearms on the table and her forehead on her forearms, and the sound that came out of her went into the wet sleeve, and kept going there.

The only time in seven chapters she breaks, and it's written exactly as small as she would let it be. Followed by "He set her coffee down by her hand, not in it."

## Cross-chapter notes (1–7)

- **Voice:** Specific, physical, and confident. The ferry details, binders, barometer and tides make me believe Beth has done this work for eleven years. It's the reason I bought the book, and it holds.
- **Heat:** Ch 5 (hands, in the fish house after the bar crossing) and Ch 6 (the cot, the condoms from Tess) both change the relationship, as they should. Ch 5's "very disappointing kit" banter is the funniest moment in the book. Ch 6's "she didn't know where the door was" is the best piece of heat writing because it's about her, not the anatomy. Neither works on its own as erotica. Ch 4's stop at the open shed door is good tension.
- **Romance/suspense balance:** Good. The romance never stalls the threat, and the threat keeps intruding on the sex (the gun on the workbench, the tanker horn).
- **Biggest structural risk:** the villain is telegraphed. Tess's tells are piling up, and Beth explains each one away in a way that clashes with how observant she is everywhere else.
- **Repetition:** Narrator tics, character tics and button endings are the main "manufactured" feel. Over seven chapters they add up.
- **Minor continuity snags:** Haskell, the trucks on the ferry, tide timing in Ch 7, and Russ's missing fingertip (lingered on and never used, so far).
