# Chapter 7 — His Handwriting

**Day 7 to Day 8 — Thursday night to Friday**

*Day 7, afternoon*

The strip's paper backing came away yellow and brittle, and the old glue under it smelled like a closed drawer.

Beth laid it across the middle of the graze first. With her thumb she pushed the edges of the cut together, then pressed the strip down over them. The skin held. She had pressed the wound twice that morning and again at noon, and waited till it quit about two, because strips won't stick to a cut that's still weeping.

Dane sat on the edge of the cot with his left elbow braced on his knee. His right hand was clamped round his left wrist, holding his own arm still for her. His watch was on the end of the workbench by the cot. She'd had him take it off before she started. The arm would swell.

Rain came at the shingles in bands. The window on the water side had gone the color of slate by half past three, and the lantern was lit on the workbench beside her father's red Pelican case.

She'd boiled water on the drum and let it cool in a coffee can. She'd cleaned out to a hand's width all round and dried the edges with gauze. Now the second strip. The third. The fourth came off its paper and wouldn't take, its glue gone to dust. So did the fifth. She turned the box over to the date. Four years past. She took the next one out anyway, and it held.

His breath was on the inside of her wrist each time she leaned in. He watched her hands. Not her face.

He leaned toward her to see. Her free palm went flat on his chest and stopped him. Under it his heart went steady, slow, nothing in it she could use. She left her hand there and laid the next strip.

"Electrical tape," he said, when she reached past the gauze for the roll. "Your father had opinions."

"It sticks wet."

"Better tape than the Service issues."

She put the gauze pad over the strips and taped it round, not tight.

"These hold it closed. They don't hold it if you work it. Nothing over your shoulder. No hard grip with that hand."

"Arm down. Hand soft."

"Through Saturday at least."

He nodded. "Pike says the system's in by dark. Nothing moves on the water till it blows through. Ferry's still Sunday."

She didn't ask what else Pike had said.

"Wake me at eleven," he said. "Night call."

"The watch can wake you."

He picked the watch up off the workbench, set it with his thumb, put it back. Then he lay down on the cot with his bad arm across his stomach. His soft-shell lay on the plank floor where he'd dropped it after the last call from the ledge, a dark heap by the stove.

He was asleep inside five minutes. His mouth went a little slack. He'd been up since half past four, and he'd bled into his sleeve on the run home.

She swept the strip papers off the workbench into her palm and fed them to the stove. Then she sat on the low sitting bench across the room, where she had the door, and she was still sitting there when the window went black.

---

*Day 7, night*

At twenty to eleven the wind had found every gap in the shingles. Rain drove against the landward door and the door held. The stove was low.

Beth got up to feed it and stepped on his jacket.

It was soaked through. She picked it up to hang it on the nail by the stove, where it had hung last night to dry. It came up heavier on one side. She got the collar over the nail, and the jacket swung open on the inside pocket, its zipper still down from the last call on the ledge, and something slid out and hit the planks.

The notebook. Yellow cover, face down, splayed.

She bent and picked it up to put it back. It had fallen open. At the top of the left-hand page, in a hand that wasn't hers or her father's, was her name: *CALLOWAY, E.* Under it, a dollar sign.

She didn't put it back.

She carried it to the workbench and turned the lantern up a quarter turn, and held the page to the light on purpose, because it had her name on it.

*D2 Sat 1000 — Quist. $9,000 cash deposited subject's acct, D1 a.m., w/ paycheck, via store bank bag. Hundreds. Teller flagged.*

She read it twice.

Nine thousand dollars. She had never seen nine thousand dollars in one place. Her last statement had said four hundred and twelve dollars and some cents, and she had known what every one of them was for. Somebody had put nine thousand dollars in her account to make her look bought.

Her eye went past *bank bag, D1, paycheck* to the top of the line. Saturday. He had known since Saturday.

Sunday in the Ashby generator shed, diesel on her hands, him at the filter asking about money. *Anything come in lately?* She'd told him about her father's boat. About the bank. She'd told him because she'd thought he wanted to know.

On the cot he breathed slow and even. Six feet away. His holster was on his hip under the blanket. His watch lay on the end of the workbench by his head, set for eleven.

She turned back to the first page.

---

*Day 7, before eleven*

The paper was slick under her thumb, waxy, nothing like the soft pulp of her father's tide tables. Above the bench his paint cans stood in a row. BOTTOM. TRIM. HULL GRAY. Square capitals, every letter its own box. The hand in the notebook was small and slanted and quick, and it leaned forward as if it had somewhere to be.

*D1 — subject's res., Harbor Rd. 2340 back door tried. Unk. subject fled on foot.*

Her kitchen. The latch lifting, sticking, lifting. Down to the minute.

*D2 — moved: Ashby place, north pt. Key via Haskell.*

*D3 0640 — Lookout Hill w/ subject, woods path. Subj. ID'd sternman by gait. Photos to Quist.*

Her walk. Her path through the spruce, the one only islanders used. She'd picked it.

*D4 2200 — Ashby compromised (fire). Moved: secondary location, B's family property, not disclosed.*

*D6 — Extraction 0600 D7 town landing, CG MLB, conf. Quist.*

The bait shed wall. The coin of gray light a foot behind her head.

*D7 0604 — abort. Shots fired from water. Graze L arm. Subj. unhurt.*

Subj. That was her. She had been Subj. all week.

She turned the book over to see if there was more. There was. Pages written from the back cover, upside down, a second book inside the first.

*ASSESS — per Pike (Quist req.). Daily. Do not disclose to subject.*

*D2 — no contact outside detail.*

*D3 — financial questions. No indication.*

*D4 — no change.*

*D5 — no change.*

Saturday, under the eave, his voice through the rain. *No. She doesn't know.* A gap. *Daily. Understood.* She had carried that around for five days with no place to set it down. It turned over now and landed the right way up. The she was her.

Financial questions. The shed had been an interview.

*Fuck.*

Under D5 was one more line, D6, struck through so hard the pen had scored the paper. She tipped it to the lantern. Ink pressed into ink. Nothing she could read. Last night he had opened his mouth on the blanket and closed it and kissed her. She'd taken that for tenderness.

She put it in order.

Day 1, he'd stood on her porch with the sat phone twenty minutes before the latch.

Day 2, *She doesn't know.* He picked the Ashby place.

Day 3, *I'll ask her.* And he had.

Day 4, it burned.

Day 6, he set the landing time. Someone was waiting with a gun.

The last two were in this book, in that hand, and the rest he had said aloud, on the phone, to the same people. She didn't have to decide which it was, him or the people he gave it to. The book went out either way. The men came after it.

The kettle he'd put on, the cup set by her hand. His hand stopping on the small of her back. The money questions, asked so gently she'd answered them. She couldn't sort it.

Her father had believed the bank.

His watch said 10:58. She picked it up off the workbench by his head. She knew this watch. Last night she'd turned its face down. She found the side button and held it, and the alarm mark went off the dial. Her hands did it steadily.

She set the watch back where it had been. Then she opened the notebook to the second page, the money, laid it on the workbench and squared its spine to the bench edge.

---

*Day 7, eleven*

She did not wake him.

He was armed and trained, and he read people for a living. If he was the leak, telling him she knew was the worst thing she could do. If he wasn't, he'd explain. And she couldn't check an explanation. She could check a binder by its handle. She couldn't check a man's answer. A fight she couldn't win would tell him one thing. That she knew.

Now, then. The storm covered sound and kept people indoors. He was asleep and the alarm was dead. By daylight he'd read it in her face before she said a word.

The ferry. Steel. Lit. A fixed VHF and a wheelhouse door that locked. Ned, who'd known her since before she could walk a deck.

Not the store. Too many people in and out. Anything that came for her there would come at Tess. She thought of Tess going up the hill at one with Mrs. Dyer's soup, in any weather.

Her go-bag was by the door. She shouldered it. She took the Icom in its freezer bag out of the side pocket, put her thumb on the knob through the plastic and felt the click stop at off, then zipped it inside her float coat, against her ribs. The sat phone stayed on the bench. The gun stayed on his hip. His kit stayed where it was. None of it was hers.

The book stayed open. If she just went, he'd think she'd been taken. He'd come out into the dark after her with a gun, and someone would get shot on the cove road. The open page would tell him she'd gone on her own, and why.

She turned the lantern down to a bead. She didn't feed the stove.

On the cot his right hand lay open on the blanket. His jaw was dark with a week's stubble. At the edge of the gauze her strips showed white.

She didn't touch him.

At the door the cold came off the planks against her face. She put her hand on the bar.

"Beth," he said.

She stopped. Didn't turn. His breathing hitched, and then it evened out, long and slow. Asleep. He'd said her name asleep.

"Deputy," she said, under her breath.

He didn't hear it.

She lifted the bar out of its brackets and lifted the door on its hinges, the way her father had taught her so it wouldn't scrape, and stepped out into the rain. She shut it behind her on the open book.

---

*Day 8, small hours*

No moon. No light. The grown-over path to the cove road ran under alders she couldn't see, and she walked it by the feel of the ruts under her boots and by thirty years of walking it. Rain came sideways off the northeast and found the gap at her collar.

The cove road. Then the Harbor Road, gravel and puddles, the Union Church a pale shape ahead on the right.

A light came along the road toward her from the store end.

It swung as it came. Long stride, short stride. The beam dipped on the short one.

She knew that walk. Lookout Hill, the skiff, the man favoring his left leg.

She was off the road and over the churchyard wall before she'd finished knowing it. Down behind the lean-to woodshed, on her knees in the bark and the old snow, the minister's cord wood stacked between her and the road. Split oak. Wet bark. Against the clapboards the bell rope knocked every time the wind leaned on the building.

The light came on slow. It went into a dooryard across the road and came out again.

A radio broke squelch on him. A burst, a low voice she couldn't make out, the hiss tail. The light moved again. It stopped. Another burst. It moved.

She counted the gap between bursts. Twenty seconds. Twenty. Twenty-two. Steady, like somebody reading down a list.

Her own Icom was off, in its bag, pressed against her ribs. She thought of its squelch tail in this quiet and kept her hand flat over it.

Her knees had gone numb in the bark. Her breath came short and she put her mouth into her sleeve so it wouldn't steam toward the road.

The light passed the church. Close enough that she could see the rain in the beam, a slant of bright needles. It went on toward the store end. The bell rope knocked, and knocked, and the light was gone.

She stayed until her hands shook from the cold and not the other thing.

She didn't go back to the road. She went down the bank behind the church on her heels and her hands, through dead bracken to the shore.

Low water had been at five past ten. The tide had turned an hour since and was making, the young flood, and the ledges under the Harbor Road were still out. The gravel spit below the church lay dry along its spine and wet at its edges, where the flood came over the gravel a sea at a time. Well over an hour before it covered. Rockweed slid under her boots. Barnacles took the skin off one knuckle. Nobody would walk this shore in this weather, and nobody watched it.

She worked along it toward the stone pier with the store's lit windows above her and off to one side. She didn't climb to them.

Under the pier the ledge was black and streaming. The iron ladder went up into the dark above her, rung by rung. Over the pier's lip the *North Reach* showed her deck lights, sodium orange through the rain.

---

*Day 8, two o'clock*

She came up the ladder and waited in the shadow of the wrecked slip's tower, out of the floods. She didn't call him. Anyone with a set on in the harbor would have heard.

Ned did his pump rounds every two hours in a storm. At two a flashlight came aft along the car deck.

She stepped into the gangway light. The gate was chained. His hearing aid would be off; it always was at night. She slapped the steel of the gate twice with her open palm.

The light swung onto her and held.

He looked at her a long time from behind it. On Saturday he'd told her to stay off his boat until this was done. His eyes went past her to the empty pier. Then he unhooked the chain.

In the crew mess he switched on the heater. It ticked and gave off the smell of burnt dust. He went out without a word.

She couldn't get the float coat's zipper down. Her fingers wouldn't close on the tab. She sat on the bench. She put her forearms on the table and her forehead on her forearms, and the sound that came out of her went into the wet sleeve, and kept going there.

The generator thrummed up through the deck plates into the bench.

When she lifted her head Ned was in the doorway with two mugs. He thumbed his hearing aid on. He set her coffee down by her hand, not in it.

"Where's your marshal?"

"Asleep."

He waited. She gave him nothing else.

"I want the wheelhouse," she said.

His eyes went to the square of the Icom under her float coat. Then he worked a key off his ring, slow, his thick fingers on the split ring, and held it out.

They went up the inside companionway. The wheelhouse smelled of burnt coffee and wet wool and warm electronics. The fixed set hissed on 16. Ned took the rag off the console and wiped the forward windows with it, and left them worse.

"Anybody know you came here?"

"You."

He looked at her for a while. Rain ran down the glass he'd smeared. Then he put one finger to his ear and turned the hearing aid off, and went down the ladder.

She turned the key in the lock.

She took the Icom out of its bag, switched it on and set it on the console beside the fixed set, both on 16. Inside companionway. Outside ladder. The door she'd just locked.

Through the aft windows, past the lighting panel on the bulkhead, she watched his flashlight go along the car deck between the two chained trucks. It went to the gangway and stopped there. Then it went out.

He was sitting in the dark at the only way aboard from the land. She couldn't tell if he was guarding it or waiting at it. Ned had known the Ashby place. He'd given her the key. And on Saturday he'd told Dane that dealer always ran his unit, when she'd had her palm on that box and it had been dead.

Out on the cove the lantern would be down to its bead, and then out. The stove would be cold iron. The book would be squared on the workbench, open at the page with her name and the money on it. He'd wake all at once, the way he had on the first night when the latch lifted, already sitting up, his hand already at his hip. And in the cold he'd find the matches, and light the lantern, and read his own handwriting.
