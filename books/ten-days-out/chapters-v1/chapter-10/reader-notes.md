# Reader Notes: Chapter 10, "First Boat" (final chapter)

**Verdict:** A quiet, controlled landing that pays off nearly everything I was promised. The Tess scene and the parting are the best things in it. The coda reads too much like a checklist of callbacks, and the book-2 hook breaks the book's own rule about who can hear what.

**Rating:** 4 / 5

---

## Did the ending pay off?

**The romance.** Mostly yes. The palm-on-the-chest reading at the pier ("True.") pays off something the book has done since chapter 1, and this time she does it in front of Quist, both deputies and Ned. That is the declaration that counts, more than the spoken one. The future is concrete: Friday boats, the transfer to Maine, Christmas in Lowell. It's a believable HFN. As someone who bought an open-door book, I noticed there's no last intimate scene. The final chapter is chaste, and a reunion scene in the coda wouldn't have been out of place. That isn't fatal, but I felt it.

**Tess.** The scene works. "Oh, fuck," then "Oh, Bethie," to the floor, then giving up the hiding place under the board before anyone asks: that is the Tess I know, a woman who adds everything twice even when she's confessing. "They said they'd only scare you" / "You warned me" is exactly enough. "Make Ned eat something that isn't coffee" got me. Beth tipping out the cold coffee and pocketing the saltines where Tess can see them took me a second to read, but I got there. What's missing is why Tess was in it. Was it only the tank loan, or was she trapped? Two years of night fuelings is a long time to be "scared." I'm left guessing at the most important betrayal in the book. Beth also never feels anything about Tess on the page after this scene. "Nothing came after it" is honest restraint, but nineteen years of braid-tugging gets a single beat.

**Ned.** The hearing aid going off "to news" is the best single gesture in the chapter. But no charge, license untouched, after a year of knowingly hauling weights-wrong trucks with the reefer dark? That's lenient, and Quist hands it to him too easily ("I don't see a charge"). His real cost is losing the Thursday run. Chapter 8 told me "No subsidy, no winter boat," and nothing in chapter 10 says what happens to the winter boat now. The coda quietly implies the ferry is fine. That thread just stops.

**The testimony.** The grand jury scene earns its place and does not drag. If anything it's too thin. Moreau's question and "Yes. I chose it. He told me before it happened that he'd report it, and he did" pay off "let them ask me" from chapter 8, and the juror who writes something down and turns the page is perfect. **But the book never tells me whether Pelletier was indicted.** The whole clock of the book was Monday's indictment, and I closed the book not knowing if it happened.

## Did Tess's scene work?
Yes. See above. Quist's speech about the pills ("One is enough to stop a man breathing. A man who thinks he's taking something for his back.") is the one place where the scene starts explaining the stakes to me instead of to Tess.

## Did Ned's fate feel right?
Emotionally yes, legally soft. See above.

## Was the first boat a satisfying last show of Beth's competence?
Mostly. "Take her off," and Ned standing aside from the wheel, is the payoff for the master's license in the drawer, and I felt it. Kenny "had his color back" made me smile. The spring-line undocking is precise, but to a non-boater it's small, a quiet show rather than a triumph. The best part is her working the binders at Pruett in the dark and crossing the welded patch "without looking down." Quist's "I did. Now I don't." and "She'd have trusted a sorry less" is a good, dry exchange.

## Did Dane's costs feel real?
Half. The relief order read flat on the gangway, the notebook going into the evidence bag, and his hand finding the pocket lining flat: those cost something on the page. In the coda they shrink to a summary paragraph: suspension, letter, transfer approved, "about where he'd figured." The transfer coming through "because it was short of deputies" is convenient, even though chapter 3 set it up. By the end he seems to have lost nothing that matters.

## Was the parting right?
Yes. "Come on the Friday boat." / "Which Friday." / "Any of them." That's the best dialogue in the chapter. She doesn't ask for a promise. Her own kitchen window lighting up as the grid comes back is a good image, if a little neat.

## Did the coda land? Was the declaration earned?
The declaration is earned by the book, but the moment itself confused me: "Hand me—" She stopped. Hand me what? I read it three times. I think it's a deckhand reflex breaking off, but it reads like a typo. The next two lines then say the same thing twice ("Her hand stayed where it was." / "Her hand didn't move on his.").

The coda hits every motif the book ever planted: XtraTufs, the horse joke, binders and bare hands, the dark fuel float, the notebook ("there was no page"), the hand on the ladder. Each one is fine. All of them in two pages feels assembled. "For ten days he had watched those hands and written down what they did. Now he watched them and there was no page" over-explains a point the image already made.

Switching to Dane's POV for the coda works. Seeing Beth from outside is the right final angle.

## Did the book-2 hook feel organic or tacked on?
Russ as the next lead is organic. He's the most likable person in the book, he was set up through Jolene's cousin, and "What's that cost me? Don't tell me. The winter." is pure Russ. The delivery is tacked on:
- **Quist shouts a federal money-laundering case across still water at a public ferry slip.** "Her voice carried up flat across the still water." This whole book is about how everyone on Cutter hears everything: scanners on kitchen counters, the check-in log, channel 9. Ending on an agent announcing "Three harbors. One account" to the float broke the book's own logic for me.
- "You're off it, so you can know this much" is backwards. Being off the case is a reason he shouldn't know.
- Why does a federal case agent hand an envelope to a part-time island constable? I'd need a line on that.
- Quist turns chatty and profane ("cleaner fucking ledger") after a whole book of flatness, and it sounds like a trailer.

## Did the last image work?
Yes. "She kept his hand until the ladder took it" keeps her job first and him second, which is who she is. The dark fuel float "the way it should be after six," just before it, is a better quiet goodbye to Tess than anything said aloud.

## Confusing or convenient
- "The cottage on the Harbor Road." Her house has been a Cape all book. Is "cottage" a different place? It pulled me out, twice (it's also where Dane fetched the subpoena).
- "The ring on its chain tucked inside her collar." What ring? Quist has never had one. It reads like a planted detail for some other book.
- "Beth listened for a cove." I got it (he leaves the fish house out of the handover), but only just.
- Pelletier's indictment is never stated.
- The ferry subsidy is unresolved.
- Ned not being charged is easy.

## Where it sounded like AI
- The callback density in the coda, as above.
- One-word verdict paragraphs ("True.") are a pattern now, and here it's on the nose.
- "He nodded as if she'd given him tomorrow's wind" is one of several "as if" similes in the book built the same way.
- Quist's pill speech is exposition.
- Otherwise the voice holds. The prose stays clipped and physical, and the characters still sound different from one another (Ned's fragments, Russ's dry cracks, Tess's "hon").

## Would I buy book 2?
Probably yes, if it's Russ's book. I'd buy it on the strength of this book's world and voice, not because the hook grabbed me. The hook is a manila envelope and a joke. It doesn't give me a question I need answered.

## Best moment

> Then he lifted one finger to his left ear and turned the hearing aid off. Beth had watched him do that to a hundred people. She had never seen him do it to news.

Runner-up: "Come on the Friday boat." / "Which Friday." / "Any of them."
