# Reader Notes — Chapter 5, "Half Tide" (plus cross-chapter, halfway verdict)

**One-line verdict:** This is the best chapter so far. The fire and the wade across the bar are the first real action set-piece, and they deliver. The sex scene is short, funny, and does actual work in the plot. But the suspect conversation afterward goes over things I already know and leaves out two clues the book itself planted.

---

## Where I was gripped

- **The generator dying.** "It choked, stuttered, ran a second at the wrong speed, and stopped." Then the smell, then orange at the edge of the sheet, then the windshield at the end of the drive with "No lights. No engine." That is a clean ramp from noise to smell to light to threat. I read it fast.
- **Beth reading the fire as bait.** "That's what the fire's for. We run for the road, and he's at the end of it." This is the payoff for the morning scene on the bar, and it makes her look smart, not lucky. It's the moment the book pays off its premise: she knows the island and he doesn't.
- **The crossing.** Her fist in his belt, the counting, the sea surging up to mid-thigh, his feet going out from under him, and the line "That was what killed you here. Not the cold. The gasp." I was tense the whole way across, and I didn't know until "a hundred and six" that they'd make it.
- **Dane turning his back on the windshield.** "He looked at the windshield once more, the way a man looks at the one thing in the night he knows how to handle. Then he turned his back on it and came." That's the romance beat and the action beat in one move.

## Where I got bored or skimmed

- **The suspect inventory after midnight.** From "Who was at the counter?" to the paint can, it's a recap: the Friday call, the log, the Saturday store, the six at the stove, Arsenault, Russ, Ned. I already had all of it, because the book showed me every piece in Chapters 2–4. The rhythm turns into a list ("Friday it's three." "Three. And a counter nobody can name." "From Saturday it's anybody...") and I skimmed to find the one new fact. There isn't one, apart from Beth deciding to stop hiding.
- **"Store at opening. In daylight, in the open, with you. Being seen is normal out here. Hiding gets noticed."** Beth said nearly the same words in Chapter 3 ("Hiding gets noticed out here. Being seen is normal."). It's not a callback. It's a repeat, and it makes the plan for tomorrow sound like one we've already had.
- The morning tide lesson ran a little long ("Knees. Ninety seconds. Then ocean." / "Then ocean."). I didn't skip it, because I could tell it was a setup. But I could tell it was a setup, which takes some of the surprise out of the payoff.

## Where I got confused

- **"Whitcomb."** Dane says "Whitcomb walked this Saturday," and later "Whitcomb asks where you sleep." I had to stop and work out that Whitcomb is Russ. As far as I can find, his last name has never appeared on the page. Then Beth writes "*Russ*" on the paint can. One man, two names, in the same scene.
- **Which shore path is which?** At Beth's house the intruder ran "toward the alders and the shore path." Russ walked "alders to the ledge, ledge to the cove road." At Ashby's, Dane stands "at the top of the ledge where the shore path came out of the alders," and Dane says Whitcomb walked *this*. Is it one path that runs from Beth's house past Ashby's and over the bar? If so, the man who tried her latch knew a route that leads straight to the safe house, and that's huge, but nobody says so. If they're two paths, then "Whitcomb walked this Saturday" is wrong. I can't tell, and it matters.
- **The tide timing.** She gives low water as "ten past seven" while standing on the bar in the morning. The escape is at ten at night, "short of half flood." I worked out that there must be an evening low around 7:30 and that 10 p.m. is about two and a half hours into the flood, so knees fits. It does add up, but only because I did the math. A reader who doesn't will wonder how a morning low tide applies at 10 p.m.
- **Fish house geography.** From the Ashby ledge she points "toward the head of the cove" at the fish house, and it's visible. So why do they need the bar to get there? I assume the bar is the only dry-ish link between the point and the cove head, but I'm assuming.
- **The vehicle.** There's a car or truck at the end of the drive. On an island with no ferry for days and a handful of trucks, that's a big clue: whoever it was either has an island vehicle or borrowed one. Nobody in the chapter mentions it, not Dane, not Beth, and not the suspect list. Beth even says "He doesn't know this island," about a man who apparently drove to the one safe house nobody was supposed to know about.
- **The generator noise is gone from the logic.** In Chapter 4 Russ told them he heard the Ashby Generac, and that "anybody up the north road with ears" would have too. In Chapter 5 Beth says "Ashby's smaller. Ned and you." It isn't smaller. The book told me the generator announced them.

## Did the escape make sense? Were her choices smart?

Mostly yes, and this is the chapter's strongest feature. She walked the bar at dawn, packed a go-bag, left the seaward latch on the hook so it opens one-handed, read the fire as a flush, picked the route an outsider wouldn't take, put herself on the downstream side, and held his belt. It's all competent and all set up earlier. Dane deferring to her on water also fits ("I'm going to stop arguing with you about water," Chapter 2).

What strained it for me:
- **Dane waits two-plus hours to call Pike** after an arson attack on a protected witness, and has sex first. I believe the sex. I don't believe a deputy marshal sitting on "my safe house was just burned" until past midnight. If the delay is deliberate (he's starting to distrust his own channel, which "Not on this phone... Not where" hints at), the page doesn't let me feel him deciding that.
- **Nobody tells the island they're alive.** Walt's fire crew is at a burning shed next to a house the generator told everyone was occupied. Tess counts people. Will the island think Beth burned? Beth has the Icom in her bag and doesn't think about it at all. That seems odd for a woman who thinks about the net constantly.
- XtraTufs filling up in thigh-deep water would weigh a ton. It isn't mentioned. That's a small thing, but this book has trained me to expect that kind of detail.

## The sex scene

- **Did it land?** Mostly. The best of it is the humor and the restraint: "I've got a trauma kit." / "Does it—" / "It's a very disappointing kit." That's the line that made me like them together. Choosing "Hands, then" because they have no condom is in character for two careful people, and I respected it.
- **Heat for the shelf: 3 out of 5.** It's explicit in vocabulary (clit, cock, nipple, both of them coming on the page) but brief, about a page, and hands only. For a first on-page scene at the halfway mark that's okay. But a reader who picked this off the open-door suspense shelf will want more length and a full scene later. His orgasm gets one sentence. Hers gets more interior. It reads like the scene was rationed.
- **Earned?** Yes. Four chapters of almost-touches (the shed in Ch 3, the kiss and "I can't keep you alive with my hands on you" in Ch 4, the strap in the mudroom earlier in this chapter), and then the bar crossing as the proof of trust. "Door's shut behind you" pays off his Chapter 4 line directly. It's neat, almost too neat, but it lands.
- **Did it change anything?** Yes, and this is the smart part. Right after, she takes her hand off his chest and tells him about the Tess call she's been holding back since Chapter 2. The intimacy buys a confession, not just closeness. And she still doesn't fully trust him afterward ("Her palm on his chest an hour ago had told her nothing she could check"). That's honest, and it keeps the tension alive.
- **What read as mechanical or borrowed:**
  - The setup itself, wet and cold so strip him and press a palm to his chest, is the stock "warm the hypothermic hero" trope. I've read it many times.
  - "His heart ran fast under it. Cold did that. So did other things." Cliché, and coy in a scene that isn't otherwise coy.
  - "His fingers found her clit, and she was wet" is template phrasing.
  - "She made a sound she'd never heard from herself" is the most generic line in the chapter.
  - "What went through her wasn't his name. It was that he'd turned his back on that windshield, that he'd walked into the water holding nothing but her word..." The book explains her orgasm's emotional meaning to me in a full sentence, which this author mostly refuses to do anywhere else. It felt written for the reader, not felt by her.
  - The cutaways that work: the shed still burning ("Someone had known exactly where to put a match"), the tanker horn, him glancing at the gun and then staying. These keep the scene inside the suspense, so it doesn't play as standalone erotica. Good.
- "When this is over, I report this. I want you to hear that before. Not after." / "I know what you are. I'm choosing anyway. Your call." This is the stiffest dialogue in the chapter. "I know what you are" sounds like a vampire novel.

## Is the suspect list fair play or cheating?

**Fair so far, with two gaps.** Every name on the paint can was planted on the page before this chapter. The Tess phone call (Ch 2), the log on the counter with "*Calloway — home (phone)*" (Ch 3), Russ's "where you sleeping tonight" (Ch 3), and the heel (Ch 3–4) were all shown, not invented now. The reveal that Beth withheld the call is fair, because Chapter 2 literally shows her eyes going over the phone when she says "You."

The gaps are the vehicle and the generator noise, which I flagged above. Both are clues the book handed me that the characters then ignore while building the list. That doesn't feel like misdirection. It feels like the characters forgot.

What's missing from the list is more interesting than what's on it: **Tess** (talked off it by Beth), **Dane** (Beth still wonders silently), and **Pike/Portland** (never raised, though Dane is now clearly hiding locations from his own phone).

## Do the two leads sound different?

Partly. Dane has a dry, self-mocking register (the boots, the horse, the kit, "This is mostly potato"), and Beth has a clipped, practical, water-first register. That's a real difference. But in plain exchanges they collapse into the same short declarative rhythm: "Put him in." "He's in." / "Three. And a counter nobody can name." They both talk in two- to five-word lines, and in a few places I couldn't tell who was speaking without the tags. "Walk me through it" is Dane's catchphrase now (twice in this chapter, once in Ch 4). That's fine, but I noticed it.

## Where it sounded like AI (cross-chapter tics that were thick by Ch 5)

- **"Longer than X needed."** Ch 3: "a second longer than the mug needed." Ch 4: "longer than diesel needs," "slower than it needed." Ch 5: "longer than she needed to," "a while longer than the answer needed." It's a single construction used over and over.
- **"The way she'd [boat task]."** "The way she'd log a fuel transfer," "the way she'd pick a mooring," "the way she'd run a checklist," "the way she'd decide which line to throw first," and in this chapter "the way she'd set down a line she meant to come back for." It was characterful in Chapter 1. By Chapter 5 it's a reflex.
- **"She put it where she put things"** and close variants appear in Chs 1, 2 and 3.
- **"His right side clear"** comes up in every chapter, sometimes twice. I get that it's his gun side. Five chapters of it is a lot.
- **Counting.** Steps, rungs, seconds, boats, primer strokes. It's her trait, but in this chapter it's the bar steps, the crossing count, the drive steps again (340, now mentioned in three chapters), and the generator. It's starting to feel like texture applied by formula.
- **Neat scene endings.** Almost every section ends on a resonant image: "She didn't step out," "His pen was still moving," "Then I wasn't the only one who knew," "Dane leaned on the bench beside her and read it upside down." Each one is good on its own. Five chapters of them in a row start to read like a pattern.
- "Neither of them pretended it was the strap they'd come for." This narrates the subtext out loud.

## Who I suspect now, and why

**Tess.** It's not close, and that worries me, because it may be too obvious. My case:
- She's $1,800 a month underwater on the tank loan.
- Her fuel float was lit after hours for the *Margaret Ann* the night of the drop (Ch 1).
- Her laugh "came late" when Beth said "Don't ask me" (Ch 2).
- Her fingers went "slow on the drawer with a man standing over it," and she rang up Arsenault's cigarettes wrong.
- Her phone buzzed and she took it in the back office (Ch 3).
- She owns the island's only cell signal.
- She's emergency management director and net control, so she knows where everyone is.
- She handles the deposit bag that carries everybody's pay. Dane asking Beth "Anything come in lately? Insurance. A settlement" makes me think someone has put money in Beth's account to frame her, and Tess initials every deposit.
- Beth herself says "Ned tells Tess. Everybody knows that." So Tess knew about Ashby, and Beth argues her off the list in the same breath. When the heroine defends someone that hard, the reader adds them back.

**Second suspicion: the Portland channel (Pike, or whoever's listening).** Dane's call goes out at 11:20 and the latch lifts at 11:40, twenty minutes later, on the first night. "She doesn't know." "Daily. Understood." And now "Not on this phone... Not where." Either Dane suspects a leak on his side, or he's the one being watched.

**Ned** is complicit in something (lying about the reefer, the hearing aid off, the rower's raised hand and his turned back), but I read him as a man looking away for money, not the one who lit the match.

**The heel** is Arsenault's sternman. I'm sure of that, and I think the book wants me to be.

## What I want to know next

- Who was in the vehicle at the end of the drive, and whose vehicle is it?
- What is "She doesn't know"? What is Dane writing every night, and what does he know about Beth's money?
- Who is the "she" who asked for ten minutes, and is that why he won't let Beth out of his sight?
- Does the island think Beth died in that fire? What happens at the store tomorrow when she walks in alive?
- Is Tess selling her? If so, I want the book to make it hurt. The hot chocolate memory in Ch 4 is clearly set up to make it hurt.
- Does Beth ever get to use the hundred-ton license? I'd bet money she takes the wheel of something before the end.

## Would I keep reading?

Yes, without hesitation. This chapter turned "atmospheric and careful" into "I want to see what happens tomorrow morning."

## Best moment

> It came in surging out of the dark and lifted the water to mid-thigh, cold enough to take the air out of her. The gravel slid out from under him. His weight swung away downstream, and her fist on the belt held, and her arm took all of him and didn't open. If he went under, the cold would make him breathe in before he could tell it not to. That was what killed you here. Not the cold. The gasp.

The runner-up, for making me like them as a couple: "It's a very disappointing kit."

## Halfway verdict on the book

**What's working:**
- The setting is real. I'd believe the author has worked a car deck.
- Beth is a heroine whose competence drives the plot: the binder check, the tide, the latch, the bar.
- The slow burn is genuinely slow and genuinely builds.
- The mystery is seeded fairly, and the clues are shown, not announced.
- The latch that sticks (Ch 1–2) is the best single device in the book: a lock that's also a fingerprint.

**What's not:**
- The prose tics listed above have hardened into a house style that's starting to read as machine-regular.
- The leads' dialogue rhythms converge.
- Chapter 4's middle (the money conversation) and this chapter's suspect recap are where the book stalls.
- For the open-door shelf, the heat at 50% is a 3 and short. Readers who bought it for spice will be patient, but only if the second half pays off more fully.

**The character I'll remember:** Beth, for the binder rap and "Lid's sprung. You'll lose your count." That's still the best line in the book. Tess is second, for the braid tug, which is going to hurt later.

**Where I'd most likely have stopped so far:** the early-middle of Chapter 3 (the overheard phone call and the Russ conversation in the road), where the book was all looking and waiting. This chapter is the reason I'd now finish.
