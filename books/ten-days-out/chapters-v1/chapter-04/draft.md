# Chapter 4 — Glassing the Harbor

**Day 3 — Sunday**

Twenty-two.

Beth went along the moorings again, left to right, the binoculars braced on her fists and her elbows in the lichen. Spray came up over the lip of the granite in fine cold handfuls and beaded on the lenses. She wiped them on her cap and went again.

Twenty-two boats where there should be twenty-four. Then she had the other two: the *Gray Lady* and Hartley's *Second Try*, both hauled the week before the storm. Twenty-two was right.

"All home," she said.

The wet had come through her bibs at the knees and hips. Behind them the woods path dropped back into spruce. Anybody looking for her would watch the north road, because it was the only road.

Reyes lay a body-length above her where the granite humped, so he could see the path mouth over his shoulder. His right side was clear of the rock. He shifted, and something in his boot squelched.

"These were sold to me as waterproof," he said.

"They are. Up to the top."

"That's a narrow definition of waterproof."

The harbor lay under her like a chart. The store dock, with the *Margaret Ann*'s red hull along Tess's float and her deck lights still burning. The co-op pier. The *North Reach* starboard-to the stone pier, the transfer bridge beside her twisted down into the water like a dropped ladder. And on the Harbor Road, small and gray, her own Cape with no smoke at the chimney.

At ten to seven a skiff came out from behind the dragger. Aluminum, riding low. One man, hood up, rowing. No outboard. On a Sunday with the wind gone north, an outboard would carry to every kitchen on the harbor.

She kept the glasses on him. Short, choppy strokes. He came across the inner harbor and passed under the *North Reach*'s stern, where Ned stood on the after deck in his green jacket with a pump hose over his shoulder. The rower lifted one hand off the oar.

Ned turned his back. He went on working the hose.

Refusal. Or an answer, from a man who answered most things with his back. She couldn't tell, so she didn't say it.

The skiff nosed in at the fish pier ladder. The man made fast, climbed, and started up the pier.

Head down. Left leg favored. The right heel coming down hard every step, as if the planks owed him something.

Her free hand went flat on the granite.

"That's him."

Reyes didn't move. "Why."

"The walk. Thursday night, off the ramp, he walked like that. And the print on my back step yesterday. Right heel deep, both pairs." She kept the glasses up. "I saw it in the morning. I wanted to watch him walk before I said."

"Left leg. Right heel. You waited to be sure."

That was all he said. He didn't ask why she'd sat on it a day. She heard him not ask, and she knew he'd put it somewhere.

"Hold them still."

He slid down the rock beside her. She braced the glasses on her knuckles and he pressed his phone lens to the right eyepiece. His cheek was an inch from hers. The phone's edge sat cold against her knuckles, and his breath was the warmest thing on the hill. She didn't move the glasses. She didn't move.

The shutter clicked four times.

The man didn't go to the store. He walked the Harbor Road, past the church, past the Pinkhams' woodpile, and turned up her driveway.

He went round the back of her house.

She counted. Thirty. Sixty. Eighty. At ninety-one he came round the front and stopped in her dooryard with his face to the kitchen window, the one that looked down on the whole harbor. He stood there while she breathed twice.

Then he walked back toward the pier.

She kept the glasses on him the whole way, down the ladder, into the skiff. When he was aboard she passed them up to Reyes without looking away from the water.

"Third piling from the ladder. He's bailing."

Reyes put the glasses up.

---

Under the spruce the duff was wet and gave like a mattress. Below, the Union Church sat white on the Harbor Road with nobody walking to it. Its bell rope knocked against the clapboards in the wind. Nobody rang it.

Reyes walked back out onto the bare granite with the Iridium, because it needed sky. Beth stayed in the trees with the Icom against her ear on 68, the volume down to a thread.

"Cutter net, Cutter net, good morning, this is Marr's, net control. It's Sunday. Reverend says church is cancelled and pray at home. I say pray near your stove. Checking in by list. Dyer, go ahead."

Mrs. Dyer went ahead. The cat had been under the bed since Thursday. The chimney was drawing, mostly. It drew better in a west wind, and it had drawn beautifully the winter of the big ice, when Harold was still alive to clean it. Tess let her run all the way down on her own.

"Chowder's coming up at noon, Ida, and I'll bring Thursday's paper, so you'll know how it all came out." A breath, and then to all of them. "Anybody short on generator gas, come down. Pay me in April, or pay me in lobsters, I'm not proud. Everybody I can't reach I'm counting home and dry unless somebody tells me different."

She didn't say Calloway. She didn't need to.

The day Beth's mother left, Tess had driven her down to the boat in the store truck and bought her a hot chocolate in Pruett Harbor, and let her hold it till it went cold. Neither of them had mentioned it since.

The wind dropped. Reyes's voice came in off the granite.

"Sternman. Left leg. She picked him out at six hundred yards."

Wind. Then: "Yes."

A long gap. "I'll ask her."

Procedure. She let it go by.

He came back into the trees with his collar dark from spray. "Quist ran the boat. Gloucester registry, Arsenault the owner, two crew listed. HSI takes her when the sea goes down. Until then, nothing."

"He's as stuck as we are."

"He is." He zipped the phone inside his jacket. "I could walk down there and find him by lunch. Then you'd be up here alone, and there's one of me and no way off this rock. If I go after him, I've left you. He isn't my job. You are." He looked down at the red hull. "What I can do is make sure he never sees you."

He nodded at the radio. "Who's that?"

"Tess."

"She's good at that."

"She's good at everybody."

"Cutter net secured," Tess said in her ear. "Stay warm, stay off the water. I mean it, Haskell."

Beth switched the handheld off and clipped it inside her float coat, and they started down.

---

She heard the truck before the trees opened. A Ford with a lifter ticking, idling rough in the cold. Russ's.

Reyes put a hand out. She shook her head.

"Duck into the trees and he sees the tracks after and wonders. Walk past and he waves."

The path came out on the old camp road, two ruts and a grass hump. Russ's pickup sat in the ruts with its exhaust hanging low and blue in the spruce. Through the trees a camp porch showed, storm shutters up. Russ leaned on the tailgate as if standing were optional. A pipe wrench and a bucket in the bed.

"Calloway." He looked at Reyes and then at the bucket. "Checking camps. Somebody's got to make sure the summer people's pipes freeze in an orderly way." He tipped his head at the hill. "You come down off the top? Nice morning for it. What can you see from up there today? *Margaret Ann* still tied up at Tess's?" Nobody had answered him. "Course she is. Nobody's going anywhere."

Reyes stepped left so his right side came clear of her. He didn't look at Russ's face. He looked at Russ's hands, lying easy on the tailgate, the left ring finger short a tip.

"Heard a Generac running out on the point last night," Russ said. "Ned doesn't usually start the Ashby one till the power's been out a week."

He took his cap off, looked into it, and put it back on.

"Who else would notice?" Reyes said.

Russ thought about it. "Anybody up the north road with ears. That's four houses. Two of them are deaf."

He pushed off the tailgate. "You need anything, Calloway, I'm on 68. Or the town office. Mostly I'm in this truck." He got in and went bumping off toward the next camp with the tailgate rattling.

They walked out along the ruts. Reyes kept his eyes on the spruce on the uphill side.

"A man who tells you he knows where you sleep is doing one of two things. He's warning you, or he's watching what your face does when he says it. I watched him the whole time, and I couldn't tell you which one that was."

"Russ has known where everybody on this island sleeps since he was twelve."

"He knows where you are."

"He knows where the generator is. Out here that's the same thing."

---

It surged twice after lunch and quit. Beth heard it from the kitchen and knew the sound: air in the line. Reyes had topped the day tank from the jugs that morning, and he'd let it run nearly dry first, and the pickup had sucked a bubble.

She had her gloves off before she got to the shed.

The door was propped open on the driveway for light, and the wind came in through it and through the spruce behind. Concrete, diesel, the green housing standing open. Cold enough that her breath sat in front of her.

"Your hands," she said. "Not mine. You don't learn it watching."

He took his place at the filter. He set himself so the open door was in front of him and the generator was between him and the wall. She'd known he would.

"Bleed screw. Top of the filter. Crack it a turn."

He found it and cracked it.

"Primer lever. On the side of the pump. Work it."

He worked it. She counted. Eight. Twelve. Foam came up around the screw, then spit, then foam again. Nineteen. Twenty-four. At thirty the diesel came up clean and steady with no bubbles in it.

"Close it."

He closed it. Diesel had gotten into the cracks of his knuckles. She took the wrench and went down to the injector lines and cracked the first nut half a turn. Fuel ran over her own fingers, cold enough to ache.

"Walk me through what the ferry pays," he said.

She did the second nut. She decided.

"Winter hours. Three days and the freight run. Summer's better." The third nut. "Bank took my father's boat eight years ago. The *Ruthie C*. On paper. He was out hauling the day they did it. The sale didn't cover the note, so I paid the rest. Four years." The fourth. "House is mine. No mortgage. I pay taxes on his fish house on the cove, which nobody's opened in four years."

"What's in it?"

"His stove. His paint." She wiped the wrench. "I've got a hundred-ton master's license in a drawer."

"You've never used it."

"Asking Ned for the wheel is asking him to be old."

He took that and didn't say anything to it. Then he came at it again, from the side. "Anything come in lately? Insurance. A settlement. Anything."

She laughed. It came out of her short and real, and it fogged in the cold. "Who'd send me money?"

He looked at her a while. She took it for a man who wanted to know her, and she liked that he'd asked twice.

"You ask a lot about money," she said, "for a man who hasn't said where he's from."

He looked at the door, and then back at her.

"Lowell." He wiped the primer lever with the rag, though it didn't need it. "My father came up from Ponce and drove a city bus for thirty years. He knows every pothole in Lowell personally, and if you get in his car he'll introduce you. My mother dispatched for the Lowell police. Her radio sat on the kitchen table at supper. I knew how to do this work before I knew it was work."

"Does he know what you do now?"

"He thinks a marshal is a cowboy. Every Christmas he asks me if they've given me a horse yet." He turned the rag over. "I tell him I've put in for one."

She laughed again, softer.

"My Spanish is fine with him. On the phone I sound like a tourist." He folded the rag. "Somebody has to stand between the person who saw it and the people who'd rather she hadn't. My mother did it with a radio. I do it in other people's sheds."

The wind came round the door and stirred the diesel smell.

"You ever lose one?"

He wiped his hands on the rag. Then again, longer than diesel needs.

"Once."

She waited.

"She asked for ten minutes. I gave them to her."

He didn't say anything else, and she didn't ask. She put the wrench in his hand.

"Crank it."

He held the switch over. The starter ground. Fuel spat clean at the nuts, and she ran them down one after another, and the diesel coughed, caught and came up into a steady hammering run that filled the shed to the rafters. The bulb overhead came on.

She put her palm on the housing and felt it settle. Even. No miss.

She turned round. He was nearer than she'd thought.

She took the rag out of his hand. There was diesel across his right knuckles, and under it the old knot of scar, pale and raised. She wiped it off him slowly. Her thumb stayed on the scar.

"What was this?"

"A door." He looked at her thumb. "It won."

She lifted her face, and he was already coming down to it. It wasn't careful. His mouth was cold and then it wasn't, and his stubble scraped her chin, and she tasted coffee and cold air and smelled diesel on both of them. His hand came up the back of her neck under the braid, spread wide at the base of her skull, and held her there as if he meant to stay. She got a fistful of his soft-shell at the hip and pulled him the last inch in. She wanted him on the concrete with the engine going, and she'd have told him so.

His hand stopped.

The fingers on her neck went still. Then he lifted his mouth off hers and looked past her, over her head, at the open door. The driveway. The spruce. He'd had his back to it the whole time.

He stepped back. He still had one end of the rag, and she had the other, and he didn't let go of it.

"I can't keep you alive with my hands on you." He said it slower than he'd said anything all day. "That's not a no. It's a not yet I can't afford."

Her face burned in the cold. She turned back to the generator and put two fingers on the bleed screw she'd watched him close. Tight. She checked it anyway.

"Okay," she said.

He let go of the rag. He was at the door with his hand on the frame when she said it.

"Dane."

He stopped. He stood there in the doorway without moving at all, and then he looked back at her as if she'd handed him something he'd have to carry with both arms.

Then he picked up the heavier jug and walked ahead of her down the path to the house, watching the drive both ways.

The furnace ticked on at ten past eleven. She lay on the couch in the room off the kitchen and counted it through: on, eleven minutes, off. Mothballs and cold wallpaper.

At eleven-twenty the porch door went. Through the gap where the sheet on the front window didn't meet the frame she saw him on the porch with the Iridium up, the stub antenna to the sky. Short. A minute, no more. Then he put the phone away and took out the notebook and wrote in it standing, facing the road.

She put her boots on without lacing them, pulled the blanket off the couch around her shoulders, and went out.

He closed the notebook. He didn't hurry it. He put it in the inside pocket of his jacket, left side, and ran the zipper up over it. She marked where it lived.

"Pike?"

"Pike."

"What does she want every night?"

"To know you're alive."

"What do you write?"

"Where you were, and when." He looked down the drive, not at her. "If something happens to me, somebody has to be able to find you."

The wind had eased. Three hundred and forty steps of gravel ran pale from the porch into the dark toward the north road.

"Lace those or go in," he said. "The boards are iced from the step to the rail."

She looked down the drive. A man had walked round the back of her empty house this morning. On Cutter an empty house was something somebody asked about by supper.

She went in and shut the door.

Through the gap in the sheet she watched him take the notebook back out of his pocket and open it. He wrote one more line. His pen was still moving.
