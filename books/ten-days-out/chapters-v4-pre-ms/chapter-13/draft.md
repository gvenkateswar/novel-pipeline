# Chapter Thirteen: Her Father's Kitchen

Dane Reyes lay in the snow under Beth's kitchen window at first light and watched the kid wash her dishes.

The kid stood at her sink, his left arm bound to his body in one of her dish towels, washing a mug one-handed and setting it upside down to drain. His pistol lay on the drainboard. His face was gray inside the black hood, and he stood on one leg.

The kid had lit the stove half an hour before full dark last night. Nobody hiding lit a stove before dark. He had bled for a day and a night and the cold was winning, so today he was weaker than he'd been all week. That was why today.

This was the last thing Ned's phone had sent her. Ned had rung Cole when they tied up at Pruett, so the truck was clean and her word was the whole case, and every bed she slept in got a visit from the boy now washing her mug. Dane wanted to put him through the glass.

In the dark the house was the kid's. At first light he stood blind in a lit kitchen. Dane's board was still across the back door, and a dish towel was stuffed in the broken pane by the front-door latch. At five Dane had walked round the house. The tracks came up from the cove, one foot dragging, and none went out.

Beth had given him the rest at four, as business. Anyone in that kitchen who had to run went out the window over the sink. She had gone out of it at sixteen, and the kid had watched her go out of it the first night. The board just inside the front door talked when you stepped on it.

At dusk Dane had radioed Russ in off the road; a man in a truck below that house was a man the kid could see. At four Russ said he'd had the kid on the back of Cole's boat two summers.

"He'll come out for me."

Dane told him twice: knock from the side, behind the chimney stone, and keep out of the doorway. Russ said it back. Too easily.

At four he had also asked Tess to sit with Ned. "I'll sit with Haskell," Tess said through her door. "Talking's extra." He took a handful of long black cable ties off her hardware shelf.

He had asked Beth to wait in Russ's truck at the foot of the lane, where the rise hid it from every window.

"I'll go," she said. "Not for you." Then: "If anybody bleeds, I'm coming up." He said yes.

He wanted her. He lay in the snow and wanted her, and thought about her voice at four, and how she'd taken the first-aid box from him without touching his fingers, and the cold in his chest and his bad knee couldn't reach any of it. He'd told his boss, on speaker, that she'd take a new name in a new state. He had lost her in two words, and he'd done it himself.

Behind the glass the kid lifted his head. Dane went still. A gull went over. The kid went back to the mug.

Then, from the front of the house, Russ's fist hit the door, hard, from the side where he'd been told. Inside, the kid's wet hand closed on the pistol.

* * *

The kid was gone from the sink, and the pistol with him.

Dane came up off the snow and went in low to the wall under the sill. His right knee had set in the cold, and getting it under him cost a second he didn't have. Through the old walls and the towel in the pane, the board just inside the front door talked. The kid was at the door.

Russ's voice came from the side of the house, from behind the stone, where it belonged.

"Kid. It's Russ. Off Cole's boat."

Then the voice moved. Dane heard it come round off the chimney to the middle of the house, square in front of the door.

"I drove you home all summer. Come on out."

Russ thought he could talk him out. He had stepped into the doorway on his own, against the plan Dane had given him twice.

Dane couldn't leave the window. If he ran for the front, the window was open to the kid, and Beth was at the bottom of the lane. His first thought was of her: the truck below the rise, out of any line from this house. He wanted her so much it hurt, and he stayed where he was.

The shot cracked through the door.

Russ's shout changed. Dane heard him go down hard on the step, and then Russ yelling about his leg. "My leg. Damn it, he got my leg." The kid had fired through the front door, and Russ was hit, low.

A review board would read it as Dane's plan and Dane's constable. He would tell them Russ stepped in front of the door, and it would sound like an excuse, and it would be true. He held the window.

The sash went up over his head. The kid came out over the sink, pistol first in his good right hand, as Beth had said he would.

Dane took the gun hand as it cleared the sill. He bent the wrist back hard against the frame.

"Let go of it."

The pistol dropped into the snow. Dane pulled, and the kid came the rest of the way out and down, and Dane put him face down in the snow with a knee across his back. He minded the bound arm only once the gun was clear. He drew the good wrist round to the bound one and pulled a cable tie tight over both, and another over that.

The kid spoke into the snow. "My arm."

"I know which one. I'm the one who shot it."

The kid turned his face out of the snow, and what was in it was hate. Dane picked the pistol up and pushed it into his belt at the back. A boy with Cole's lawyer's number on his phone could sit in a room in Portland and say who sent him. He could only do it alive.

Dane hauled him up. The left heel dragged a line round the corner of the house. Russ lay on his back on the step with both hands clamped on his thigh. Dane took the kid round him and in through her front door, put him face down on the kitchen floor, and tied him to the iron leg of the sink with one more tie.

Out on the step Russ swore at the sky. From the bottom of the lane came boots, running, uneven, one knee that wouldn't bend, and Beth's voice saying Russ's name.

* * *

Beth came up the lane running stiff-legged on the bad knee, the ferry's first-aid box under one arm, pulling her gloves off with her teeth. She dropped to her knees on the step.

Russ was shot in the thigh, high up, and bleeding hard. His blood lay bright on the snow of the step, and it steamed in the cold.

"Knife," she said.

Dane took Ned's rope knife from his right coat pocket and put it in her hand. She slit the trouser leg from the knee up. She tore open the last dressing in the box and packed the hole with two fingers, deep, and Russ made a sound Dane had never heard from him.

"Shit," Beth told the wound. "Hold this."

Dane put his hands where hers had been.

"Press. Harder."

He pressed. She pulled a strap from the box, ran it round the thigh above the hole and pulled the knot tight with her teeth. The box lay empty on the step.

"Don't move," she told Russ.

Russ was gray, on his back on her step. "Did I stand in the door? I stood in the door." He turned his head to Dane. "You said the side."

"I did."

Russ's shotgun leaned on the chimney stone, where he had set it down to have his hands empty for the boy.

Dane watched her work. Her braid had fallen forward over her shoulder, and her hands were red to the wrists. Her voice had gone level and sure, and between the orders she said nothing at all. She was the best thing he had ever seen at work, and he wanted her. A new name in a strange state would keep her breathing and kill her. He didn't say it. It wasn't his to say yet.

The fire truck came up the lane, its engine loud in the still morning. The island had no doctor and no ambulance, so the fire truck carried the stretcher. Tess got down and went straight to Russ. She didn't speak to Beth or look at her. She took the strap end out of Beth's fingers without a word and checked the knot herself.

To Dane she said, "Haskell's cuffed to the pipe. He's not going anywhere on that hip."

"Town office couch," Dane said. "I'll bring the kid down behind you."

They got Russ onto the stretcher and into the back of the truck. Tess picked the shotgun off the chimney stone and laid it on the seat beside her. Then the fire truck went back down the lane with Russ in the back, and Beth stayed on her knees on the step with his blood to her wrists and didn't watch it go.

* * *

Beth came in wiping Russ's blood off her hands on a dish towel and stopped at the sink.

The stove the kid had lit was still going. Under the woodsmoke the kitchen smelled of somebody else's two nights in it, wet wool and canned stew. Russ was gone down the hill in the fire truck. On the floor the kid breathed short and wet against the leg of the sink.

The mugs he had washed hung on her hooks with their handles out. Nobody on this island hung a mug like that.

"You put the mugs away wrong," Beth told the kid.

She took one down, turned it, and hung it back with the handle in. She stood with her hand on it. One of her dish towels was round the kid's arm, and another was stuffed in the pane by her door, and she was holding a third.

Dane nearly laughed. He stood in her kitchen with a tied boy on the floor and Russ's blood on her step, and he wanted her so badly it was stupid. Then it passed, or he let it.

"That's the one they sent," he said to her. "He'll be in the town office back room with Ned by noon. On this island it's finished."

He texted his boss in front of her. *Have him. Alive.*

Beth folded the towel and hung it on the rail of the stove.

The pantry door stood open where the kid had eaten off her shelves. On the inside of it, in pencil, someone had drawn the harbor rocks: the ledges, the rocks just under the water at the harbor mouth, as small careful shapes, and one pencil line running inside them that wasn't on any chart.

"What's the line?" Dane said.

"My father's way home. Nobody's taken it since him."

It was the first thing she had said to him since Wednesday that wasn't business. Dane let it stand. He didn't answer it.

The kid had been listening. He laughed once, from the floor, and it hurt him. He turned his face up off the boards.

"You think I was what's coming?" he said. "They've got a boat."
