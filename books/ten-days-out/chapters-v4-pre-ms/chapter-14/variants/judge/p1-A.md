# Chapter Fourteen: The Wheel

By Friday night Beth Calloway could hear her own boat breaking itself against the pier through the town office wall.

Steel on stone: a long shriek, then a thud, each time the sea lifted the ferry and dropped it. She had her coat half on before she'd decided anything.

"I'm taking it off."

In the back room Dane ran a thumb under the kid's cable ties and added a fresh one. The kid kept his face to the wall.

Beth watched the thumb, slow and careful, and thought about it on the inside of her wrist. Then she made herself look at the door.

Tess held the shotgun out to him. "It's loaded, Marshal. I loaded it."

He broke it open, looked, closed it and handed it back. "We'll be on the ferry till light."

At the pier the ferry rolled on bare granite. The float, the floating dock that used to sit between steel and stone, had gone in the first storm.

"By light it's holed," Beth said. "A holed boat takes nobody to Portland. I'll hold it off inside the harbor till the wind goes round."

"The steering's never had the sea on it," Dane shouted. "Out there it's the rocks in the dark. Here it only grinds."

"Here it's holed for certain. Out there it might hold."

"What do you need?" he said.

"Two hands on the ropes that know what they're doing."

"I worked the back of a lobster boat two summers. I'm good at exactly this."

She took the flashlight out of her coat and put it in his hand, and Ned's knife after it. "Tonight you hold the light."

He looked at the flashlight longer than a flashlight needs.

The rod from the wheel to the blade under the boat that steers it was back in, as straight as the co-op vise could get it. At the stair she told him, "It might stick with the sea on it."

In eleven years she had never stood at this wheel with nobody beside her. Her father had stood at a wheel every working day of his life, and her hands went to this one as if they already knew it.

She called the ropes off from the cabin window, and he threw each one clear. The last held the whole boat, so tight it hummed. In the first storm a rope like that had parted like a gunshot.

"It won't come off the post," Dane shouted up. "Cut it?"

One second.

"Cut it."

He stood clear of the rope's line and cut it, and the end whipped back across the empty pier. She watched him do it right.

The boat swung free into the snow, and the stone fell away from its side. Dane stood on the car deck with the knife in one hand and her flashlight in the other, looking up at her window.

* * *

An hour in, the wheel went heavy under Beth's hands at one point in its turn.

Her palms knew it before she did. The nose began to swing off the wind, and she turned to bring it back.

The wheel stopped.

It wouldn't come back. The rod she'd straightened had stuck, with the blade under the boat turned all the way to one side, so the boat kept turning. Slowly at first. Then faster. Toward the rocks.

"Come on," she said. "Come on, damn you."

She threw her whole body on the wheel, and the rod came free all at once. Nothing held the blade then. The sea shoved it, and the wheel spun back through her hands with it. She held on as it spun, and it took the skin off both palms. The white rope scar across her left palm opened with the rest. She could have let go. She held on until it stopped, and then she held it there.

Dane was in the doorway. He had felt the boat turn wrong through his feet, down among the trucks, and come up the stair. He looked at her hands and said nothing.

A white light came on along the shore.

It swung across the snow and stopped on the rocks, and behind it was the shape of the red fire truck on the harbor road. The rocks were far closer than she had guessed, and the boat was still swinging toward them.

If she turned all the way, the rod might stick again, so she turned the wheel most of the way with her raw hands and held it. The boat came round slowly. The rocks went by in the white light a few yards off.

Tess. She had put the light on the rocks for Beth, and she still wouldn't say one word to her on the radio.

"Who's on the prisoners?" Dane said.

"Russ. She'll have given him the shotgun."

The first-aid box up at the town office was empty since Russ's leg. Dane took off his coat, then his shirt, and put the coat back on over his bare chest. He held the shirt up to her without a word.

For a second she kept both hands where they were. Then she took her left hand off the wheel. "Hold it here. Don't turn it."

He took the spoke exactly where hers had been, as if it might go off. He stood on one sleeve, split the shirt down its seam and wrapped her left palm while she steered with her right. Then she took the wheel back with the wrapped hand and gave him the other.

"That was my good shirt," he said.

"You don't have a good shirt."

"I had one. You're wearing it. Open and close them for me. Every few minutes, or they'll set."

"Stop," she told him, and he stopped.

His coat hung open over bare skin in a cabin cold enough to show her breath. She looked at the hollow of his throat and thought about her mouth there, and she was angry enough to keep both hands on the wheel.

Hours on, the light was still on the rocks. When he turned to watch it, she opened and closed her wrapped hands on the wheel, as he'd told her to, where he couldn't see her do it.

* * *

In the first light Beth saw the big boat before she saw anything else.

It lay off the point, past the white water at the harbor mouth, showing its one light, closer in than at dusk. Neither of them said what it was. On the harbor road the fire truck's light went off, and the truck drove away up the hill. All night Tess had never once swung the light up to the cabin.

Her hands sat on the wheel in his shirt, gone stiff and brown.

Dane propped his phone on the shelf under the front windows and called his boss on speaker. "The big boat came in closer overnight."

"Your Hal Mercer comes back to nobody," his boss said. "There's no license out of Gloucester in that name. That's a lie to a federal officer, and in this sea it still doesn't put a boarding team aboard. Nothing flies before Tuesday. The Coast Guard's on the storm's distress calls. If that boat comes at you, that's a distress, and they'll come from an hour off."

"The harbor mouth is white water till Sunday evening," Dane said, "and the next storm's in before light Monday. Sunday night or not at all."

"Sunday," Beth said.

"And the hold stands for Monday."

Beth kept her eyes off the phone. She looked at Dane, her wrapped hands on the wheel. "Cancel it."

He looked at her hands. Then he leaned to the phone. "Withdraw it. Tell the prosecutor this morning. She doesn't get held, and she doesn't go into the program unless she says so. Write that down."

"You asked for it Wednesday."

"I was scared, and I called it the job." He took a breath. "And I'm involved with her. Write that down too."

The line went quiet.

"Written."

"Then she's on her own after Monday."

"Not all of her. I've put in for the Maine office." He took the phone off the shelf and held it out to Beth.

"Say it to me," Beth said.

"After Monday nobody from my office stands between you and those people. Do you understand what you're choosing?"

"I know what I'm choosing. I'm the one standing here."

The call ended. Dane stayed standing, an arm's length off.

"I still think you should go. It was never mine to say. I knew that before last night, and then you took it off the stone when I'd have left it there, and you held that wheel with the skin coming off your hands, and you were right both times."

Beth held the nose where it was. "Then you ask me. Every time. Even when you're sure. And you never go round me to anybody again."

"Yes." He thought about it, which she liked. "What if somebody's shooting at us?"

"Then ask fast."

"And my terms," he said. "When I think you're wrong, I say so, to your face, as loud as it takes."

"Fine." She watched the light off the point. "Wednesday I said, 'And so do we.' For both of us. Same thing I hated you for."

"Then we split Wednesday," he said. "I'll take the bigger half. I'm the one who filed it."

It was five days since he had said he loved her on the lighthouse, and she had told him to ask again in five. He didn't ask. She looked at him in his open coat with nothing under it and wanted to be asked.

"What do you want, Dane?"

"I'll tell you on the other side. When nobody's paying me to stand here."

By seven the wind had gone round. She brought the boat's nose toward the pier, and he went down the stair to take the ropes without being told.

* * *

Dane Reyes had the ham in the town-office oven by three, because that was the one thing on Cutter he could still fix.

The back room smelled of cloves. He was in Russ's flannel shirt, short in the arms. That morning Beth had called the ferry board on his phone and asked for the boat for tonight, and they had given it to her. Her palms were in black electrical tape from the co-op.

Low under the point, the one light hadn't moved since dawn yesterday. He shut the window on it.

Beth stood in the doorway where Russ and Tess could both see her.

"Russ. Tess. He's going to ask me something, and I'm going to say yes. I'm telling you myself, so nobody ever says it for me."

Tess's eyes came up off the desk to Beth's face and stayed there.

Dane still had the oven mitt on. "You just told them the answer."

"Ask anyway. Even when you're sure."

He took the mitt off. "Can I kiss you? I'm asking."

"Yes." She didn't come to him yet. "And what you said to me at the lighthouse. Yes to that too. I love you. I have since the rock, when you opened your hand."

Dane didn't move. On the couch Russ set his glass down.

"Come here," she said.

He went. She couldn't put her palms on him, so she hooked her wrists over his shoulders and held her taped hands up behind his neck. He kept his own hands at her waist, clear of her palms. She kissed him slowly, after four days of not, and the oven was hot at his back and Russ was watching, and some part of him went on working out how this would sound in a courtroom, and then it quit. He thought her name, and her mouth, and nothing else.

Tess stood with the shotgun. "I'll be on the steps. Somebody ought to watch the harbor." She went out past Beth without a look, and the door shut hard.

Beth watched it shut over his shoulder. She didn't let go of him.

From the couch Russ said, "Well."

* * *

By eight it was full dark, and Dane Reyes lay on his back under the bait truck with his flashlight, in the cold smell of old herring, looking for a man or for anything left behind.

Up behind the back wheel he found a cheap phone in a plastic bag, taped to the frame. Its screen lit through the plastic when he touched it.

Friday's spray had iced everything on this deck by morning. This bag was dry, the battery near full. Somebody had come aboard Saturday, while the ferry sat empty and he held the light for her at the co-op vise. That one was his. Ned and the kid were tied up all day; anybody else on Cutter could have come down the harbor road.

"It tells the boat off the point when we leave the pier," he told Beth. "Can I switch it off?"

"Off."

"When it goes dead, they'll know we're moving."

"Then we go dark. Let them find us by ear."

He held the button through the bag until the screen went out. Ned wore his life jacket as if it were somebody else's.

At the hatch Beth thumbed the rod's bright new mark. "If it sticks, I'll feel it first."

At the wheel she asked for Ned beside her, because he heard breaking water in the dark before anybody saw it.

"He's the man who sold you to them," Dane said.

"Down there he's an arm's length from the kid."

"Your call." He cuffed Ned to the rail by her left hand and dropped the key in her pocket.

"Three hours at best," she said. "We cast off at ten."

"Why ten?"

"High water's an hour after. I want every foot of it under us at the harbor mouth."

"Do we go?"

"Go. If it comes at us, I run inside the ledges, close in to the point."

Every light aboard went off at her word. His hand went to the cabin light switch out of habit.

"Touch that and you swim."

At ten she took it off the pier and across the harbor by feel.

He stood at her right shoulder with the rifle and looked at the back of her neck under the braid, and thought about his mouth there.

At the mouth she slowed. White water broke loud on both sides, and Ned turned his head toward it, listening.

Then, late, under their own engine, Dane heard a bigger engine come up out of the dark off the point.

Her taped hands stayed where they were on the wheel. "Tell me where it is."

Dane took the rifle to the back window.
