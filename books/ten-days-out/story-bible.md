## THE CORE

**Premise:**
On a Thursday night in November, Beth Calloway, 33, a deckhand on the Cutter Island ferry, watches a crate split open on the car deck and sees fentanyl packed under the lobsters, with the island's well-liked lobster dealer, Cole Varney, at the wheel of the truck. She calls the police. A little before three in the morning a man breaks into her house and chases her through the snow, and Deputy U.S. Marshal Dane Reyes arrives in time to get an oar swung at his head. The truck was clean by the time police stopped it, so her eyes are the whole case. The grand jury sits in Portland on Monday the 23rd, and the prosecutor won't go in without Beth in the room; if they don't indict him that morning, she says, Cole walks. Beth refuses to leave her island. Dane refuses to leave her on it. A storm shuts the island off with the two of them, the man hunting her, and somebody she loves who keeps telling him where she sleeps.

**Emotional question:**
Can a woman who has never asked anyone for anything ask out loud for what she wants (the captain's job, and this man), and can a man who decides for the people he protects learn to ask her first, and still keep her alive?

**Tone:** Hot, funny, scared.

**This story is NOT:**
- **A boat book.** The ferry is where Beth works. Her skill shows in what her hands do. A listener who has never been on a boat follows every line (World rule 5).
- **A slow burn.** They spark on the first page they share, kiss in Ch 03, and sleep together in Ch 04. The reader knows they want each other from the oar on. The suspense in the romance is what wanting each other costs, with a jury watching.
- **A misunderstanding.** Nothing between Beth and Dane is overheard, withheld or guessed wrong. They fight to each other's faces, both partly right, and the fights tip into flirting.
- **A clue puzzle.** No keys, bags, seals, tags, tides or logs. The mystery is one question a listener can hold: who knew where she was?
- **Danger at a distance.** The man comes for her on the page. Something bad happens at least every second chapter.
- **A dark romance.** Dane never menaces, claims or overrides her body. The danger comes from the plot.

**Prose register, at a high point (danger, a fight, a crisis):**
Real time, beat by beat, no cutaway. Plain words, short and medium sentences, cause before effect so a listener can follow ("That made no sense. A dealer who let his lobsters die lost his whole load. Unless the lobsters weren't the load."). The viewpoint character's fear is said plainly and at its true size ("every step of the way she wanted to run"; "That frightened her more than anything"). Then the body: teeth, feet, breath, the sound of a door. The two leads break in opposite directions, and the contrast is the scene. Beth goes quiet and listens; her dialogue drops to two or three words. Dane talks more, low and steady, present tense, so she knows where he is. If both go quiet, the scene has lost a character. In anger Beth gets plainer and bossier and uses his full name; Dane gets polite and slow, and "Ms. Calloway" comes back.

**Prose register, at a quiet point:**
A quiet scene is built on one want the reader can feel, and something still happens in it (World rule 7). Beth's interior runs a little longer than her speech: plain, physical sentences about what is in front of her, landing on a short one. She thinks about wanting him in those words. Dane's interior runs long, on "and," about people and how a thing will sound later to a jury, ending on a short clause that undercuts him. Give one ordinary pleasure (a hot mug under her chin, onions in butter, dry socks) three or four sentences of room. The comedy lives in the gap between his paragraph and her one-line verdict, and nobody laughs at their own line.

**Prose register, at a sensual point:**
Real time, close in the viewpoint character's body and history. Beth says what she wants ("Come here." "Don't stop." "I want you."), and her interior turns into a long *and*-chain she notices she isn't steering. Dane goes quiet: her name, a question, "Can I?" In Dane's POV scenes his interior keeps its long sentences while his mouth stops, and he hears her ask. Humor survives inside the scene. Bodies are specific (her scar, his stitches, cold feet, stubble, bandaged hands). Frank words used sparingly and exactly (HEAT RULES).

**What changed from v2, in one line each:** feeling and fear may be named, plainly, at their true size; plot facts are said in plain words and said again; attraction is said in words, with gestures as extras; there is no cap on longing.

**Point of view (binding):** Close third, past tense. Dual POV per the outline: Ch 01 Beth then Dane (by scene); from Ch 02 Beth in even chapters, Dane in odd; Ch 17 Beth then Dane (by scene); Ch 18 Beth. **Every chapter and every POV switch names the viewpoint character in its first sentence** ("Dane Reyes lost the argument…"). No head-hopping inside a scene.

**Audit targets (whole book):** mean sentence 11–16 words; sentences of five words or fewer under 20% (the test chapter sits in this range); every chapter has at least three interior passages longer than the dialogue around them; jargon per World rule 5.

### Beth Calloway

- **Name:** Elizabeth Ann Calloway on her license, Beth to everyone. A plain name for a Maine girl born in 1993. Calloway is an old island family. Ned is the only person alive who calls her "Bethie." Tess calls her "Calloway." Dane calls her "Ms. Calloway" on the first night and when he's angry, and "Beth" after that.
- **Age, body, clothes:** 33. Five foot ten, almost Dane's height, so they meet eye to eye (the test chapter: "tall, almost his height"). Strong through the shoulders and arms from eleven years of chains and ropes. Long dark-blond braid, gray eyes, a red wind-burned face from October to April. A white scar across her left palm from a rope at 23. For work: an orange work coat, rubber boots, a black knit hat. At home: her father's old wool sweater, elbows darned in the wrong color. The prose never makes her size or shape a topic.
- **Physical habit others notice:** **she takes her gloves off to do anything that matters**: a chain, a knot, a cut on a man's arm, a dead engine. In sleet, bare-handed, and the gloves go back on after. Dane notices in Ch 04 when she tapes his arm. Tess scolds her for it. It costs her from Ch 11 on, when her hands are burned and she does it anyway in bandages.
- **Age-true behavior (33, in 2026):**
  1. **Her phone lives in a zip bag in her chest pocket.** She has a muted group text with two friends from high school, one in Portland and one in Bangor, both with small kids, and she reads it all at once on Sunday nights and hearts the baby pictures without writing anything.
  2. **She tried a dating app once.** From Pruett, at a fifty-mile radius, it found her nine men, and two were cousins. She deleted it in the ferry office parking lot. She tells Dane this in Ch 04 or Ch 08 as a joke against herself.
  3. **Her body is 33 and works for a living.** Her lower back is stiff after a double run and she stretches it against the kitchen counter without thinking. She can still go out a window at a run, but she feels it the next morning, and she says so.
  - *Also:* she has a saved search for cheap flights to somewhere warm (Florida, once Puerto Rico) that she has never booked. She has never been farther south than Boston. Her music comes from her phone through one earbud on the night runs, and it's mostly what she listened to at 19.
- **The age gap:** five years. Beth: "He was thirty-eight, which on Cutter counts as young." Dane: he notices she's younger than him for about a second on the first night and then forgets it for the rest of the book. It's never mentioned as a problem.
- **Background (single lines):**
  - Born and raised on Cutter. The island school through eighth grade, then high school on the mainland, boarding in the week.
  - Her mother left for Bangor when Beth was nine. They talk at Christmas.
  - Her father, Alton Calloway, was a lobsterman. The bank took his boat eight years ago and sold it to a summer man who made it a picnic boat.
  - Alton died of a heart attack six years ago, at 61. She lives alone in his house and keeps his fish shack on the rocks below it, with his oars still in the corner.
  - One year at the maritime college in Castine. She came home when her father got sick the first time.
  - Deckhand on the island ferry for eleven years, under Ned.
  - She got her captain's license at 30. The island newsletter ran her picture. She has never once taken the wheel on her own.
  - She owes nobody a dollar, on purpose.
- **What she wants that has nothing to do with Dane:** **The captain's job.** Ned retires in June. If no islander asks for it, the ferry board will hire a stranger from the mainland. Beth has the license and the eleven years. She hasn't asked Ned to put her name forward, because asking means saying out loud that he's old, and that she wants something. *What she's doing about it before the book starts:* she keeps the license current **[DC: was "she renewed the license this spring"; the credential runs five years and she got it three years ago]**. She has driven to the ferry office in Pruett twice and sat in the parking lot without going in. She watches every move Ned makes at the wheel and does it in her head after him. (Pays: Ch 08 she tells Dane, and he dares her to ask; Ch 09 she asks Ned on the beach and he says yes; Ch 13 her first time at the wheel; Ch 16 she lands the boat; Ch 18 the job is hers.)
  - *Small want:* to see a warm ocean once. Never booked. It sits behind "Those boots are for a different ocean" and can pay in one line in Ch 18.
- **Wound, and what it makes her do:** Her father did everything right and lost his boat on paper anyway, and she carried the bank man's papers out to his truck because her father asked her to. *Behavior:* she would rather do a job herself than ask for help with it. She pays back every favor at once. She never asks for anything she wants, so she can never be told no. And she trusts her own people completely, because they're all she has.
- **Need:** To ask out loud for what she wants (the job, the man) and let someone give it to her.
- **What she would never do:**
  1. Run. Not from her house, not from her island, not from a man with a gun. Running would tell Cole's people that shooting at a witness works.
  2. Leave a job half done because someone told her to stop.
  3. Lie about something she did. If they ask whether she slept with the marshal, she'll say yes, then tell them what she saw. (Ch 03, Ch 17.)
- **The choice a reasonable reader would condemn:**
  - **Primary (Ch 08 s2, the tower).** With Dane begging her not to, she phones Ned and Russ and asks them both to come down to the beach at three and see her off. Tess has to know, because she clears the landing. The other two don't. *Why she does it:* they're the two men she loves, it may be years before she sees them again, and she won't leave her island treating her own people like suspects. If she does that, Cole has already taken them from her. Cost: the ambush, and a Marine Patrol officer shot. The page gives her the reason in her own words, out loud, to Dane's face. **[DC: Tess doesn't "clear" the landing; she talks the boat in on the island radio. See Lines as they now stand.]**
  - **Secondary (Ch 10 s3, the test).** She lies to all three people she loves, one call at a time, and gives Tess the church. *Why:* she would rather know than go on loving all three of them and wondering about each one. Cost: her best friend runs into a burning building for her (Ch 11). Beth knows the test could hurt whoever turns out to be innocent, and she does it anyway.
- **How she is funny, and at whom:**
  - **The flat verdict.** One plain sentence, conclusion first, no smile to signal the joke. "Those boots are for a different ocean." "That ham is load-bearing." Her target is Dane's mainland softness and anything a man has bought to look prepared.
  - **She says the embarrassing thing out loud on purpose.** Everyone else talks around it, and Beth walks up to the counter and buys the condoms in front of Tess, two lobstermen and God. She asks Dane, "How many witnesses have kissed you?" as if it's an ordinary question. The joke is that she won't be embarrassed. Her targets are Dane, Tess and the island's gossip.
  - **She won't bet.** "I don't bet on what I can see." The relationship's joke (below).
  - *Sample:* Dane: "I'd like to point out that I jumped." / Beth: "You fell toward the boat. It's different."
  - *Who she's funny with:* Ned on the boat, where his slow understatement sets up her verdict. Tess at the counter, where Tess announces and Beth corrects her in four words.
- **What she finds attractive in Dane, plainly:**
  - *First thing she notices:* the boots, and then that he's warm. She leans into him in the snow before she's decided to.
  - His mouth. She thinks about it the first night and every night after, and says so in her head in those words.
  - That he talks to her in the dark, low and steady, when everyone else she knows goes quiet.
  - That he jumps when he plainly jumps badly. He doesn't wait to be good at a thing before he does it.
  - His forearms with his sleeves pushed up, cooking too much food.
  - That he loses an argument to her and says so, out loud, without sulking.
  - That he says what he wants. She has never been with a man who does.
- **Syntax profile:**
  - *Sentence length and shape.* Dialogue: short to medium, and always whole sentences now (no clipped deckhand fragments). Conclusion first; the reason only if pushed. Imperatives with no "please": "Come here." "Give me your arm." "Move." She asks short questions she already knows the answer to, one at a time, and waits. No hedges, no "I think," no "maybe." Interior: plain and physical, a little longer than her speech, and it says what she wants in plain words ("She wanted him"). It lands on a short sentence.
  - *Under pressure.* **Fear:** she goes quiet and listens, because she knows the island by sound. Two or three words at most ("Stop. Listen."). This is the core contrast with Dane, who talks more. **Anger:** she gets plainer and bossier and uses his full name, then does something with her hands (throws the mug, laces her boots). **Lying:** a bad liar out loud. The lie is too short and she follows it with a task or hangs up ("I'm fine. Hold this."). **Wanting:** she says it. "I want you." "Come here." "Don't stop." In the heat scenes her interior runs long and she notices she isn't steering it. This is her high-temperature register, and it has to be on the page.
  - *What she notices first.* Hands, what people are doing with them and whether they know how. Then sounds: a door, a step, an engine that's wrong.
  - *One habit of thought hers alone.* **She sums people up in one plain sentence.** "Russ Whitcomb was a good man who was bad with money." "Cole Varney tipped twenty dollars at Christmas and never once looked her in the eye." She has a verdict on everyone, and the book breaks the one she has on Ned. *Second habit:* when she can't decide, she fixes the nearest broken thing, and by the time it works she knows what she's going to do (the generator in Ch 08, the steering in Ch 13).
  - *Her words, never Dane's:* "Marshal" (as a tease, when she's flirting; "Dane" when it's serious), "Fine." (her pivot), "Quit it.", "Come here.", "Go home.", "That's a no.", "ours", "from away" (once or twice, for mainland people), "the boat" (always the ferry), "wicked" (once in the book at most). She never says "okay" as a pivot and never uses a police or legal word without making fun of it.
- **Ten lines of dialogue:**
  - *Flirting*
    1. "You keep looking at my mouth, Marshal. Say something or do something. I'm cold either way."
    2. "I'm going to kiss you. If you've got a reason why not, you've got about three seconds, and it had better be better than a jury."
  - *Angry*
    3. "You told your boss I'd go. With me standing right here. You decided I'd rather be alive in Ohio than be me, and you didn't even ask."
    4. "Move, Dane, or I go out the window. I've done it before. In socks."
  - *Lying*
    5. "Tess. It's me. We're in the church tonight. Don't tell anyone. Not Russ, not Ned. I have to go." (Ch 10 s3)
    6. "I'm fine. Hold this. No, the other end."
  - *Afraid*
    7. "Stop. Listen. He's on the steps. That's his bad foot."
    8. "Hold my hand and do exactly what I do. Don't think about it. Just do it."
  - *Tender*
    9. "Come here. You're shaking. Let me be the warm one for once."
    10. "Will you stay? I'm asking. I don't, mostly."
- **Five lines of interior narration:**
  1. She wanted him. She'd wanted him since the oar, and she was done calling it the cold.
  2. Russ Whitcomb was a good man who was bad with money, and she had known both of those things since the fourth grade.
  3. She didn't know what to do, so she fixed the generator, and when it caught under her hands she knew she was going to make the calls.
  4. In eleven years Ned had never been afraid, and now he was afraid every time she looked at him, so she stopped looking.
  5. He tasted like bad coffee. Fine. Let the jury hear about it.
- **Three ordinary pleasures:**
  1. Onions frying in butter in a cold kitchen at dusk, and the smell filling the house before anything else is ready. (Ch 06 opens on it.)
  2. A dead machine starting under her bare hands: the generator, the steering, the heater in the truck.
  3. The first hot mug of the morning, held under her chin with both hands until it's cool enough to drink.

### Dane Reyes

- **Name:** Daniel Efraín Reyes on paper. His mother's family called him Dane from the crib to tell him apart from her father, Daniel, and it stuck. Efraín was his father's name. A Lowell, Massachusetts name from a Puerto Rican father and an Irish-American mother, which is common in that city. Beth can learn the full name in one flirting line, optional, no earlier than Ch 07.
- **Age, body, clothes:** 38. Six foot one, lean, a runner. Dark hair cut short, going gray at the temples. Brown eyes. Clean-shaven on the first night and stubbled by Day 3, and it suits him. Old scar across the right knuckles. A dark gray jacket, jeans, and brand-new waterproof boots from a store on Route 1 that also sells fudge, which are wrong for snow, ice and everything else on Cutter. A gun on his right hip and a small first-aid kit he calls the trauma kit. No gun model on the page.
- **Physical habit others notice:** **he sits with his back to the wall and watches people's hands before their faces.** Tess: "Your marshal looks at my hands like he's pricing them." Beth takes the other chair without being asked by Ch 05. Second habit: **he talks.** In a crisis, low and steady, all the way through. Everyone on the island notices that the marshal never shuts up, and Beth notices when he finally does.
- **Age-true behavior (38, in 2026):**

## RELATIONSHIPS

### Beth and Dane

- **The friction that isn't the plot:**
  1. **Talk against silence.** When she's scared she goes quiet and listens; when he's scared he talks. Each thinks the other's way is a little crazy. In danger the contrast is the scene: Dane talking low and steady, Beth silent and listening. It pays in Ch 16: "You came down." / "You stopped talking."
  2. **How it will look against what's in front of her.** He thinks about everything the way a jury would hear it later. She thinks about the thing in front of her and does it. "How's that going to sound in a courtroom?" / "It's going to sound like I did it." Neither is wrong, which is why it's funny and not only a fight.
  3. **Owing.** She pays back every favor on the spot: the coffee, the hand up, the coat. He lets favors ride and would rather be owed. "You're welcome." / "I didn't say anything." / "I noticed."
- **What they enjoy about each other before they admit it:** She likes being talked to in the dark, and that he loses an argument to her without sulking. He likes that she's the first witness in forty-one he can't predict, that she bosses him around, and that she's rude to him and right.
- **The relationship's own joke: the bet she won't take.** He bets a dollar on everything ("Dollar says Tess has us married by noon"). She answers with the outcome instead: "I don't bet on what I can see." Planted in Ch 06 s1 while she cooks. In Ch 15 she takes her first bet and wins. ### The strip-the-names test

Run before saving on the twenty lead dialogue lines and the ten interior lines, with names and labels removed and the order shuffled. All thirty sorted without hesitation. The cues:

- **Beth:** short whole sentences, conclusion first; imperatives with no "please" ("Come here." "Move." "Hold this."); "Marshal" as a tease; "Fine."; one-sentence verdicts on people; wanting said plainly ("I want," "Come here"); hands and fixing things; in fear, two or three words and then silence.
- **Dane:** long sentences with "because" and "which" building to a dry turn; "Here's how this goes," "Dollar says," "I'm keeping count," "Ms. Calloway"; jokes against himself; how it will sound to a jury; asking permission ("Can I?"); in fear, more words, not fewer; lies in three words.

Lines that needed a rewrite to pass:
- **Beth's tender line 9** first read "Come here. You're freezing." Dane could have said that. "Let me be the warm one for once" is hers: it answers his coat in the snow in Ch 01, and only she owes him that.
- **Dane's afraid line 7** first ended at "you keep breathing where I can hear it." The self-mocking turn ("ask anybody in Massachusetts") makes it his.
- **Beth's interior 2** and **Dane's interior 2** both run on "and." Hers is a verdict about another person; his is a count about himself. Keep those shapes in any edit.

**For the dialogue doctor and voice editor:** an earlier voice editor flattened both leads toward one register. If a line could belong to either lead, it's wrong. The danger scenes are the test: Beth goes silent and Dane talks. The heat scenes are the second test: Beth says what she wants and Dane goes quiet. If both leads do the same thing, the scene has lost a character.

### Flags for the orchestrator

**Outline events against these people.** All are plausible for these two. Four need a line on the page so the reader believes them:

1. **Ch 08 to Ch 13, Dane's arc runs backward once, on purpose.** In Ch 08 he lets her make the tower calls against his own judgment; in Ch 13 he decides the program for her. That reads as a contradiction unless the page shows what changed in between: he watched her run into a burning church in Ch 11 and lost the argument with his own fear. Ch 11's interior ("the terror of watching her go in") has to be there in plain words, and Ch 13 should name New Bedford and the church in the same breath.
2. **Ch 16 s2, Beth hands Ned the wheel.** She has just learned he sold her. She'll do it only with one interior line of reasoning: he's the only other person aboard who can hold the boat in that sea, and he can't swim, so he won't put it on the rocks with himself, Tess and Russ aboard. Without that line, a reader calls it the comps' top complaint, a heroine's senseless choice in danger.
3. **Ch 05 s1, Ned at the counter hears everything.** In character for Beth: she would tell Ned straight out anyway, and she doesn't think of Ned hearing as a leak. That's her blind spot. It's still the line an attentive reader may notice (outline Pacing flag 8). My recommendation for this cast is to have Beth tell Ned directly, as she tells Tess and Russ. It's more like her, and it reads as her choice instead of a planted overhearing.
4. **Ch 10 s3, Beth's lie to Tess has to work.** Beth is a bad liar out loud. On the phone it works because her lie is short and she hangs up (line 5 above). The beats shouldn't give her a long, smooth lie.

**Choices for the author to confirm (defaults in bold):**

## WORLD & SETTING

**Time and place:** Cutter Island, Maine (invented), in an unnamed midcoast bay, an hour by ferry from **Pruett** (invented), a small mainland harbor town a few miles off Route 1. Portland, where the grand jury sits, is **two hours' drive** from Pruett. Year-round population about 240; summer about 1,100. One ferry, one store, one fire truck, a part-time constable, a church, an island school (and the old one-room schoolhouse beside the church), a town office, the fishermen's co-op on its wharf, a summer yacht club shut for the season, about forty summer houses shut for the winter, and an old lighthouse on the north end. **No airstrip, no bridge, no doctor, no police station.** The story runs Thursday night, November 12, 2026 (Day 0) through Monday, November 23 (the grand jury), with the HEA on Thanksgiving, Thursday, November 26. Weekdays match the 2026 calendar (domain-checked). The year is never printed.

**Atmosphere:** a small working island in the dark half of the year, shut in by weather and then shut off. Shuttered summer houses, generators starting one by one at dusk, everyone knowing which house is whose. The cold is a working condition: wet socks, numb hands, a stove that has to be fed. Nobody on the page calls the sea beautiful or angry. Gossip is the island's other weather, and most of it means well, which is why it's dangerous.

### Island geography, plain (binding)

The island runs north to south. **The harbor and the village are at the south end**, facing the mainland. The north end is woods, shore rocks, the north beach and the lighthouse. A low ridge runs up the middle.

- **The harbor road** runs along the harbor: the ferry pier at the west end, then the store, the co-op wharf with its net loft, the church and the old schoolhouse, the yacht club boathouse, Russ's house, Ned's house, and on up the hill. **[BB]**
- **The Dyer house** sits above the harbor road on the hill behind the village. Its upstairs window looks down on the whole harbor: the church, the old school, the co-op loft and the yacht club boathouse are all in view (Ch 10–11).
- **Beth's house** (her father's) stands on a rise on the east side of the village, above a small cove of its own (Calloway Cove) where a skiff can land in the dark if you know the rocks. Her father's fish shack sits on the rocks at the bottom of the hill.
- **The east road** leaves the village and runs up the island's east side past shut summer houses. The kid's camp is in one of them (Ch 05).
- **The north side** has the summer house Russ looks after (Ch 05–06), in woods above the shore rocks, and about a mile on, the **old lighthouse** on the north point, with **the north beach** below it, sand between rocks, with trees along the top.
- **The harbor mouth** is narrow, with **ledges** (rocks just under the water) on both sides. A boat has to slow down to get through.
- **Pruett** (mainland): the ferry ramp; a small floating dock where the kid keeps his skiff; Cole's cold-storage building on the Pruett wharf; a motel on Route 1 ten minutes from the harbor (Dane's, night 0); Route 1 a few miles inland.

### Phones, power and radio (binding)

- **Power:** the island's power fails in the first storm on Day 1, Friday night (Russ's house is dark in Ch 03; his generator runs). It stays out for the rest of the book. Houses with generators have light; the rest don't. Every lit window on the harbor road can be seen by every other house.
- **Cell signal:** one cell tower on the ridge above the village, on its own backup generator **[DC: correct; someone refuels it from Tess's fuel store, unsaid on the page]**. It covers the village and the harbor, weakly. **No signal** inside the co-op loft (sheet-metal walls; Dane can't step outside with a shooter looking, Ch 04), at the north-side summer house, at the north beach (Ch 09), or at the lighthouse keeper's house. **One bar from the iron walkway around the top of the lighthouse** (Ch 08), which sticks up above the ridge. Signal at the Dyer house upstairs window (Ch 10) and in the town office (Ch 13, Ch 15).
- **Finding a phone [DC §8]:** a modern phone can report its own GPS location when the carrier asks it. **The kid keeps his phone off** and turns it on for a minute to read and send, then off again. By the time the carrier asks where it is, it's off, and all they have is the island's one tower. That is why nobody can find him by it, and why the bait has to wait for his reply.
- **Landlines** work through Day 1 night (the Dyer call to the store, Ch 03). Beth's Ch 01 call to the police is from her kitchen, phone unspecified, as written.
- **The island radio [BB]:** the fire company and the constable carry handheld radios, and Tess has a base set at the store. Plain on the page: "the island's emergency radio." Ch 03 (Tess sends Russ to the Dyer house), Ch 09 (Tess talks the Marine Patrol boat in to the beach) and Ch 14 (Tess called to Russ) use it.
- **Tess's emergency [DC §7]:** Tess declares an island emergency on Friday (said once at most, Ch 03). It gives her no power over who lands where, and it goes unsaid after Ch 03.

- **Tessa Bailey, *It Happened One Summer*** (2021). A fishing-town romance with open-door scenes at full length. Take: heat scenes where the leads' humor stays alive inside them, and wanting said out loud. Avoid: her heat level (above ours) and the fish-out-of-water setup.
- **Suzanne Brockmann, *The Unsung Hero*** (2000). Romantic suspense with a protector hero and dual POV. Take: danger scenes that stay inside the characters' bodies and fears, and a hero whose interior the reader can hear wanting her. Avoid: the military jargon and the large cast.

### Sample A. High temperature (Beth's close third: danger), from the test chapter

A little before three, the back door handle turned.

Slowly. Somebody testing it. Then a pause, and a crack, and the whole door jumped in its frame as a shoulder hit it from outside.

Beth didn't think. She was in her wool socks, and she went out the window over the kitchen sink, on the far side of the house from the door, the way she'd gone out it at sixteen, and dropped into the snow. Behind her, the back door gave with a bang. She heard boots in her kitchen. A man's voice said her name, quietly, like he was calling a cat.

"Beth. Come on. Nobody wants to hurt you."

She ran.

Down the hill behind the house, through the snow in her socks, the cold so sharp it felt like fire. Her father's old fish shack stood at the bottom on the rocks by the water, a little shingled building where he'd kept his traps. She got the door open and pulled it shut behind her and stood in the pitch dark with her back against the wall, breathing through her mouth so she wouldn't make a sound.

### Sample B. High temperature (Dane's close third: the spark), from the test chapter

He looked down at her. Her face was six inches from his. He could feel her breathing against his chest, fast and shallow, and he wanted, very badly and very unprofessionally, to find out what her mouth would do if she ever actually smiled.

### Sample C. Quiet (Beth's close third: an ordinary morning), original

Beth Calloway had been watching the marshal lose to her father's toaster for ten minutes, and she was enjoying it more than anything since September.

The toaster had one slot that worked and one that didn't, and you had to hold the lever down with a fork, and everybody on the island over the age of six knew that. Dane Reyes didn't. He had it upside down on her counter with crumbs all down the front of his good gray jacket, and he was explaining to it, in whole sentences, that he had a badge and a college degree and he would like his toast.

"It wants a fork," she said.

"I'm not putting a fork in a toaster. My mother was a police dispatcher. I know how that call ends."

"Fine. Starve."

She sat at her father's table with the first mug of the morning held under her chin in both hands, too hot to drink yet, and let the steam come up into her face. Out the window the harbor was flat and gray, the gulls were working the end of the pier, and she had nowhere to be for three hours, which hadn't happened on a weekday since she was twenty-two. She watched his hands. They were careful hands, rough across the palms in a way she hadn't figured out yet, and she wanted them on her more than she wanted breakfast, which was saying something, because she was starving.

He looked up and caught her looking. He didn't say a word about it. He smiled, slow, like a man who'd been dealt one good card and meant to hold onto it, and went back to losing to the toaster.

### Sample D. Sensual (Beth's close third: heat register), original

The space heater had two settings, off and Florida, and somebody had left it on Florida. Beth had her back against a cold wall, and everything in front of her was warm: the orange bars of the heater, the lamp, and Dane, who had stopped talking. That was what she noticed first. He had talked the whole way here, low and steady, about his father's bus route and a dog on his street in Lowell that bit the mailman and nobody else for nine years, and he had kept on talking while she unzipped his jacket. Then she put her hand on his stomach under his shirt, and he stopped in the middle of a word and didn't start again.

"Look at that," she said. "Quiet."

"Yes."

"Come here, then."

He put his forehead against hers and breathed out, and she felt him laugh without a sound. His hands found the bottom of her sweater and waited there, and she knew what they were waiting for, so she pulled it off herself, and her shirt after it, and stood in front of him in the hot little room and let him look. She wanted to cross her arms. She didn't. She wanted him to stop looking and start touching, and she said so, in about those words, and he did.

"Beth," he said. Only that. Then, against her mouth: "Can I?"

"I said so. Don't make me say it twice." She took his hand and showed him where, and his hand was careful and hard-skinned and slow across her breast until she had to put her head back against the wall and shut her eyes, and the wind was banging on the roof and somewhere out there a man with a gun was looking for her, and she was thinking about that, and then she wasn't, and she noticed she wasn't, and she had no plan for any of this past his mouth, which was on her throat and then lower. He got her jeans open and his fingers found her wet, and she heard herself say his name like a question, and he answered it the way she'd asked it, slowly, and she stopped keeping track of anything but that.

### Sample E. Dane's interior (quiet into wanting), original

Dane Reyes had sat across a lot of tables from a lot of witnesses, and he could usually tell you, by the second cup of coffee, what each one would say on the stand, and how a jury would hear it, and which line a defense lawyer in a good coat would read back slowly. He couldn't do it with her. She ate soup like it had personally wronged her, with her elbows on the table and her braid over one shoulder, and she had just told him, without looking up, that his plan for tomorrow was stupid, and given him three reasons, and the third one was right.

"Fair," he said.

"I know it's fair. I said it."

He laughed, which he hadn't meant to do, and she looked up then, and he watched her decide whether to be pleased about it. She was. She hid it badly, in the soup. He wanted to reach across the table and take her face in both hands and find out what she tasted like when she was pleased, and he thought about how that would sound to twelve people from Maine, and then he thought about it some more, and then he stopped thinking about the twelve people from Maine entirely, which had never once happened to him in nine years of this job.
