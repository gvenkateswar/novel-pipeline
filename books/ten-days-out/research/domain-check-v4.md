## 1. The clock: "The grand jury has to hear her in person by Monday, or Cole walks"

**Claim (bible, The clock; outline, The case and the clock):** On Monday the 23rd a judge decides whether to keep Cole locked up. Before that, the grand jury has to hear Beth in person. If they haven't heard her by Monday morning, Cole walks out.

**Verdict: wrong as a legal rule. The Monday date and the stakes can stay.**

**What is actually the case:**
- **The real deadline is the preliminary hearing.** For a defendant in custody it must be held "no later than 14 days after the initial appearance" unless he is indicted first (Fed. R. Crim. P. 5.1(a), (c)). If it isn't held in time and there's no indictment, the man "shall be discharged from custody" (18 U.S.C. § 3060(d)), though he can be charged again. An indictment returned before the hearing date cancels the hearing (§ 3060(e)). So an indictment is how the prosecutor keeps him in without a hearing.
- **Fourteen days doesn't land on Monday the 23rd.** Cole is arrested about 1 a.m. Friday the 13th. Initial appearance Friday the 13th gives a deadline of Friday the 27th. Initial appearance Monday the 16th gives Monday the 30th. But the judge may set the hearing earlier ("within a reasonable time, but no later than…", Rule 5.1(c)), so a hearing set for Monday the 23rd is fine.
- **The grand jury doesn't need her in person.** An indictment may rest entirely on hearsay (*Costello v. United States*, 350 U.S. 359 (1956)). DOJ policy: "As a general rule, it is proper to present hearsay to the grand jury," as long as jurors aren't misled into thinking it's a first-hand account (Justice Manual 9-11.232). A case agent could read them her statement.
- **The preliminary hearing doesn't need her either.** Probable cause there "may be based on hearsay evidence in whole or in part," and the rules of evidence don't apply (Rule 5.1 committee notes; FRE 1101(d)(3)). The defense can cross-examine whoever testifies (Rule 5.1(e)).
- **The judge can also move the date.** Without the defendant's consent, only on "a showing that extraordinary circumstances exist and justice requires the delay" (Rule 5.1(d)). A storm that strands the witness is an argument for it, though not a sure one.
- **So the honest version is a prosecutor's judgment, not a rule.** The prosecutor won't ask twelve strangers to indict a well-liked man on a midnight phone call read aloud by an agent, with a few grains of powder. She wants Beth in the room. Without her, Cole's lawyer gets a judge on Monday and a weak case.

**Smallest change (same date, same stakes, same scenes):**
- Bible and outline: "Cole's lawyer pushed for a quick hearing, and the judge set it for Monday the twenty-third at two: a judge decides whether there's enough to keep Cole locked up. The grand jury sits that morning at ten. The prosecutor won't go in on a phone call and a little powder. She wants Beth in the room, telling them what she saw. If they indict him that morning, he stays in. If they don't, the prosecutor says she can't keep him."
- On the page the plain line can stay close to what's written: "The grand jury has to hear her by Monday morning, or the prosecutor says Cole walks." Putting it in the prosecutor's mouth makes it true.
- Move the hearing to the afternoon so it doesn't collide with the ten o'clock grand jury.
- "Why not postpone?" gets one line if the beats want it: "His lawyer won't agree to wait, and the prosecutor won't bet a judge will."
- *Lawyer's nuance, from working knowledge, not page-critical:* "until she testifies, killing her saves Cole" is a little overstated. If Cole had her killed, her earlier statements could still be used against him (FRE 804(b)(6), forfeiture by wrongdoing), but the government would have to prove he did it. Keep the line; nobody on the page needs to say this.

## 1b. Fallback clock: Cole's detention hearing is Monday

**Verdict: correct, if the page says the judge set it.** By statute the detention hearing is held "immediately upon the person's first appearance" unless a party asks for a continuance: up to five days on the defendant's motion and three on the government's, longer only for good cause, and "during a continuance, such person shall be detained" (18 U.S.C. § 3142(f), as quoted in *United States v. Montalvo-Murillo*, 495 U.S. 711 (1990); CRS). Missing the deadline doesn't free him automatically (*Montalvo-Murillo*).

**Smallest change:** combine the two clocks. Initial appearance Monday the 16th. His lawyer asks for time, and the judge sets the detention hearing and the preliminary hearing together for Monday the 23rd. Five business days from Monday the 16th is Monday the 23rd (*the weekend-exclusion wording of § 3142(f) wasn't in the quote I could fetch; unclear*). Either way, a judge-set date is safe.

**Sources:** [*Montalvo-Murillo* (law.resource.org)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/495/495.US.711.89-163.html) · [CRS IG10084](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-07-30_IG10084_1bce12c600206397f8b855ce6769d00d8d8c5969.html)

## 2. Residue plus an eyewitness holds a man a few days

**Claim:** The truck was clean. Powder in the seams of one crate is "enough to hold him a few days and not enough to convict him."

**Verdict: correct.**
- A warrantless arrest needs a complaint filed promptly (within 48 hours) and probable cause, which an identified eyewitness with a photo of the plate supplies (CRS; Rule 5.1 notes on hearsay for probable cause).
- He can be held through a detention continuance (§ 3142(f), above).
- Fentanyl distribution in any amount carries a maximum of twenty years. That makes him eligible for a detention hearing and for the presumption of detention on probable cause (18 U.S.C. § 3142(e)(3)(A), (f)(1)(C)). *I couldn't fetch the statute's text (Cornell blocked); this is from working knowledge, but the presumption is applied in the New Hampshire opinion below.*
- "Not enough to convict" is fair: trace residue on one crate with no load is weak at trial without her.

**Smallest change:** none. Optional realism: troopers held Cole on her word and the plate photo at 1 a.m., and the dawn residue went into the federal complaint.

## 4. The Marshals protect a witness before trial; protection is voluntary

**Claim:** Dane, a Deputy U.S. Marshal, protects Beth from the first night. She can refuse to leave. "Protection is voluntary. Dane can't make her leave. The subpoena makes her testify."

**Verdict: correct on voluntary; one wrong assumption about who protects her.**
- **Before a witness is admitted to the program, protecting her is the case agency's job, not the Marshals'.** Protection "remains the responsibility of the sponsoring investigative agency" until the Office of Enforcement Operations (OEO) approves admission and the Marshals can arrange the move (Justice Manual 9-21.220).
- **The exception is emergency protection.** If a witness is "in imminent danger of harm and the investigative agency is not able to provide the necessary protection," OEO may authorize emergency program protection, provided by the Marshals before the risk assessment and the signed agreement, after a preliminary interview (JM 9-21.220). That fits a deputy sent out on the first night. The manual doesn't say how fast that approval can come (*unclear: a midnight approval by phone is plausible but not sourced*).
- **Emergency protection means a temporary move.** The Marshals protect by relocating, which is why Dane's first line is "At first light I'm taking you off this island." When she refuses, the storm is what keeps him there. That's already the story.
- **Voluntary.** Adults sign a memorandum of understanding to enter the program (JM 9-21.500; 18 U.S.C. § 3521(d), from the setup pass). Nothing compels her to accept a protector. The grand jury subpoena compels her to appear (setup §3).
- **Testimony comes first.** A witness is accepted after testifying to the grand jury or committing to testify at trial, and the testimony must be "significant and essential" (JM 9-21.100). That fits "after she testifies, the program."
- **"They stop protecting her after Monday" if she declines.** Consistent with all of the above.
- The prior correction stands: never "material witness".

**Smallest change:**
- Bible, The clock / Protection: "Dane is on emergency protection, approved that night because the case agents couldn't get anyone out to the island. It's meant to move her somewhere safe. She won't go, and the storm keeps him there."
- The approved Ch 01 already works ("Somebody from the U.S. Marshals is coming out to you tonight"). No change to the test chapter for this item.
- On the page, plain: "emergency protection" once, if needed. Never "OEO" or "WITSEC".

## 5. Witness protection: "a new name in a new state, no contact with anyone from her old life, ever, including him"

**Verdict: can't confirm as worded.**
- **Confirmed:** "Witnesses and their families typically get new identities with documentation." The program covers "authorized family members." The Marshals give "24-hour protection to all witnesses while they are in a high-threat environment," including court appearances (USMS fact sheet, 2024). The witness "should be instructed to keep secret the area of his/her relocation" and goes back to the danger area only for court, returned "as soon as practicable" (JM 9-21.950, 9-21.700). Leaving the program is possible, and getting back in "is exceedingly rare" (JM 9-21.050).
- **Not found:** a written rule of no contact with anyone, ever. Family can come with her. Public accounts describe controlled contact (forwarded mail) rather than none. *I found no primary source on the contact rules.* "Including him" (a deputy) isn't a rule I could find either.

**Safest wording (the boss's line):** "A new name in a new state. She can't come back here, and she can't tell anyone from here where she is. Not her friends, not this island, and not you." That holds up as the program's practice and keeps the rupture. Family going with her doesn't matter here: Beth's only family is a mother in Bangor she talks to at Christmas.

## 7. The island emergency director clears an emergency landing once she has "declared the island closed"

**Claim (outline Ch 08 s2, NB8; bible STILL UNCHECKED 7):** "Since Tess declared the island closed on Day 1, the boat won't land without the emergency director clearing the beach."

**Verdict: wrong as a rule. Tess still has to know.**
- Maine law requires each municipality to have an emergency management director, appointed by the municipal officers, "to facilitate cooperation in the work of disaster mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery" (37-B M.R.S. § 782). The statute gives the director no power over landings.
- Local emergency proclamations come from a town's own civil preparedness ordinance. In one Maine example, the director "shall have the power and authority to issue a proclamation that an emergency exists," may issue regulations "restricting the movements of persons within the town," the proclamation must be filed with the town clerk within 24 hours, and it lapses after five days unless renewed (Raymond, Maine ordinance).
- A town ordinance doesn't bind a state police boat. Marine Patrol needs nobody's clearance to land.
- What is real practice (*can't confirm; working knowledge*): a rescue boat wants someone ashore on the radio who can see the beach and the sea, and on a small island that's the emergency director with the fire company's radio.

**Smallest change:**
- Ch 08 s2, the boss's line: "Marine Patrol wants somebody on the island radio who can see the beach and tell them when to come in. That's your emergency director." Beth: "That's Tess." The reason Tess must know is unchanged.
- "Declared the island closed": safest plain wording is "Tess declared an island emergency on Friday." No power should hang on it. If it's used, the five-day lapse in the example ordinance means it would need renewing by Wednesday the 18th; better left unsaid.

## 8. One cell tower, so the kid's phone pings only "Cutter Island"

**Claim (outline Ch 13 s3, N3; bible Phones):** "The man's phone pings only 'Cutter Island,' because there's one tower."

**Verdict: wrong for a modern phone.** A tower-only fix can only put a phone "in the radius of that cell tower." But a carrier "ping" can return the handset's own GPS-based location (Phase II), which the FCC requires "generally to within 50 to 300 meters" (Officer.com). Police request pings under exigent circumstances or a warrant, and the provider returns location "via GPS data or by using cell tower triangulation" (San Diego Sheriff policy). A cop or telecom reader will say one tower doesn't matter if the phone answers with GPS.

**Smallest change:** the kid keeps his phone off and turns it on for a minute to read and send. Dane, plainly: "He turns it on, texts, and turns it off. By the time the carrier asks it where it is, it's off again, and all they've got is the island's one tower." That also explains why the bait text works only on his schedule. The rest of N3 (forty shut houses, a door-to-door search in a storm) stands.

**8b. The tower on its own generator: correct.** Tower batteries "typically last between 4 to 8 hours," and towers also have "backup generators running on diesel or propane," with runtime limited by fuel (Fuel Logic). Over ten days somebody refuels it. One line, or leave it unsaid: Tess's store sells the fuel.

**Sources:** [Officer.com, cell-tower ping evidence](https://www.officer.com/investigations/forensics/article/10710588/what-can-law-enforcement-and-the-justice-system-count-on-from-cell-tower-ping-evidence) · [San Diego Sheriff, Policy 49, cell phone pings](https://apps.sdsheriff.net/PublicDocs/SB978/Law%20Enforcement%20Services%20Bureau/Field%20Operations%20Manual/POLICY%2049_Cell%20Phone%20Pings.pdf) · [Fuel Logic, cell tower backup power](https://www.fuellogic.net/cell-tower-backup-power/)

## 9. "Taken for questioning" for the lawyer at the courthouse (NB11)

**Verdict: wrong.** Police can't take a person in for questioning against his will without probable cause; that's a seizure (*Dunaway v. New York*, 442 U.S. 200 (1979)). Two deputies taking him by the arms is an arrest. Deputy marshals may arrest without a warrant for "any felony cognizable under the laws of the United States" on "reasonable grounds to believe" the person committed it (28 U.S.C. § 566(d)). The kid's sworn naming of the lawyer at 4 a.m. is plausible grounds (*can't confirm that one accomplice's word suffices; it often does*).

**Smallest change:** "two deputies arrested him at the door." Or, to keep the softer word, he goes with them on his own feet: "two deputies asked him to come with them, and he went." Drop "by the arms" in that version.

## 10. Coast Guard: icing, distress calls, "the nearest cutter is in Boston"

- **10c, "the wind doesn't, so nothing flies": wrong.** Wind is not what grounds a Jayhawk (*working knowledge*; the source above names icing, ceiling and visibility).
  - **Fix:** "The sea drops Sunday night. The icing and the low cloud don't lift till Tuesday, so nothing flies." That keeps the outline's B4 logic and avoids the contrast construction.
- **Distress calls: can't confirm, plausible.** Coast Guard stations' missions include law enforcement as well as rescue (manuscript pass). The Coast Guard may carry people for another federal agency "when so requested by proper authority" (14 U.S.C. § 701, setup §2). So the only honest reason it doesn't come is priority. Say that.
- **Watch item:** "The Marine Patrol boat is laid up with a bullet through its hull." Marine Patrol has more than one boat on the coast. Add a clause: "and Marine Patrol won't send another boat into a shooting in this sea." *(Can't confirm; a believable command call.)*

## 16. The ferry: about 100 ft, one big truck and four pickups, a two-person winter crew, and Beth's license

**Verdict: correct as carried from the v2 checks, with three watch items.**
- **Size and load: correct.** MaineDOT's 104-ft *Charles Norman Shay* carries 149 passengers and up to 8 cars, or one tractor-trailer plus four light trucks, and is under 100 gross tons (setup §6, Baird Maritime). "One big truck and four pickups" matches it exactly.
- **Beth's license: correct only if the boat is under 100 gross tons.** A 100-ton master's credential doesn't cover a 300-ton boat (setup §6). ## 17. The constable holds an arrested man in the town office until the crossing

**Claim:** Russ cuffs Ned on the float on Thursday Nov 19 and holds him in the town office back room until the crossing on Sunday night, then hands him to police at Pruett at 4 a.m. Monday.

**Verdict: can't confirm for the constable; the delay is defensible.**
- **The constable's powers.** Constables are appointed "in the same manner and with the same effect as special police officers," and the certificate says whether they may carry a weapon (30-A M.R.S. § 2673). The constable arrest-power section (30-A § 2671) didn't load. *Whether a Maine constable may make this arrest is unclear.*
- **Better: Dane makes it.** Ned's crime (helping traffic fentanyl, setting up a witness) is federal. A deputy marshal may arrest without a warrant for a federal felony on reasonable grounds (28 U.S.C. § 566(d)). Ned's confession is more than that.
- **The delay.** A person arrested without a warrant gets a judge's probable-cause finding within 48 hours, unless the government shows "a bona fide emergency or other extraordinary circumstance" (*County of Riverside v. McLaughlin*, 500 U.S. 44 (1991)). The federal rule is "without unnecessary delay" (CRS). An island cut off by a storm is about as good an extraordinary circumstance as exists. Dane reporting it to his boss on the day makes it defensible on the page.

**Smallest change:** Ch 12 s2: Dane says the arrest out loud ("Ned, you're under arrest"), Russ cuffs him on Dane's word, and Dane tells his boss on the phone that afternoon. Ch 13 can carry it in the boss's first line. Russ guarding the back room stays. Ned in cuffs aboard for the crossing: he wears a life jacket (manuscript pass §9).
