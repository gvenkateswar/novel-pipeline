The generator roared out back of Marr's. Tess held a dish towel of snow to her mouth with one hand and squared cartons in the cigarette rack with the other, the menthols down at the end where they belonged.

"Whitcomb came through that door at a run," Tess said into the towel. "Three tries to put Arsenault on the floor. Three."

Through the front window, Lyle Arsenault sat in Russ's truck at the dock head under the dome light, his hands behind him.

Ned sat on the stool, the clean collar from supper buttoned to the top, the store's set on the shelf behind his head still on nine. Beth's palms were raw from the buoy line, and she laid them on the cold wood of the counter.

"Lip's nothing," Tess said. "Rack's a disgrace."

"I heard you on nine," Beth said. "Tell me how."

"Wind's gone round northwest," Ned said. "She'll lie quiet now."

"Ned," Beth said.

He looked at his hands on his knees. "I come up after supper for the phone. It charges under the hook, you know that. I stood out back where it gets its bar, holding it up high toward the co-op, and I was on to Sector about the inspector, whether he's on the eight o'clock boat if the glass holds, and he is, and I looked down the harbor and there's a skiff going along under the dragger with no light on her, so I came in and keyed nine. That's all it was."

"Where's your cop," Beth said.

Ned stopped.

"The boy said it on my deck," Beth said. "On the air I was by myself. You sent Russ to the pier. You knew the marshal was aboard."

"Lyle Arsenault came aboard that first Thursday," Ned said, "with nine thousand in hundreds. He said if you went to Portland, I went with you. I cut the tag off the store bag. I put the money in your envelope, over your slip, and I sealed the bag again with a red one out of my drawer."

"Yellow," Tess said into the towel. "Mine are yellow, Haskell. Nineteen years yellow, eleven dollars the hundred from the supply house. I bagged that envelope myself. A paycheck and a slip."

So the teller had written down the store's bag, and the red on it was Ned's.

"Ashby," Beth said.

"He said a store like hers lives on the boat. So I gave them Ashby."

Tess's hands stopped on the rack. A carton of menthols stood half out of its slot, and she didn't push it home.

"The fish house I knew," Ned said. "I've seen you row shingles out to it every fall. That one I kept."

"Tonight," Beth said.

"Save it for Whitcomb, Haskell," Tess said. "Whitcomb'll want it in order."

"Tonight I gave them the whole boat," Ned said. "Russ on the pier in the light. Your marshal aboard. Land side watched, so they'd come by water, under the floods, where you'd see them first. You wanted a trap. I gave them one."

"You gave them the marshal," Beth said. "They put the boot on his arm."

"Arsenault wanted night fuel," Tess said. "Ten-thirty, cash, twice the pump. I came down and lit the float for him. I lit it, Beth."

"Then I heard Lyle out front on his own phone," Ned said. "Send the boy with the gas, he said. I never thought of gas. I went in the back and keyed nine, and when Tess came through the curtain he put her into the counter."

"Corner of the counter," Tess said. "Right there. Eight dollars of Life Savers on the floor."

Ned turned his head toward the window. "Stars out over the mainland," he said. "Every New Year's she tells me ask me sober."

"Haskell," Tess said. "Don't you."

"I laid that ham in to ask her at Thanksgiving," Ned said. "I asked her tonight at supper instead. Sober."

Tess took the towel away. Her lip was split and swelling. She looked at Ned for a long time. Then she went through the curtain into her office without turning her head toward Beth, and behind it the safe's dial began to turn.

The bell over the door rang. Russ came in and took his cap off.

Before Russ could open his mouth, Ned held his wrists out together across the counter where he had bought his coffee since Beth was in diapers. Russ's hand went to his belt. Behind the curtain the dial went round, and stopped, and went round again.
