# Chapter One: Night Freight

Beth went down the trucks with her gloves off.

Sleet came flat across the car deck at twenty to nine, orange where it crossed under the floods, and it found the gap between her cuffs and her wrists every time she bent. She read the binders by feel. Handle down. Chain hard. The D-ring packed with slush, which she knocked clear with her thumb. The propane truck, then the co-op's bait truck, stinking of herring even in this, then the plumber's van. On the van's aft chain the binder gave a quarter turn under her fingers that it had no business giving, and she had it taken up before she'd thought about it.

The dealer's reefer came up the ramp last. The unit on its nose was dark.

She stood with the next chain in her hands and watched it roll aboard. Every Thursday she could remember, that unit had droned the whole crossing, loud enough that Ned turned the radio up to talk over it. Tonight the display was black and the reefer came on as quiet as a hearse.

"Bethie." Ned was at the wheelhouse rail above her, ice in his beard. "I'll work it short. Go home."

Eleven years, and he had never once sent her home.

She walked to the next D-ring and knelt to it.

The driver set his brake and stayed in his cab with the dome light on. She chained the trailer's forward corners, then the aft, and on her way between them she put her hand on the box. Aluminum, wet, slick. Not cold. A live load in November, and the box was the temperature of the night and no colder, and her hand stayed on it longer than the chain needed.

At the trailer's side door a man in a black hood was stacking the last crates in, face down, quick. A sea came under the ramp and the boat lifted. A crate in the doorway tipped. The band let go with a twang and the lid came up, and there were lobsters, green-black and slow in their bands, and under the top layer, where the lift had shifted them, bricks wrapped tight in gray tape.

The hooded man looked at her. Up in the cab, the driver looked at her. Nobody moved for the length of the next sea.

Beth put the lid down with both hands and leaned on it and ran the band back round. "Lid's sprung. You'll lose your count."

She finished the binder. Then she went aft the way her feet always went: a hand down the stern chain across the transom to feel it bar-tight, a kick at the ice on the ring buoy's bracket, past the quarter ladder with its rungs gone white, and up the outside ladder to the wheelhouse, where she stopped on the top rung with her shoulder against the breaker box on the aft bulkhead and listened. Diesel through her soles. The sea booming under the ramp. Her own breath.

Below her, the hooded man came out from behind the reefer and went down the ramp onto her island, before Ned brought it up. He favored the left leg. The heel dragged a line through the slush on the slip, and she watched the line go all the way up into the dark.

The driver's dome light burned in the cab under the wheelhouse windows the whole way over. Ned, with his eyes on the black water, asked about the lid.

"Strapped it."

He didn't ask again. At Pruett the reefer went off the ramp first, and she photographed its plate with the phone held low against her bibs, and had it put away before the taillights reached the road.

They came back into Cutter near the top of the tide. The fuel float at Marr's was lit, at an hour it never was. Ned met her at the foot of the ladder with both bags under his arm, the fare bag and the store's, Tess's yellow tag bright on the one in the floods.

"Go home," he said. "I'll lock up."

He went up slow on the bad hip. At the top he turned and lifted the hand with the bags in it, the way he always did.

Her kitchen was dark and she left it dark. She sat at the table in her coat with the phone on the wall and its twelve-foot cord, which would reach the table if she pulled it. She didn't pull it. Calling meant saying her name to a stranger and listening to it go down on paper, and she had watched a man in a good coat write her father down once, in an afternoon, in blue pen.

Past midnight she dialed.

"Beth Calloway. Deckhand on the Cutter Island ferry. Calling about a truck." She told it the way she'd tell Ned a binder had slipped: the dark unit, the warm box, the crate, the tape. "I have the plate." She read it off her phone, letter by letter, while the trooper wrote.

"You stay home tomorrow, Ms. Calloway. Don't go to work."

She hung up. Somewhere on the mainland her name was going onto a form, and the form would go to somebody, and somebody would come looking for the woman on it. She took her father's alarm clock off the shelf, wound it till the key stopped, and set it for the first boat.

* * *

The bow line had frozen stiff in the night, and the ice cracked off it in plates in her bare hands as she paid it out.

Twenty to eight, and Pruett Harbor hadn't decided it was morning. The sodium lights over the ramp were still on in the overcast, and the wind was coming on from the east in long cold pushes that smelled of snow. Behind her in the wheelhouse Ned had the heater going. In front of her, a man was coming down the ramp in new boots.

She heard him first. A sole that had never met ice, and then the skid on the iced plate at the foot of the ramp, and his arm going out for a rail that wasn't there.

She didn't look up from the bollard. "Stand on the yellow. There's grit in the paint. Those boots are for a different ocean."

"They're for a courtroom."

He stepped onto the yellow. She glanced once, from the line to his boots and back. "Hundred and ninety. The place on Route One that sells fudge."

He looked down at his feet as if they'd told on him. He didn't say anything at all, and she liked that more than she meant to, a man who could take one in the teeth without swinging back.

Then she looked at the rest of him. Dark gray jacket, zipped to the throat. Short dark hair going gray at the sides, wet already. Clean-shaven, which wouldn't last out here. He was looking at her hands on the line before he looked at her face, and when he got to her face he stayed there. Men from the mainland didn't look at her that long on a ramp. They looked at the boat, or their watches, or past her for whoever was in charge. She let him, and paid out line, and kept her face where it was.

"Ms. Calloway." Very polite. "Dane Reyes. Marshals Service. You were told not to work."

She paid out another fathom.

"Wade Pelletier spent the night in custody in Portland," he said, "which makes you the case, and the case was asked, nicely, by a state trooper, to stay in her house."

The courier's van backed down to the head of the ramp and the courier lifted one hand out the window. Beth went up the deck, came back with the pouch, and passed it down. Reyes watched it go. His eyes went with the pouch from her hands to the courier's and stayed on it until the van door shut. She had been looked over on this ramp by eleven years of men and had never once come second to a canvas bag.

"Pouch is zipped and going to the bank. You can stop watching it. I'm the one casting off."

That brought him back. Brown eyes, tired at the corners. "I watch everything that leaves your hands. It's a habit. I'll try to look at you more."

He did. She felt it on the side of her face while she worked, steady, unhurried, the way a man looks at weather he means to go out in, and she made the line do a thing it didn't need to.

"Kenny's puking and there's no relief on a storm Friday." She put a turn on the bollard. "Master and one deck. That's the certificate. Without me she doesn't leave the slip."

He took that in. She watched him take it in, and file it, and not like where it went.

"That's a good point," he said. "I'm going to go around it. Okay. Here's how this goes. You hand the gentleman anything else that's his. You walk up this ramp with me. You get in the gray car at the top, which has a heater, and which I'll point out is not moving."

"You're not arresting me."

"I'm not arresting you."

"Then I'm working." She coiled slack. "I'll work her in and the two o'clock back, and I'm yours on the ramp at quarter to three."

"No."

His phone buzzed in his chest pocket. He took it out, read it, and turned the screen away from her with his thumb, a small neat movement, as if he did it in his sleep.

"Am I keeping you?"

He put the phone away. For a second his hand stayed on the pocket. Then he came a step closer down the yellow, close enough that she could see the sleet melting on his lashes and smell mainland coffee on him, the kind that came with a lid, and he said it slowly, every word set down like a box he didn't want to drop. "The last witness who knew her own town better than I did is buried in it."

The wind went over them both.

She had a dozen things for that, and none of them were any good. She wanted him to say something else, something stupid about the boots, so she could go back to not liking him. He didn't. He stood there in the wrong boots and waited for her to be sensible. Nobody had waited on her like that since her father, and her father had mostly been waiting for the same thing.

From the wheelhouse Ned gave one short blast.

Beth lifted the eye off the bollard. The bow ramp groaned and started up under her, and she stepped back with it, and the gap opened between the steel and the slip, a hand, a foot, a stride of black water with the ice turning in it. A gust set them toward the dolphin and she felt Ned's correction come through the deck a breath before he made it.

Reyes jumped.

He jumped badly, all knees and no plan, in those boots, and they hit the lip of the rising ramp and went straight out from under him. She had the front of his jacket in both fists before his knee came down, and she leaned back against the whole weight of him and the slope of the steel and hauled, and he came over the lip and down onto one knee on her deck, and his hand closed over her bare wrist and held.

Neither of them let go. She could feel the sea working under the ramp through the soles of her boots, and the cold of his hand, colder than hers, wrapped all the way round the bones of her wrist like he had measured it, and the water widening behind him where Pruett had been, and his breath going in and out against her knuckles, and she was still holding on. She had him. She'd had him for a while now. Somewhere in there it had turned into two people on a cold deck holding each other by the clothes, and she couldn't have said which of them was supposed to move first.

She let go first.

"You're welcome."

"I didn't say anything."

"I noticed."

* * *

She coiled the bow line in the lee of the deckhouse, working the ice out of it a fathom at a time, and he stood beside her and dripped. His right knee was soaked dark where he'd gone down on her deck. Spray came over the bulwark in sheets.

"For the record, I jumped. I'd like that on the record, since you seem to be the one keeping it."

She went on coiling.

"I'd also like it noted that you caught me, which was decent of you, and that I'd have done the same for you, in better boots."

"You'd have gone in."

"Probably. Decently, though."

Her right wrist still knew where his fingers had gone round it. She kept that hand on the line.

Off the ledges a lobster boat was hauling in the slop, her stern lifting and dropping, a man in orange bent over the rail. Reyes watched her with his whole face. Beth had seen boys look at a girl like that across the church at Christmas.

"What's a Webbers Cove thirty-four with a rebuilt Cat worth?"

"To who?"

"To me."

"Less than you're paying."

That got the corner of his mouth. "I was supposed to look at one this afternoon."

"Bank took my father's boat," she said. "Man from away bought her. Made her a picnic boat." She laid the next coil down. "You want a working boat to sleep on."

"I want a boat I can't be called off of." He said it to the lobster boat, and quieter than anything he'd said on the ramp.

Russ was the *Second Wind* and four hundred traps. Ned was the *North Reach*. The summer man was a varnished picnic boat with her father's lines under the paint. She had a hull for everyone she knew, and she knew everyone she'd ever have reason to, and a man with no boat was from away, and there was no slot in her for him. She had him in one anyway. She'd put him at the rail of that boat in the slop, knee wet, sleeves shoved up, hauling, and she'd been standing here a while getting his hands right. She looked down at the line and made the next coil too tight.

"So," he said. "The two o'clock. Your deal."

"Last boat today."

"Then I'll be very interested in it."

His hand went inside his jacket and came out with a folded paper, dry, the only dry thing on the deck. He held it out and waited until she took it.

"I can't put you in the car," he said. "This I can do."

She opened it with her thumb. The court at the top, in capitals. And under it, typed, all three of them: *Elizabeth Ann Calloway*.

She turned it to the light. It didn't give under her thumb the way a binder gave. There was nothing in it she could feel. He'd had it on him the whole time, at the bollard, on the ramp, with her fists knotted in his jacket an inch from it, and he had let her haul him aboard first. He was watching her hands again.

Somebody had sat at a desk in a warm room and spelled her right. She folded it once along the crease he'd made, and put it in her bib pocket, where it would get wet.

* * *

Tess was on the slip with the store's handcart when they came in, waiting on her freight. She looked at the marshal's boots before she looked at his face.

"Calloway. Heard you had a man on the eight. Ferry's quicker than the internet."

"He came aboard late."

"I'll bet he did." Tess turned the look up to his face. "Morning, Marshal. Coffee's on the urn. Free to islanders. Two dollars to you."

"That seems fair," he said, and Beth watched Tess price him, hands, jacket, boots again, and come out somewhere she'd talk about later.

While Tess talked, Beth watched every man on the slip walk: the plumber come for his van, the co-op boys at the bait truck, an old man down for nothing but the news. Not one of them dragged a heel.

They went up the Harbor Road into the wind, past the store and the church, the road frozen in ruts. She walked slower than she wanted to so the boots could keep up. He saw her do it and let her. Her father's house sat up on its knoll with its front to the road and every window dark, the way she'd left it. Under the open sky his phone buzzed. He read it and turned the screen from her. She didn't ask. She took him in by the front door.

In the kitchen she pinned the subpoena under the barometer and tapped the glass. The needle dropped.

"It won't run. Pack if you want. You'll be unpacking by two."

"Dollar says it runs."

"I don't bet on what I can see."

She packed anyway, to make the point, a duffel on the kitchen table, her father's sweater folded on top. He stood at the window with his hands in his pockets and looked down at the harbor, where the wind was laying the moorings over one by one.

"My mother would like this kitchen," he told the window. "She'd also have wiped the counter by now. I'm making no comment about the counter."

"Counter's clean."

"It is. I said nothing."

He hadn't taken his jacket off. He stood on her floor in the wrong boots with his back to her and his shoulders a little up against the cold of the glass, and every so often he turned his head to watch her hands, and she let him catch her watching him back once, and then she looked at the duffel. She packed slower than she needed to. She folded the sweater twice.

At a quarter past one the handheld on the counter, which she'd turned to sixteen, cleared its throat.

*"All stations, all stations, this is the ferry North Reach. The fourteen hundred departure is cancelled. Next departure to be announced. North Reach standing by one-six and one-three."*

It was Ned's radio voice, clipped and formal, a different man from the one who'd sent her home.

Reyes didn't turn from the window. "There goes my boat."

"It was never your boat."
