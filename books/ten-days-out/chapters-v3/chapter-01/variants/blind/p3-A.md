# Chapter One: Night Freight

At eight-forty the sleet came flat across the car deck out of the east, orange where it crossed the floods and black where it didn't, and Beth Calloway went down the trucks with her gloves off.

Gloves were for carrying things. Anything that mattered she did by hand, because the floods turned every chain the same color and a hand didn't care what color a chain was. Handle down. Chain bar-tight. Hook seated all the way in the D-ring, where it couldn't walk out on the first sea. The propane truck was right. The co-op's bait truck was right, and smelled the way it always smelled, like a decision somebody had made about herring in August. The plumber's van had been chained by the plumber, who chained the way he billed, generously and with gaps. The binder on its port rear gave under her fingers, a quarter turn it had no business giving, and she had it up and locked before she'd made up her mind to.

The dealer's reefer came down the slip last, the way it came every Thursday, and rolled onto the plates with its trailer lights smearing in the wet.

It came aboard quiet.

Every Thursday since the summer before last, that unit had droned the whole way across, seven miles of one flat note off the nose of the box, a noise she'd stopped hearing the way you stop hearing your own refrigerator. Live lobster. You ran the unit or you sold chowder. Tonight the little display on the front of the box was dark.

"Bethie." Ned was at the wheelhouse rail above her, big in his green jacket, sleet in his beard. He was watching the reefer. "I'll work it short. Go home."

In eleven years Ned Haskell had sent her for coffee, for the grease gun, below for a fender, and once, when she was nine and riding over for the day, up the slip for a bucket of steam, which she had never forgiven. He had never sent her home.

She walked to the next D-ring.

She chained the reefer's rear axle first, because it sat nearest the wind, and when the binder was down she put her bare hand on the side of the box to steady herself as the boat rolled. The aluminum was wet, and under her hand it was the same temperature as the sleet. Nothing in that box was keeping anything cold.

The side door of the box stood open a foot. A man in a black hood was stacking the last crates through it with his back to her. Under the floods his hood went orange and his hands went orange and the crates went orange. A sea came in round the end of the slip and the boat lifted. The top crate in the doorway tipped. Its band went with a crack like a hand slapped on a table, and the lid came up.

Lobsters, dark and stirring in the weed.

Under the top layer, plastic. Bricks of it, taped, packed tight as shingles.

She looked up. The driver was looking at her out of his cab, a heavy face lit green by the dash. The hooded man had turned in the door. Neither of them moved.

The boat came down off the sea. Diesel through her boot soles. Sleet ticking on the box.

She put the lid down with both hands and pulled the band over it and pressed the clip home. "Lid's sprung. You'll lose your count."

Nobody answered. She finished the binder. Then she went aft, because aft was the job, and checked the stern chain across the transom with one hand going by and knocked the ice off the ring buoy's bracket with the heel of the other and passed the quarter ladder without looking down it, the way she'd done it ten thousand nights, so that anyone watching would see a woman doing what she always did.

Up the outside ladder. Steel, iced. The gray box of the floods' breakers on the wheelhouse's aft bulkhead went by her shoulder. At the top she held the rail and listened. Ramp hydraulics. Wind. A chain shifting forward and settling.

Below her the hooded man stepped off the bow ramp onto the slip. His left leg moved half a beat behind the rest of him, the heel dragging a line in the slush. He went up the slip into the dark of her island, and she watched the limp all the way to the top, and then the ramp came up and took him away.

All the way over, the dome light burned in the dealer's cab right under the wheelhouse windows, and the heavy face in it never once looked at the water.

"Lid all right?" Ned said, somewhere past the ledges.

"Strapped it."

At Pruett the reefer drove off up the ramp under the sodium lights. She stood at the bow with her phone in her wet hand and took one picture of its plate.

They came back into Cutter near the top of the tide. The fuel float off Tess's dock was lit, both pumps and the pole light, at an hour it was never lit, the glow lying on the black water like something spilled. Ned came down the ladder with both bags in one fist, the fare bag and the store's, Tess's yellow tag bright under the floods.

"Go home," he said. "I'll lock up."

He went back up the outside ladder, slow with the hip. At the top he turned and lifted the hand with the bags in it.

Her kitchen was dark and she left it dark. She sat at the table in her bibs with the phone on its twelve-foot cord in her lap and the float still lit in the window, and she didn't dial. Dialing meant somebody wrote her down. The last time anybody wrote a Calloway down it was a man from the bank with a good pen, and she had carried his folder out to his truck for him because her father asked.

Past midnight she dialed.

"Beth Calloway. Cutter Island. I'm deck on the ferry." She gave it the way she'd give Ned a bad weld. The unit dark. The box warm. The lid, and what was under the lid. "Pills, I'd say. Wrapped. I've got his plate." She read it off her phone, twice, slow.

She could hear the trooper's pen. Then: "You stay home tomorrow, Ms. Calloway. Don't go to work."

She hung up. Her father's alarm clock sat on the shelf over the stove, the old wind-up with the dented bell. She took it down and wound it until it wouldn't wind, and set it for the first boat.

\* \* \*

The bow line had frozen overnight into something closer to a stick, and at seven-forty on the Pruett Harbor ramp Beth paid it out bare and let the ice crack off it in plates. Gray morning. The wind backing into the east and getting ideas. The sodium lights over the ramp were still burning, as if nobody in Pruett had noticed it was day, which was fair, since it barely was.

A man came down the ramp in new boots, hit the iced plate at the bottom, and went a yard further than he'd planned.

She didn't look up from the cleat. "Stand on the yellow. There's grit in the paint. Those boots are for a different ocean."

"They're for a courtroom."

"Hundred and ninety. Place on Route 1 with the fudge."

He looked down at the boots. He looked at her. He opened his mouth, and whatever was in it wasn't good enough, and he shut it again. She took a turn off the cleat and let him stand there on the yellow and think about the fudge.

"Ms. Calloway." Slow and polite, every syllable signed for. "Deputy Reyes, Marshals Service. You were told not to work."

She had him in her head before he finished. *Ms. Calloway*, slow, like a man ordering off a menu in a language he'd paid to learn. Other people's voices were a thing she did, alone, at the sink, on the dark end of the night run. Tess on the storm net. Russ saying "somebody." The summer woman who called the ferry "the little boat" as if it might hear and be hurt. Nobody off the island knew. She'd have this one down by lunch.

"Wade Pelletier has been in custody since before it got light," he said, "which makes you the case."

The courier came down the ramp from his van with his hand already out. She handed the pouch down to him. The marshal watched it go from her hands to the courier's and all the way up the ramp, and never once looked at her, and she minded that more than the boots.

"Pouch is zipped and going to the bank. You can stop watching it. I'm the one casting off."

He looked at her then. That was worse. He had a face built for waiting in parked cars, shaved too close for the weather, and his eyes went to her hands before they came up to the rest of her, as if somebody had told him once to watch the hands and he was a man who did what he was told.

"Kenny's puking," she said, "and there's no relief on a storm Friday. Master and one deck. That's the certificate. Without me she doesn't leave the slip."

"Okay." He put his hands in his jacket pockets, like a man who'd decided to be patient and wanted credit for it. "Here's how this goes. You've handed the gentleman his pouch, thank you. Now you walk up this ramp with me, carefully, because of the paint. You get into the gray car at the top, which is warm, and I drive you somewhere else that's warm, where a second deputy meets us, who would be here already except he's behind a plow on Route 1 doing about nine miles an hour."

"You're not arresting me."

"I'm not arresting you."

"Then I'm working." She coiled slack onto the ramp. "I'll work her in and the two o'clock back, and I'm yours on the ramp at quarter to three."

"No. The two o'clock is printed on a sign at the top of this ramp, which means anybody who'd like to know where you'll be this afternoon can read."

His phone buzzed. He took it out and read it and turned the screen in toward his chest, the way you'd shade a match.

"Am I keeping you?"

He put the phone away. He didn't hurry it. When he spoke again he'd gone slower still, and quieter, so that she had to stop coiling to get it.

"The last witness who knew her own town better than I did is buried in it."

She looked at the water. The tide was making against the pilings, black and thick with cold. She had nothing for that, so she gave it nothing.

Ned gave one short blast from the wheelhouse.

Beth lifted the eye off the bollard and let it go.

The bow ramp started up under her with a groan of hydraulics, and the gap opened between its lip and the shore, a stride of gray water. The gust came round the end of the breakwater the way it always did, ten seconds of it, and set them toward the north dolphin, and she knew the touch of starboard Ned would give her before he gave it, and he gave it.

She watched him look at the gap and do the sum, the boots and the ice and the water, and come out on the wrong side of it.

The marshal jumped.

He did it badly, the way a man does it when he's decided before his boots have, and the boots hit the lip of the rising ramp and kept going. She had the front of his jacket in both fists before she thought about it and hauled him over the lip onto the steel.

He came down on one knee on her deck with her fists in his jacket, and his hand came up and closed over her bare wrist, and stayed there. His hand was cold, and then it was less cold. The sea went hollow under the ramp, boom and suck, and behind him the gap was a stride and then two and then the whole gray harbor going away, and she had him, she had him the way you'd have a fender or a dog gone overboard, by the scruff, by whatever came to hand, and she was still holding on when there was no reason left in the world to be holding on, and so was he, and she only knew how long it had been because her knuckles had started to ache.

She let go first.

"You're welcome."

"I didn't say anything."

"I noticed."

\* \* \*

Past the ledges the run home was all slop, short steep seas that came over the starboard bulwark in sheets and ran aft along the plates. Beth coiled the bow line in the lee of the deckhouse, working the ice out of it a turn at a time, and the marshal stood beside her and dripped. His left knee was black with wet where he'd gone down on her deck.

"For the record, I jumped," he said. "I'd like that on the record, since you seem to be the one keeping it."

She went on coiling.

Off the ledges a lobster boat was hauling in the slop, white hull, stern to the sea, a man in orange gaffing a buoy. The marshal watched it with his whole face, mouth a little open, the way a boy watches a fire truck go by.

"What's a Webbers Cove thirty-four with a rebuilt Cat worth?"

"To who?"

"To me."

"Less than you're paying."

He laughed once, through his nose, and didn't argue, which she hadn't expected. "I'm looking at one this afternoon. I was. I am."

"Bank took my father's," she said. "Man from away bought her. Teak rails now, and a little flag." She took another turn. "You want a working boat to sleep on."

"I want a boat I can't be called off of."

She coiled. Russ was the *Second Wind* and four hundred traps and a radio voice like a man reading a will. Ned was the *North Reach*, and had been since before she could walk, and you didn't think of one without the other coming along. The summer man was the picnic boat with the little flag, and she had stopped looking when it went by. Everybody she knew sat somewhere in a boat. A man with no boat was from away, and from away was weather: you watched it, you dressed for it, you let it pass. This one had no boat and no slot, and she was standing in the spray with forty feet of iced line in her hands fitting him to that lobster boat off the ledge anyway, his hands on the gaff, his wet knee braced on the rail, those boots, and she set the coil down on the plates harder than it needed.

"The two o'clock," he said. "Your deal. I'd like to hold you to it."

"Last boat today."

"Then I'll be very interested in it."

He reached inside his jacket. What came out was folded in thirds and dry, the only dry thing on the deck, and he held it out to her, and her hand went out and took it before the rest of her had been consulted.

The court across the top. Under it, typed, her name. *Elizabeth Ann Calloway.* All of it, the Ann too.

He couldn't make her get in the car. This could.

She stood in the spray and looked at herself in type. A sea came over the bulwark and spotted the paper. She folded it once, along the crease he'd made, and put it in her bib pocket, where it would get wet.

\* \* \*

Tess was on the slip when they landed, red vest, glasses pushed up in her hair, a hand truck for the store's freight. She looked at the marshal's boots first and got to his face a good while later.

"Calloway." Net-control voice, pitched for the far end of the harbor. "Heard you had a man on the eight. Ferry's quicker than the internet."

"Tess."

"Marshal." Tess gave him a nod like a price tag.

"Ma'am."

"Don't ma'am me. I charge extra for it."

While Tess talked, about the propane and the bait and what the wind meant to do by dark, Beth watched every man on the slip walk. The propane driver. The Dyer boy with the bait totes. A caretaker from the north point with a carton of light bulbs. Not one of them dragged a heel.

They went up the Harbor Road under a sky the color of a wet sail. Halfway up the knoll his phone buzzed. He read it and turned the screen in against his jacket, and she didn't ask. They went into the house by the front door.

In the kitchen she slid the subpoena under the barometer by the door, where it held, and tapped the glass with one knuckle. The needle dropped. It went on dropping.

"It won't run. Pack if you want. You'll be unpacking by two."

"Dollar says it runs."

"I don't bet on what I can see."

She packed anyway, to make the point: her father's other sweater, long johns, a toothbrush, a duffel on the kitchen table. He stood at the window over the sink with his hands in his pockets and looked down at the harbor, at the boats swinging to the east on their moorings, as if one of them might explain itself. He told the window the forecast she'd heard before light and the marine forecast she'd heard after it, and asked the window whether forecasts out here were considered advisory.

"Wind's the forecast," she said.

Twice he took the phone out and put it back without reading it. She watched his hands do that, and then she watched the harbor, because the harbor was her business. The wind came round the house and leaned on it.

At a quarter past one the handheld on the counter, which she'd turned to one-six, cleared its throat. It was Ned's radio voice, the one with all the creases ironed out of it, a different man from the one who brought coffee by way of saying sorry.

"All stations, all stations, this is the ferry *North Reach*. The fourteen hundred departure is cancelled. Next departure to be announced. *North Reach* standing by one-six and one-three."

The marshal didn't turn from the window.

"There goes my boat."

"It was never your boat."
