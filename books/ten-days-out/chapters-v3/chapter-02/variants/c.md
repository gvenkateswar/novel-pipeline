# Chapter Two: The Weather Hold

Reyes went out through the mudroom with his right hand on his hip, and the kitchen door swung shut behind him. She heard the outer door, and his boots on the back step, and then nothing for a while but the wind.

Then the kitchen door shook on its latch, iron on iron, and stopped, and shook again.

He was trying to come back into her kitchen politely, and then less politely, and the old iron wasn't having either. Beth lifted the thumb-piece hard and put her shoulder to the boards, and the door went open on him.

He stood in the mudroom with snow in his hair. Behind him the outer door stood open on the weather.

"Lift and shove."

He shut the door between them and tried it. A clank, a rattle, the whole door bucking in its frame. Then he lifted and shoved, and it came, loud. The third time it opened without a sound, and he stood in it with the snow going to water in his hair. There was gray over his ears that the wet made hard to find. She stood there finding it.

"Nobody who's been in this house makes the noise you made first."

She went past him and out onto the step, into a wind making from the east with the first of the snow in it. The path dropped away steep to the Harbor Road and the store. Below the store the fuel float was bare.

She went along the moorings with her eyes and found the skiff past the last of them: one man at the oars, his face to her hill, pulling long and even for the outer harbor. He rowed like somebody who had learned before he could walk. He never once looked over his shoulder to see where he was going.

A squall came down across the water in a gray wall with snow inside it and took the outer moorings, and then the skiff, two lengths short of any boat. She stood in the cold and looked at the place where he had been. The moorings came back out of the snow one at a time, a pot buoy, a float, a lobster boat swinging to her chain, and he didn't come back with them.

Reyes said nothing beside her. His eyes were on the same place.

He made the call there on the step under open sky, with the harbor at his back. She went in. Through the kitchen window she watched his mouth and couldn't hear a word of it, and she put a stick in the stove it didn't need.

He came in with snow on his shoulders. "Portland knows about your man with the heel now. This morning they didn't." He let her hear that. "And the grand jury sits Monday. It's the last sitting before Pelletier walks, and they'd like you in front of it."

She looked once at the subpoena under the barometer.

"If there's a boat that would take you across before then, I'd like to hear about it."

"Nothing's crossing in this."

"Somebody's rowing in it."

"Not across."

"Then tonight I want one way in from the land, a door that locks, and somebody awake behind it." He didn't say where. He waited for her to.

She looked down at the *North Reach* in the slip, going white along her rails. One way aboard from the shore. A wheelhouse door that locked. Ned, who never slept in weather.

"Boat."

He looked where she was looking for a while, at the slip and then past it, out to where the squall had gone through.

"The man in the skiff rows like he was born in one," he said. "Who out here rows like that?"

* * *

Out on the visitors' mooring the dragger's deck lights came on all at once, stem to stern, and nobody came out on deck under them.

Reyes had stopped on the granite pier beside the slip to watch her. He was still watching when the handheld on Beth's coat opened up, and she thumbed it off nine to the net.

*"Cutter storm net, this is Marr's, net control. Roll call by house. Calloway."*

Beth keyed it. "Calloway. Aboard the *North Reach*."

*"Copy, Calloway, aboard. What do I put your marshal down as?"*

She looked at him. He took a moment over it, as if it cost him something.

"Dane," he said.

She keyed. "Dane."

*"I'll put down Marshal."* Tess didn't stop for breath. *"Candles a dollar apiece, and I'm not haggling on the air. Bread's gone. Don't ask."*

Then, in the same bright voice, as if it were bread:

*"Margaret Ann, Margaret Ann, this is Marr's. Float'll be lit at ten if you want fuel tonight."*

The handheld hissed. Nothing moved on the dragger. Then the speaker gave two short clicks, somebody's key pressed and let go twice, and that was all.

Dane was looking at the radio on her coat. "Does she do that for everybody?"

"No."

She turned the handheld back to nine, where it lived, and went aboard.

The tide came on all evening on top of the storm. Beth went forward down the one lane of steel between the two trucks chained there for the two o'clock that never ran. Over the bow hung the bridge: the steel ramp the cars drove down from the shore onto the *North Reach*, its sea end hung from two towers. Every sea shoved the bow up under it, and it ground on its pilings over her head, a long iron groan and a knock, and again.

She wanted the bow lines doubled before the top of the tide, by her own hands. Dane had asked her twice to come in, and now he was a stride behind her in the snow.

She had the spare line in her fist when the left tower screamed, a shriek she heard in her back teeth, and a cable came across the bow at head height, whipping, alive.

He hit her from behind and took her down flat under the steel rail along the bow, and on the way down her fist went into his collar and closed.

Then it came to her in order. The steel, so cold against her cheek it burned. Then him, the whole length of him across her back, his arm over her head. Then his voice at her ear, low.

"That was the cable. It went over us and over the bait truck, and now it's lying by your boots doing nothing. I'm on your back. The left tower's leaning out over the water, and I'm watching it so you don't have to. Your rope's under my hand. Line. Your line's under my hand, and it's staying there."

She gave him nothing. Under his voice she heard the cable's end rattle along the deck and go quiet; the bridge groaning; a sea booming hollow under the bow; a hum up in the towers, high and thin, from whatever was still holding.

The cable lay still. A sea lifted the two of them and set them down. Her fist was in his collar. She found it there, closed hard, and left it for one more sea.

Then the bridge came down.

It folded down onto the bow like a dropped drawbridge, with a sound she took through the deck into her ribs, and its far end went into the water. Beth let out all the air she had. Her shoulders came down from where they'd been since the night freight. Here it was at last, the thing that had been coming, and it was only steel.

Then she was up. The *North Reach* had to come off the slip before that wreck ground through her bow. Beth threw the bow line across to the granite pier, and the eye dropped over an iron post on the first throw. Dane had the tail round the cleat and was taking his turns the wrong way.

"Other way. Unless you want to go with it."

He took them the other way, and the line came taut and held. Ned backed the boat off with the wreck scraping down her bow and laid her alongside the pier as if it were June.

The power went in the same surge. Every light along the Harbor Road went out at once, and out on her mooring the dragger didn't so much as blink.

Ned came aft with a flashlight, wet to the shins. "Water's coming in up there." He put the light on the bow, bent under the wreck. "Well. That's the bridge."

"Does it do that a lot?" Dane said.

"Just the once."

"When does it run again?"

"She's not carrying anybody till somebody from the Coast Guard has his hand on that." Ned would call it in tonight, by the book. "Ten days, if the state gets a barge out."

Dane laid a hand on the rail. "At least it's steel."

"She," Beth said.

"Steel doesn't burn," Ned said. "Everything in it does."

Dane walked aft to where the deck ran out low and open to the black water, with nothing across it but a chain. A sea slopped in round his boots and drained away.

"It's holed at the front, and it's got no back door," he said. "And half the island just heard you say aboard. So tonight you're not." Then, to the chain: "Anyone with a skiff."

"Nobody's coming over that stern in this," Ned said.

"I believe you, and I'm grateful, and she's still not sleeping here." He said it politely, and went up the aluminum gangway to the pier, its wheels grinding on the stone each time the boat surged, and on to the road end, out of earshot.

"Where'll you be, Bethie?"

"Home. Don't tell anybody."

"Nobody but me and God." He tapped the handheld on her coat. "You need me, nine. Like your dad."

He went forward to his pumps. Halfway there, in the dark between the trucks, he turned round and nodded to her once, and went on.

From the road end Dane whistled, low, two notes, and she was already on the gangway with its wheels grinding under her.

* * *

They went home by the shore with no lamp, her first, because it was her shore. Rockweed under the snow, and the knee-high ledge below the church that her feet stepped over without asking her, and that she heard him find behind her with his shin.

The back steps were iced, so she thumbed the flashlight on, and they came up out of the dark glazed and gray. She was most of the way up before he spoke.

"Light."

She killed it. In the dark she crossed the mudroom, found the inner latch under her thumb, lifted it and shoved, and the kitchen door went open without a sound.

The mudroom's outer door knocked against its latch in every gust. A hollow knock, then nothing, then the knock.

They sat on the kitchen floor with their backs to the chimney, the one wall in the house a bullet wouldn't go through, under her father's blanket, with no lamp and no stove. Out on the harbor the dragger lay in her deck lights, and a man stood on her afterdeck, facing the hill.

Beth knew the set of him. The man in the skiff had sat that way, square to her house. Standing still, from up here, a man showed you no limp at all.

"Him, maybe," she said.

"Maybe."

The door knocked.

"Who's awake?" Dane said, low. "Give me the island."

She gave him the two yellow windows at the bend in the road, which would have the pair of them married by Tuesday. Then, because she still had it in her ear, she gave him the net, bright and quick, Tess on a bad night:

"Calloway, aboard. Marshal, aboard." A small pause, exactly Tess's. "Allegedly."

She felt him turn his head in the dark to look at her.

"Does Tess know you do that?"

"Nobody does."

Below them the store went dark. A flashlight went east along the Harbor Road to Tess's, and a window came up in Tess's house and stayed lit a while. When it went out she whispered, "Tess, bed. Late, for her."

In the mudroom the door went on knocking.

"You left one out," he said.

"Which."

"The house that's talked about me most today."

She waited.

"You. Since this morning."

She put her face in the blanket and laughed without a sound. It shook her against his shoulder, and his knee was against hers.

A little after eleven an engine came up the Harbor Road from the village end in low gear, a pickup with no lights. It slowed under the house on the road side and idled there, and the idle came up through the floor into her.

"Still," Dane said, on a breath, and then nothing.

The door knocked, and the pickup went on up the road and was gone. For as long as it had sat there he hadn't said one word, and she had heard every second of that.

He put his mouth close to her hair. "I'd move you if I had anywhere better. The boat's open at the back, the sea's what it is, and that truck could be sitting up the road with its engine off, waiting for us to try. This brick is the best wall on the island. I'm sorry it's the best I've got."

Then: "Portland has to hear about that truck, and the phone needs sky. I'll be on the front step with the door open. Two strides. You'll see me the whole time."

He went down the hall and opened the front door on the road side and stood on the step, half turned from the house, with the phone up. From the floor she could see the faint glow under his chin through the open door, and hear nothing of him at all over the wind.

The mudroom door knocked, and the next gust went over the roof, and the door didn't.

The one after came hard off the water and shook the kitchen window in its frame, and in the mudroom nothing moved. That door had knocked in every northeast blow since she was born. Her father never fixed it, because he said it told him when the wind went round. It went quiet for one thing. Somebody was inside the outer door, holding it, standing in her mudroom in the dark, waiting for the house to settle.

She listened. Her father's alarm clock on its shelf, going on. The wind on the clapboards. Snow ticking at the window. From the mudroom, nothing, and nothing, and nothing.

She went down the hall without a light, along the wall where the boards didn't talk, reached through the front door and got him by the sleeve. At his ear: "Don't talk. Door's gone quiet."

Back at the brick his hand was on the gun. Then he talked, so low it came to her through his shoulder more than through the air.

"I'm on your right. The brick's at your back, and it's still warm, which is good of it. Your father's clock is going to keep going, and that's fine, let it. Anything that comes through that door comes to me first." His hand found her knee under the blanket, once, and went back to the gun. "He comes to us. Not the other way."

In the snow-light from the window, the clock's hands on its shelf stood at twenty to twelve.

The kitchen door's latch lifted, and stuck, and lifted again.

Nobody who had been in this house made that sound.

Dane was at the kitchen door before the second lift had finished, flat to the wall on the hinge side. "Evening," he said through the boards, in an ordinary voice, the first in the house all night.

The outer door banged. Feet went down the back steps, fast, and away toward the harbor.

He waited the length of a long breath. Then he lifted and shoved, and past his shoulder she saw the mudroom door swinging and the steps white under the sky, with dark scuffs going down them in the sleet.

He shut both doors and came back to the brick, and didn't sit.

"Who knew you were here?"

"You."

She saw it again: the small glow under his chin out on the front step, a few minutes before the door went quiet.
