# Chapter Two: The Weather Hold

Reyes went out through the mudroom to look at the back steps, and when he tried to come back in, the kitchen door wouldn't have him.

The iron thumb-latch clanked and held. He tried it harder, and the whole door bucked in its frame. Beth crossed the kitchen, lifted the thumb-piece from her side and put her shoulder to the boards, and the door went open on him.

He stood in the mudroom with his right hand on his hip under the jacket, and behind him the outer door stood open on the weather.

"Lift and shove."

He shut the door between them and tried it. A clank, a rattle. Then he lifted and shoved, and it came, loud, and the third time it opened without a sound.

"Nobody who's been in this house makes the noise you made first."

Snow was melting into the gray above his ears, and she wanted to put her fingers there and find out whether it was as soft as it looked.

She went past him onto the back step. The wind was making from the east with the first of the snow in it, and below her the path dropped to the Harbor Road and the store, and the fuel float lay bare.

She found the skiff out among the moorings: one man at the oars, his face to her hill, pulling long and even for the outer harbor, toward a red dragger on the visitors' mooring, a Gloucester boat in for the weather. He rowed like somebody who had learned before he could walk.

A squall came down across the harbor in a gray wall with snow inside it. It took the outer moorings, then the dragger, then the skiff, two lengths short of any boat. She stood in the cold and watched the place where he had been. The moorings came back out of the snow one at a time, and the dragger came back, red and swinging, and he didn't.

Reyes said nothing beside her.

He made his call there under open sky with the harbor at his back. She went in. Through the kitchen window she watched his mouth and couldn't hear a word of it, and she put a stick in the stove it didn't need.

He came in with snow on his shoulders. "Portland knows about your man with the heel now. They didn't this morning. And the grand jury sits Monday. It's the last sitting before Pelletier walks, and they'd like you in front of it."

She looked once at the subpoena under the barometer.

"Any boat that would take you across before then, I'd like to hear about it."

"Nothing's crossing in this."

"Then tonight I'd like one way in from the land, a door that locks, and somebody awake behind it."

She looked down past him at the *North Reach* in the slip, going white along the rails. One way down to the boat from the land. A wheelhouse door that locked. Ned awake aboard in every blow since Beth was a girl.

"Boat."

Boots came up the path below the back steps, slow on the ice.

Reyes was at the door before she'd turned, his hand at his hip, his body between her and the glass.

A red vest came up out of the snow under an open oilskin, then a clipboard, then Tess Marr, blowing from the climb. He lifted and shoved, quietly, and let her in.

"Calloway. Net's at six, and you're the one I couldn't guess. Where you sleeping?"

"On the boat."

Tess wrote it in ballpoint on the back of her left hand, *CALLOWAY — BOAT*. Then she looked past Beth at him. "What do I call him?"

"Dane," he said.

She dug in her vest for the pen cap, and a fold of bills came up with it, hundreds on the outside. It was back in the pocket before the cap was on.

"Batteries went up a dollar at noon. Storm price. They come back down when the boat runs or when I die, whichever's first. You've got salt for those steps, Calloway? You've got salt." She was out the door again before anybody answered her.

Dane stood at the glass and watched her down. Halfway to the road she stopped on the path, her inked hand held out to dry, and stood a long moment looking out at the dragger. Then she went on.

"That was hundreds," Dane said to the glass. "Where does a store on an island take in hundreds in November?"

Beth didn't answer him.

* * *

Out on the visitors' mooring the dragger's deck lights came on all at once, stem to stern, and nobody came out on deck to use them. Dane stood at the rail and watched them.

Beth went forward down the one lane of steel between the two trucks, the fire company's propane truck and the co-op's bait truck, chained aboard for the two o'clock that never ran.

Over the bow, the steel ramp the cars drove aboard on ran down from the shore onto the *North Reach*, hung at its seaward end from two towers. The island called it the bridge. The tide was making on top of the storm, and every sea lifted the boat and shoved the bow up under the bridge, and the bridge ground on its pilings, a long iron groan and a knock, and again.

She wanted the bow lines doubled before the top of the tide, by her own hands. Dane had asked her twice to come in. Now he was a stride behind her in the driving snow.

She had the spare line in her fist when something above them screamed.

It came from the towers, a shriek she heard in her back teeth, and a cable came across the bow at head height, whipping, alive.

He hit her from behind and took her down flat under the rail, and on the way down her fist went into his collar and closed.

Then it came to her in order. Steel against her cheek, so cold it burned. His weight across her back, his arm over her head. His voice at her ear, low.

"Cable. It went over us. It's on the bait truck's hood now, I can see the end of it, it's jumping. Okay, it's slowing down. Keep your face where it is. The left tower's leaning toward town, and I don't love the lean. The bridge is still up. Still up. I'm watching it so you don't have to."

She didn't answer him. Under his voice the rest came in order: the cable's end rattling along the deck plates and going quiet, the bridge groaning, a sea booming hollow under the bow, and high in the towers a thin hum from whatever was still holding.

The cable lay still. A sea lifted the two of them and set them down. Her fist was still in his collar, warm from his neck, and she knew it was, and she kept it there through one more sea.

Then the bridge came down.

The towers let it go, and it folded down onto the bow like a dropped drawbridge, with a sound she took through the deck into her ribs, and its far end went into the water. Beth let out all the air she had. Her shoulders came down from where they had been since the night freight, as if the thing she had been braced for had come, and fallen, and was done.

Then she was up. The *North Reach* had to come off the slip before that wreck ground through the plating. Beth threw the bow line across the gap, and the eye dropped over an iron post on the granite pier beside the slip the first time. Dane had the tail round the bow cleat and was taking his turns the wrong way.

"Other way. Unless you want to go with it."

He took them the other way, and the line came taut between the boat and the stone and held. Up in the wheelhouse Ned backed the *North Reach* off the slip with the wreck scraping down the bow, and laid the boat in alongside the pier.

The power went in the same surge. The lights along the Harbor Road went out together, and the harbor went black. Out on the visitors' mooring the dragger didn't so much as blink.

Ned came up out of the bow hatch with a flashlight, wet to the shins. "Water's coming in up there."

He put the light on the wreck lying across the bow and looked at it a while. "Well. That's the bridge."

Dane was up off the deck. "Does it do that a lot?"

"Just the once." Ned kept the light where it was. "She's not carrying anybody till somebody from the Coast Guard has his hand on that." He'd call it in to them tonight, by the book. "Ten days, if the state gets a barge out."

Dane laid a hand on the deckhouse. "At least it's steel."

"Steel doesn't burn," Ned said. "Everything in it does."

Dane took his hand off the steel. They went aft, Ned behind them with the light. At the stern the deck ran out low and open to the black water with nothing across it but a chain, and a sea slopped in around their feet and drained away.

"It's got a hole in the front and no back door," Dane said. "And the whole island has her aboard tonight, because Tess has it on her hand. So tonight she isn't." He looked at the chain. "Anyone with a skiff."

"Nobody's coming over that stern in this."

"I believe you. Thank you, Captain. I'm still taking her off."

Dane went up the aluminum gangway to the pier, its wheels grinding on the granite each time the boat surged, and along to the road end, out of earshot.

"Where'll you be, Bethie?"

"Home. Don't tell anybody."

"Nobody but me and God." He tapped the handheld on her coat. It lived on nine. "You need me, nine. Like your dad."

He went forward toward his pumps. Halfway along the deck he turned, found her with the spill of the flashlight, and nodded once, and went on.

From the road end Dane whistled, two low notes. She was already on the gangway, its wheels grinding under her.

* * *

They went home by the shore with no lamp, her first, sleet off the water in her face and rockweed under the snow. Below the church she stepped over the knee-high ledge without thinking about it, and heard him find it behind her with his shin.

The back steps were iced. She thumbed her flashlight on, and they came up out of the dark glazed and gray, and she was most of the way up before he spoke.

"Light."

She killed it. In the dark she crossed the mudroom, lifted the latch and shoved, and the kitchen door opened without a sound.

The mudroom's outer door knocked against its latch in every gust. A hollow knock, then nothing, then the knock.

They sat on the kitchen floor with their backs to the chimney, the one wall in the house a bullet wouldn't go through, under her father's blanket, with no lamp and no stove. Out on the harbor the dragger lay in her deck lights, and a man stood on her afterdeck, facing the hill.

Beth knew the set of him. The man in the skiff had sat square to her house like that. Standing still, from up here, a man showed you no limp at all.

"Him, maybe," she said, and Dane said, "Maybe."

The door knocked.

"Who's awake?" Dane said, low. "Give me the island."

So she gave it to him in a whisper. The two yellow windows at the bend in the road would have the pair of them married by Tuesday and his people looked up by Wednesday. Tess had done the net by now, so Beth did it for him, bright and quick, net control on a bad night:

"Calloway, aboard. Marshal, aboard." A small pause, exactly Tess's. "Allegedly."

She felt him turn his head in the dark to look at her.

"Does Tess know you do that?"

"Nobody does."

Below them the store went dark. A flashlight went east along the Harbor Road to Tess's, and a window came up in Tess's house and stayed lit a while, and went out.

"Tess, bed. Late, for her."

In the mudroom the door went on knocking.

"You left one out," he said.

"Which."

"The house that's talked about me most today."

She waited.

"You. Since this morning."

She put her face in the blanket and laughed without a sound. It shook her against his shoulder, and his knee was against hers.

A little after eleven an engine came up the Harbor Road from the village end in low gear, a pickup with no lights. It slowed under the house on the road side and idled there, and the idle came up through the floor into her.

"Still," Dane said, on a breath, and then nothing.

The door knocked, and the pickup went on up the road and was gone. For as long as it had sat there he hadn't said one word, and she had heard every second of that.

When he spoke again it was into her hair. "If I had anywhere better, I'd move you. The boat's open at the back, that truck's sitting up the road somewhere or it isn't, and it's blowing a gale. This brick's the best thing on the island, and I'm sorry it's a brick."

He shifted under the blanket. "Portland has to hear that, and the phone needs sky. I'll be on the front step with the door open, two strides from you. You'll see me the whole time."

He went down the hall and opened the front door and stood just outside it on the step, his back half turned to the house. From the brick she could see a dark shape against the snow, and a faint glow under his chin.

The mudroom door knocked, and the next gust went over the roof, and the door didn't.

The one after came hard off the water and shook the kitchen window in its frame, and in the mudroom nothing moved. That door had knocked in every northeast blow since she was born. Her father never fixed it, because he said it told him when the wind went round. It went quiet for one thing. Somebody was inside the outer door, holding it, standing in her mudroom in the dark, waiting for the house to settle.

She listened. Her father's alarm clock on its shelf, going on. The wind on the clapboards. Snow ticking at the glass. From the mudroom, nothing, and nothing, and nothing.

She went down the hall along the wall where the boards didn't talk, reached out through the open front door and got him by the sleeve. At his ear: "Don't talk. Door's gone quiet."

Back at the brick his hand was on the gun, and he talked so low it came to her through his shoulder.

"I'm on your right. The brick's at your back, and it's still warm, which is good of it. Your father's clock is going to keep going, and that's fine, let it. Anything that comes through that door comes to me first." His hand found her knee under the blanket, once, and went back to the gun. "He comes to us. Not the other way."

In the snow-light from the window, the clock's hands on its shelf stood at twenty to twelve.

The kitchen door's latch lifted, and stuck, and lifted again.

Nobody who had been in this house made that sound.

Dane was at the kitchen door before the second lift had finished, against the wall on the hinge side. "Evening," he said through the boards, in an ordinary voice, the first in the house all night.

The outer door banged. Feet went down the back steps, fast, and away toward the harbor.

He waited the length of a long breath. Then he lifted and shoved, and past his shoulder she saw the mudroom door swinging and the steps white under the sky, with dark scuffs going down them in the sleet.

He shut both doors and came back to the brick, and didn't sit.

"Who knew you were here?"

"You."

She saw it again: the small glow under his chin out on the front step, a few minutes before the door went quiet.
