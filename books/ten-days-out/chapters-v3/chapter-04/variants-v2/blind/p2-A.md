# Chapter Four: Glassing the Harbor

Sunday came up gray at first light, and the granite on top of Lookout Hill was wet with it. The cold came out of the rock into her by inches, through the knees of her jeans and the front of her coat, until her breastbone ached against it. They lay flat in the blueberry stems with his binoculars between them, and she had them, because she had taken them.

"My turn," Dane said.

"Your turn was the dark."

A hood came up the path from the store side. A right boot set down clean in the slush. A left that came after it late, the heel dragging.

She didn't say anything. She put one finger on the rock between them, and beside her Dane went still the whole length of him.

The man went round her house once, front and back, and up the steps into the mudroom. Through the kitchen window she saw the inside door give. One lift, his shoulder, open at the first try.

She had shown one person how that door went: a marshal, Friday, on her own back step, up first and then the shoulder, three tries to do it quiet. Or the man had taught himself on her door that same night, lifting, sticking, lifting, with Dane on the other side of the boards. She lay on the rock and couldn't make it come out one way.

"Hands?" Dane said.

"Empty."

"Then he's opening doors, and he's in no hurry. It's a small house." He let that sit. "It won't take him long."

It didn't. The man came out onto her back step, pushed his hood back, and turned his face up to the hill.

Young. Younger than the walk, wide and pale, and nobody she knew. Every man who wintered on Cutter had a boat she could hang him on, and she had no slot for this one. He didn't care who on the island saw his face, and that was the part she liked least.

He kept looking at the top of the hill, as if somebody had told him where. She moved nothing but her eyes.

"Binoculars," Dane said.

"He'll see them move." She kept them.

The hood went up again. He ran the skiff out from under the store dock and pulled into the chop, and a man on the dragger's afterdeck leaned down and took his painter. The skiff went up the *Margaret Ann*'s side on a line.

She gave Dane the binoculars then. He typed once, flat on his stomach, the phone in the lee of his body. When it buzzed against the granite he read it and turned it round to her himself.

*Pike: Q says hands off the vessel. Observe only. Keep witness in place.*

"In place," Beth said.

"She wants me alive long enough to be useful." He looked at the line. "Both of us."

"What else is on there?"

He pressed the arrow once, for her. *Sold. Sorry.*

He took the notebook out of his jacket and, without opening it, slid a sheet from the back cover, folded in quarters. The yard's listing. A white lobster boat up on stands, Webbers Cove across the top.

She laughed. A deputy marshal flat in the blueberries with a lobster boat folded in his coat like a picture of a girl. Then she saw his face and stopped. She took the sheet from him, folded it back along its creases, and held it out.

"Good hull," she said.

He folded it smaller and put it away, and left the two words lit.

"Down," he said. "Now. That boat has binoculars of its own."

This time she went.

They went off the back of the hill on their bellies and then on their feet, down the islanders' path into spruce so close the wind stayed up in the tops. He set a pace that was nearly a run. A clump of snow let go overhead and burst across his shoulders, and he didn't slow for it.

Below them something came up the path, puffing. Dane's hand went to his hip and came away again. Russ came round the bend with a thermos, red-bearded and blowing, and stopped.

"Somebody rowed out to that dragger at sunup," Russ said. "Saw him from my kitchen." He turned and fell in beside them going down. "So I'm going to take the *Second Wind* out and ask the *Margaret Ann* what she wants with a skiff at that hour."

"Don't," Dane said. "I'm asking you to stay off that boat."

Russ looked at him, and at Beth, who gave him nothing. "Feds told me stay off that boat," Russ said. "So I'm staying off that boat. I'm not a complicated man."

"Nothing's flying, either," Russ said. "LifeFlight wouldn't lift an old woman with a bad heart yesterday. So it's boats. The minute this lays down I'll run the two of you to Pruett myself. The *Second Wind*'s got the legs for it."

Beth opened her mouth to say yes. Dane got there first.

"Thank you, and no," Dane said, "because any boat on this island could be theirs, I can't read this sea, and whoever runs her across leaves a wake the whole bay can follow."

Russ took it without offense. "That's three," he said. "I'd have taken any one of them."

"Answer me one," Dane said. "That cable on the slip tower. Did it have help?"

Russ thought about it for a dozen steps. "Somebody'd have to beat the rust to it," he said. "That tower's been on the district's list since Labor Day."

Dane looked across at her, a man who'd guessed right and wanted a little credit for it. She walked on.

At the fork Russ took the path that came out behind Marr's, and left the one to his own house standing in the snow. Beth knew where that path came out. She turned up toward the north road.

* * *

The set had run dry at noon and sucked air, and the last of the fuel can was in it now and doing nothing till the line was bled. Dane lay on his back with his head under the engine, hunting the injector pump by feel. Beth was down on one elbow beside him, hip to hip, with the wrench. The concrete came up cold through her hip, and her hands stank of diesel. The shed door stood propped on its frozen track, and through it the long drive ran straight up to the spine of the north road.

"Where does a person out here keep money?" he said, the way he'd ask for the wrench.

"Credit union."

"And who do you owe?"

"Nobody." She pointed past his ear with the wrench. "Left of that. The squat one. No truck, no boat." She heard it after she'd said it. "He had one."

The *Ruthie C*. Thirty-eight feet, named for her mother. Her father fished her out of Calloway Cove and paid the note the day it came due, and never lost a string to a blow he could have seen coming. Then the price went soft, and the bank called the note on a paper he'd signed in a good year. A man came over on the noon boat with a box of folders. At the end of the afternoon her father signed where the man's pen showed him. Then he asked Beth to carry the papers down to the man's truck, because his hands had started to shake and he wouldn't have the man see it. She carried them. The box was heavier than paper ought to be.

She stopped there. Dane had found the pump. He hadn't touched it.

"Walk me through it," he said. "The bag."

So that was what this was. She gave it to him anyway, in order, the way she'd give a man the tide.

"Envelopes from the store. A slip inside, my name on the front in Tess's hand. Flap tucked. Never sealed."

"Who's alone with it?"

"The boat."

"What keeps a bag shut?"

She took Thursday's spent seal out of her coat pocket, red, cut off the fare bag and sent back in the empty pouch. She pushed his cuff back with her thumb. His wrist was warm, and his pulse went under her fingers while she knotted the strap round it and pulled the knot hard.

"Fare bag's red. That's the boat's. Tess seals hers yellow." She gave the knot one more pull. "Now you're the boat's."

"I've been the boat's since I jumped."

"Comes off with a knife. If it's off, I'll know."

One thing about him she could check, then.

*Is this going in your log?* It was in her mouth. She kept it there.

"Crack it," she said. "Quarter turn. Now pump."

He worked the lever. The screw spat air and a froth of fuel, and spat again.

Over his chest, through the propped door, a man stood on the spine of the north road. Hood up. Binoculars. Standing still, his face turned toward the north point.

"Stop," she said.

He stopped with the lever halfway.

One more stroke and the screw would run clean, and one pull after that the set would catch. A diesel hammering at a sheeted summer house would tell a man from away which house it was.

Dane turned his head on the concrete to see. "Where—"

"Don't."

He didn't.

His gun lay on the pad by his right hand. He didn't reach for it. They lay hip to hip on the cold concrete, the bleed screw open between them, and neither of them moved.

*North end*, he'd told Portland last night, on the porch. She didn't say it.

The man looked a long time. Then he lowered the binoculars and walked off down the east-shore fork, and his left heel dragged going over the rise.

Dane sat up and hit his head on the frame.

"He's checking houses," he said. "We move tonight."

"Where to? Mine's done. There's the fish house at the head of the cove. Cove's been over the sill every tide since Friday's surge. Floor's under water at the top of it, and there's one door. It drops on Monday's tides."

"Then Monday."

He lay back down and worked the lever. The fuel came nearly clean. She kept her eyes on the screw and asked it.

"Last night. Was that the end of it?"

He was quiet long enough for her to hear the tide on the point.

"That's a not yet," he said. "One I can't afford."

"Then save up," she told the pump.

The screw coughed once more and ran clean, amber down the pump body. "Close it." He closed it.

Ned came up the long drive at dusk with two cans off the *North Reach*'s day tank and his flashlight. Beth went out to meet him and stood where she could see the spine. It was empty against the last of the light.

Dane held the light while Ned poured. He kept it low, with his own body between the beam and the road.

"Bit higher," Ned said, not looking up. "You hold a light like nobody ever handed you one."

"Nobody did," Dane said. "I've been holding them wrong on my own since I was twelve."

Ned emptied the second can. "Glass is coming up, Bethie. Breeze by morning. Pump's eating impellers. I'm going back aboard, and I'll stay aboard till the barge. She's taking water slow up forward. She'll keep."

She told Ned nothing about the spine. He had a boat taking water and a bad hip, and she kept it off him.

"Marshal." Ned took his light back from Dane, picked up both cans in his other hand, and went off down the drive. At the foot of it he turned, and the flashlight came up once, a small yellow swing in the dark, and he went on toward the harbor.

Full dark came down on the drive. The spine was a black line against a blacker sky. Dane came and stood at her shoulder.

"Do we run it?"

Ned ran it every storm. A man from away had stood on that road this afternoon with binoculars. Her hand was already on the cord, and she took it off and put the handle in his fist.

"Pull it."

* * *

Monday came in between squalls, a week before the Monday that mattered in Portland. The last squall had gone through at first light and left the air raw and still, and the next one stood out over the bay like a gray wall. At dead low the bar lay dry from the Ashby shore toward Calloway Cove, the cove on the east shore that carried her family's name, a long way round from her house on the harbor. Gravel and cobble, hers end to end. Ashby had one road out. She wanted a back door. That meant finding where the gut dropped while she could still see the bottom.

Across the cove, at its head, the fish house showed the tide line dark on its new shingles, and its sill stood out of the water for the first time since Friday.

At the seaward end the bottom dropped into a dark trench. The wash came in over the cobbles knocking and drew back with a long suck.

Dane came up at her shoulder. "Let me go first. I walk it, then you walk it. These are waterproof."

She was already in to the shins. "That's a narrow definition of waterproof. Stand there."

"It's the one rule I've got," he said.

"Keep it from there."

He stayed at the edge of the dry bar, where she'd pointed, and didn't come.

In to the knee. The thigh. Each wash shoved at her. Each draw pulled at her feet, cobbles rolling out from under the boot, and she leaned the other way. Then the bottom went out from under her front foot, and the water took her at the waist like a hand closing. "Son of a bitch," she told it, and went on swearing, steadily and with variety: the water, the cold, the island, Connecticut.

"You're swearing at Connecticut," Dane said from the bar. "Which seems fair."

"Shut up. I'm listening to it."

He shut up.

The water worked along the edge of the drop, and under it her boot found the lip, a step of ledge she could set a heel on. She paced it back one stride from the drop. Then she climbed out.

She came up onto the dry bar soaked to the ribs. Her jeans weighed what the water weighed. Her teeth went. Her legs were somebody else's. He opened his jacket, and she walked into it.

He closed it round her. His arms came after it. Her wet front went into his fleece and soaked it. The cold went on biting her from the waist down. From the waist up, him.

Her face was against his neck, where the stubble had come in since her father's razor. Rough, where she'd seen it go smooth at the sink. The red tag sat on his bare wrist at the edge of her eye, where his arm crossed her back. She shook. He held on.

"Beth," he said against her temple. After a while: "What's the plan?"

She had looked at it already.

"Half tide, in the dark, the bar's knee-deep. You come behind me, a stride in from the lip, and you put your feet where mine go. Ninety seconds, if you don't stop. And you stay inside where I put you."
