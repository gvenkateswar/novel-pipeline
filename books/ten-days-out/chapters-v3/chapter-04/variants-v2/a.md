# Chapter Four: Glassing the Harbor

Sunday, first light, and somebody was coming up the path from the store side.

Beth lay flat on the bare top of Lookout Hill with Dane's binoculars, the blueberry stems wet against her face. The cold came up out of the granite into her breastbone by inches. Below them the harbor ran gray and white-topped, exact as a chart.

A hood. A right boot set down clean in the slush, and the left coming after it late, the heel dragging.

She put one finger on the rock between them. Dane went still.

The man went round her house once, front and back, in no hurry. He climbed her back steps into the mudroom. Through the kitchen window she saw the inside door give: one lift, then his shoulder, open at the first try.

She had shown one person how that door went. A marshal, on Friday, on her own back step: lift first, then the shoulder, three tries before he could do it quiet. Or the man down there had taught himself, on her door, that same night, lifting, sticking, lifting with Dane on the other side of the boards. She lay on the rock and couldn't make it come out one way.

She passed him the binoculars without taking her eyes off the house.

"He's opening things and shutting them again," Dane said. "Small house. It won't take him long."

It didn't. Dane put the binoculars back in her hand, and the man came out onto her back step with nothing in his hands.

He pushed the hood back and turned his face up to the hill.

Young, younger than the walk. A wide pale face she didn't know. She placed every man who wintered on Cutter by his boat, and she had no slot for this one. He kept his face on the top of the hill, as if somebody had told him where. He didn't care who on the island saw that face, either, and that was the part she liked least.

She moved nothing but her eyes.

He put the hood up, ran the skiff out from under the store dock and pulled into the chop like a man born in a skiff. A figure on the dragger's afterdeck took his painter, and the skiff went up the *Margaret Ann*'s side on a line.

Dane typed once, flat on his stomach, the phone held low in the stems. When it buzzed against the granite he read it. Then he turned the screen to her himself.

*Pike: Q says hands off the vessel. Observe only. Keep witness in place.*

"In place," Beth said.

"She wants me alive long enough to be useful." He looked at the line a while. "Both of us."

He pressed the arrow once. *Sold. Sorry.*

He took the notebook from inside his jacket and, without opening it, slid a sheet out of the back cover, folded in quarters. He spread it on the rock between them: a white lobster boat up on stands, the yard's name across the top.

She laughed. A deputy marshal on his stomach in the blueberries with a lobster boat folded in his coat, like a picture of a girl. Then she saw his face and stopped. She folded the sheet back along its creases for him.

"Good hull," she said.

He folded it once more, smaller than it had been, and put it away. The phone stayed lit in his other hand with the two words on it, and he didn't clear them.

"Down," he said. "Now, while that deck's got binoculars of its own."

They went back off the top on their elbows, then on their feet, and down the islanders' path into the spruce at a pace that was nearly a run. A clump of snow let go of the tops and burst across his shoulders. Through the trees she caught the *Margaret Ann* once more on her mooring, and then the hill was between them.

Boots came up the path below them, crunching on frozen needles. Dane's hand went to his hip. It came away again when Russ Whitcomb rounded the bend with a thermos.

"Somebody rowed out to that dragger at sunup," Russ said, falling in beside them. "Saw him go from my kitchen. After breakfast I'm taking the *Second Wind* out to ask the *Margaret Ann* what she wants with a skiff at that hour."

"Don't," Dane said. "I'm asking you to stay off that boat."

Russ considered him over the thermos. "Feds told me stay off that boat. So I'm staying off that boat. I'm not a complicated man."

They went on down, Russ in front now, his boots knocking snow off the roots.

"Nothing's flying, either," Russ said over his shoulder. "Pilot wouldn't lift an old woman with a bad heart yesterday. So it's boats. The minute this lays down, I'll run the two of you to Pruett myself."

"Thank you. No," Dane said, "because any boat on this island could be theirs, because I can't read this sea, and because whoever runs you across leaves a wake the whole bay can follow."

Russ took it without offense and nodded, the way he nodded at a forecast.

"That cable on the slip tower," Dane said, walking. "Did it have help?"

"Somebody'd have to beat the rust to it," Russ said. "That tower's been on the district's list since Labor Day."

At the fork Russ stopped and tucked the thermos under his arm. "Stove to feed," he said. He took the path that came out behind Marr's, and left the one to his own house standing in the snow.

Beth knew where that path came out. She turned up toward the north road.

* * *

The set had run dry at noon and sucked air. The last of the fuel can was in it. Dane lay on his back on the concrete pad with his head under the engine, hunting for the injector pump. Beth was down on one elbow beside him, hip to hip, the cold of the pad coming through her coat. The shed door stood propped on its frozen track. Through it the long drive ran straight up to the spine of the north road, and she kept that line at the edge of her eye. Wherever the man was, he had binoculars.

"Where does a person out here keep money?" Dane said, the way he'd ask for the wrench. "Assuming it isn't a coffee can."

"Credit union. Pruett."

"Who do you owe?"

"Nobody." She pointed past his ear with the wrench. "Left of that. The squat one."

"Truck? Boat?"

"No truck. No boat." She heard it, and went on anyway. "He had one."

The *Ruthie C*. Thirty-eight feet, named for her mother, fished out of Calloway Cove. Her father paid the note the day it came due and never lost a string to a blow he could have seen coming. Then the price went soft two winters running, and the bank called the note on a paper he'd signed in a good year. A man came over on the noon boat with a box of folders and sat at the kitchen table. At the end her father signed where the man's pen showed him. Then he asked Beth to carry the man's papers down to his truck, because his hands had started to shake and he wouldn't have the man see it. She carried them. The box was heavier than paper ought to be. The corner of it bit into her forearm the whole way down the hill.

She stopped there. Dane had found the pump. He didn't touch it.

"Walk me through it," he said. He was looking past her, out the door at the drive. "Your pay. Counter to teller."

"Envelopes from the store. Slip inside, my name on the front in Tess's hand. Flap tucked. Never sealed."

"Who's alone with it?"

"The boat."

"What keeps a bag shut?"

She had Thursday's spent seal in her coat pocket, a red one cut off the fare bag. She took his wrist, pushed his cuff back with her thumb and knotted the strap round his bare wrist. His pulse went under her fingers.

"Fare bag's red. That's the boat's. Tess seals hers yellow." She gave it one more pull. "Now you're the boat's."

"I've been the boat's since I jumped."

"Comes off with a knife. If it's off, I'll know."

One thing about him she could check, then.

He turned the wrist over and looked at the tag lying across the inside of it. *Is this going in your log?* It was in her mouth. She kept it there.

"Crack it," she said. "Quarter turn. Now pump."

He worked the lever. The screw spat air and froth. It spat again.

She looked past his chest, out through the door, up the drive.

A man stood on the spine of the north road. Hood up. Binoculars. He held dead still against the gray, the binoculars on the north point, where the summer houses were.

"Stop," she said.

He stopped with the lever halfway.

One more stroke, one pull of the cord, and the set would catch. A diesel running at a sheeted house would tell a man from away exactly which house. Islanders heard Ned's generator out here every storm and never looked twice. The man on the spine wasn't an islander.

Dane started to say something.

"Don't."

He didn't. His gun lay on the pad by his right hand, and he left it there. They lay on the cold concrete, hip to hip, her face an inch from the shoulder of his fleece, and breathed through their mouths.

*North end*, he'd told Portland last night, on the porch.

She didn't say it.

The man looked a long time. He ran the binoculars along the point, house by house, and back. Then he lowered them and went off down the east-shore fork. The left heel dragged as he dropped over the rise.

Dane sat up. "He's checking houses. We move tonight."

"Where? They've had the cottage." She kept her eyes on the empty spine. "The one place I've got that nobody looks at is the fish house at the head of Calloway Cove. The cove's been over the sill every tide since Friday. Floor's under water at the top of it, and there's one door."

"When does it drop?"

"Monday's tides."

"Then Monday."

He lay back under the set and pumped. The froth thinned.

"Last night," she said. "Was that the end of it?"

He was quiet long enough for her to hear the tide working on the point.

"That's a not yet," he said. "One I can't afford."

"Then save up," she told the pump.

The fuel came clean.

"Close it."

He closed it.

Ned came up the long drive at dusk with two cans off the *North Reach*'s day tank and his flashlight. Beth stood in the shed door, where she could see the spine the whole time. It stayed empty.

Dane held the light while Ned poured. He kept the beam low, his body between it and the road.

"Bit higher," Ned said, not looking up. Dane moved it. "You hold a light like nobody ever handed you one."

"Nobody did," Dane said. "I picked it up on my own, mostly at night, from people who were annoyed with me."

Ned emptied the second can. "The glass is coming up, Bethie. Down to a breeze by morning, with squalls in it." He set the can down. "Pump's eating impellers. I'm going back aboard, and I'll be staying aboard till the barge. She's taking water slow up forward. She'll keep."

He had a boat taking water and a hip that hated this drive. Beth kept the man on the spine off him.

"Marshal." He picked up both cans in one hand and started down the drive. At the foot of it the flashlight came round toward the shed, held there a second, and went on toward the harbor.

Full dark came down. The spine was a black line against a blacker sky.

"Do we run it?" Dane said at her shoulder.

Ned ran it every storm. Her hand was already on the cord. She put the handle in his.

"Pull it."

* * *

Monday came in between squalls, a week before the Monday that mattered in Portland. Nobody stood on the spine. She had looked for him twice from the Ashby porch, and once more from the shore before the house hid it.

At dead low the bar lay dry from the Ashby shore toward Calloway Cove, the cove on the east shore that carried her family's name, a long way from her house on the harbor. Gravel and cobble, firm underfoot, hers end to end. Ashby had one road out, and the man knew that road. She wanted a back door.

Across the cove, at its head, the fish house stood on its blocks with the tide line dark on the new shingles. Its sill was out of the water for the first time since Friday.

At the seaward end the bottom went out from under the bar in a dark trench. The wash came in over the cobbles knocking, and drew back with a long suck.

Dane came up at her shoulder. "Let me go first. I walk it, then you walk it. And these are waterproof."

She was already in to the shins. "That's a narrow definition of waterproof. Stand there."

He stopped at the edge of the dry bar where she pointed, and stayed there, with his hands open at his sides.

She went in. Knee. Thigh. Then the bottom dropped from under her front foot, and the water took her at the waist like a hand closing. "Son of a bitch," she told it, and kept on telling it with variety: the water, the cold, the island, Connecticut.

"You're swearing at Connecticut," Dane said behind her. "Which seems fair."

"Shut up. I'm listening to it."

He shut up.

Nobody on the island could see this end of the bar from any road. The water worked along the edge of the trench. She felt for the lip with her boot and found it, a step of ledge she could set a heel on. She paced it back from the drop, one stride, and fixed the place in her head against a split boulder on the shore. Then she climbed out.

She came up onto the dry bar soaked to the ribs, teeth going. He opened his jacket. She walked into it.

He closed it round her. His arms came after it. Her wet front went flat against his fleece and soaked it through. She put her face against his neck, where the stubble had come in since her father's razor, rough where she had seen it go smooth. She breathed. From the waist down, the cold went on biting. From the waist up, there was him. The red tag lay on his bare wrist at the edge of her eye, where his arm crossed her back.

She shook. He held on.

"Beth," he said against her temple.

After a while: "What's the plan?"

She had looked at it already. "Half tide, in the dark, the bar's knee-deep. You come behind me, a stride in from the lip, and you put your feet where mine go." Her teeth knocked on the words. "Ninety seconds, if you don't stop. And you stay inside where I put you."
