# Chapter Eight: Not Water

At last light she took the second mug from Ned and wrapped both hands round it, and her eyes went down to the deck plates.

"Good," Ned said. "Don't tell me."

He set his own mug on the table beside the notebook. His eyes went over the notebook and on to the coffee, and he drank.

"She'll blow all night," he said. "Snow behind it by morning, by the look of the west. Crew cabin's made up, and there's dry gear in the locker. Kenny's. He's in Pruett. Lines'll want looking at on the top of the tide."

Kenny's shirt was short in the arm, his jeans short at the ankle, and they were dry, which was all she asked of them. She lay in the lower bunk with the blanket to her chin and the notebook under her pillow. All night the forepeak pump ran under her through the steel, a pulse she could have counted by and didn't. Once she heard Ned's step on the deck overhead, going forward to the lines and coming back. She slept in pieces. Each time she woke, her hand was already under the pillow on the cover of the book.

Toward the top of the tide the boat began to work against the pier, and she couldn't lie there any longer. She went up the inside companionway to the wheelhouse in the dark. The snow had come back with the gale. It went sideways past the windows Ned's rag had smeared, and down on deck his flashlight moved from line to line. Below her the brow's wheels ground on the granite at every surge, louder with the water high.

A figure was coming along the riprap below the pier from the town end, out of the store's sightline, slow, trying each stone. One arm was held across the body. In the other hand something square caught Ned's light as it swung, and showed red.

She was out the wheelhouse door and down the outside ladder and the rungs were iced under her hands and the snow went down her collar and below her the brow lifted and dropped and ground and Ned's light went across the stone and the red of the case came and went in it, and her body was a rung ahead of her the whole way down.

Ned stood halfway down the brow with the light on the man at its foot.

"Top of the tide, Marshal," he said. "She's lively on the brow."

"Captain." Dane stood on the stone in the snow and didn't come on.

The brow rose under Ned and settled. The light didn't move off Dane.

"Let him on, Ned," Beth said from the gate.

Ned held the light on him a breath longer. Then he backed up the brow ahead of him, the beam going down to the treads.

Dane came up the grinding brow behind him with the bad arm held in against his coat, stopped at the gate, and held the red Pelican case out to her, shut, with both its catches done up, and she took it from him in both hands.

* * *

The sleet came in flat through the deckhouse door before she got it shut behind him.

In the crew mess his wet coat steamed at the heater. He set his phone on the table to work the coat off the bad arm and sat down across from her, the red case between them. She had the notebook under her hand, and the cold came up out of the steel into her forearms.

"May I have the book?"

"No."

"Then I'll tell you what's in it, Ms. Calloway. I'm going to do it once, in order, and you can check me. I investigated you. The money questions in the generator shed were for the case. I wrote down every place we've slept. I went to bed with you, and I kept writing. 'Walked the bar' was a lie. I was on the ledge."

"I know where you were."

"On the ramp I didn't know there was any money. Nobody told me till Saturday. I look at whatever leaves a witness's hands, and on the ramp that was the pouch. Somebody put nine thousand dollars in your envelope so you'd look bought in front of the grand jury."

"Wasted their money."

"A few pages before your plan. 'Every place we've slept, and B's family property.'"

She opened the book at the place, tipped up off the table so he couldn't see the page. He had it word for word, the comma too. She shut it.

"Before two on Tuesday morning I left you asleep behind a door nobody could bar from outside." He looked at her hand on the cover. "Yesterday you did the same to me."

"My report was due by two, and it needed sky. At the top of the tide nobody could cross the bar to you, and from the ledge I could see the cove road, the only way in by land. The light at the Ashby end, short, then long, was a man off the *Margaret Ann* calling his skiff in off the burned shore. When the skiff's light came across the cove, I went down off the rock into the sea under the bank and stood in it till they'd gone. That's the jeans. Then I came round to your door the long way."

"There's a line about the pouch. Seal noted: red. You told me the fare bag's red. I took it for the teller writing down the one on top, and I didn't ask you, because asking was telling you what was in the other one."

She looked at the heater grille and let the book lie shut under her hand.

"The fish house is in that book," he said. "It was never in a report. Portland has 'east shore.'"

"East shore's half the island."

"That was the idea. After the ramp head Pike asked for status, and I sent one word. Graze. Give Portland more than a word and Portland sends something, and whatever it sends comes down in the open, on the ballfield or at the float, where that deck can see it. The water was shut by mid-morning anyhow. 'Not again today' means the Coast Guard won't land under fire. And a man got shot on a lit road at dawn, and nobody on this island opened a door."

"One shot over the water at first light. That's somebody's eiders. Nobody on this island gets out of bed for eiders."

He put his good hand over his eyes for a second and took it away.

"You came by the road," she said.

"I sat out the night low at the fish house in case you came back along the shore. When the flood covered the ledges, I took the road. There's one boat on this island you'd sleep on. At four, at the top of the north road, before I walked down, I told Pike I've been sleeping with my witness, and that she has my book. That's all of it."

Her finger found the old mug ring on the table and went round it once.

She stood and took his phone off the table into her fist.

"Give me Pike."

She was out through the deckhouse door into the weather before he answered, turning for the seaward side with the phone held in against her coat, the bench scraping behind her as he came up off it to follow and pulled his coat off the heater, the bad arm pinned to his ribs, his good hand reaching for the door as it swung back at him.

* * *

On the seaward side of the deckhouse the snow came through in squalls. She waited in the lee for light enough to read pencil by, his phone in her pocket, and Dane waited with her. The day came up behind the squalls, and between them the open water ran gray to the south with nothing on it. The deckhouse steel was cold at her back through the coat.

"Before you do," Dane said. "What I told Pike at four goes to the defense, whatever you say to her now. Anything that makes a marshal look bent, the prosecutor has to hand them. You'll hear it from the defense. Hear it from me."

She kept the phone in her own fist. She found Pike at the top of his calls, pressed it, put it on speaker and lifted it to the sky between them, and they bent their heads to it. It rang twice, thin, a long way off. Sleet rattled on its case.

"Pike."

"This is Beth Calloway."

The line hissed for a breath. "I don't apologize for orders, Ms. Calloway. I'll tell you what they were. Homeland Security asked for a daily log on you, and I ordered it. You weren't to be told. Nine thousand dollars came into your account in your own envelope, and you weren't to be told that either, not till we knew whose it was. Q is Quist, in Portland."

Beth had the notebook open in the lee of her coat. With her free thumb she turned back to Saturday night and came forward one night at a time. His pencil was small and even, and it didn't hurry anywhere. At the foot of the first night's entry were two words, *No indication*, and she put her thumb under them. She turned the page, and her thumb went to the foot of it and found them there. It found them at the foot of the next, in the same place, as if he had measured where they would go. Snow spotted the paper and didn't run the pencil. She quit reading the rest of each night and only turned and looked at the bottom, page after page, till the pages ran out on the morning she had gone out his door.

"He's asked me for a second location every night since Monday," Pike said. "Every night I've said no. There isn't one that doesn't cross open water."

Beth's thumb stopped.

"At four this morning he reported himself. I had a detail once that—" The line gave them nothing for a breath. "Nothing flies before first light tomorrow. Till then she's held in the most defensible place you have. Nothing else."

Beth brought her mouth down to the phone.

"I have a condition. Nothing about me goes on paper I haven't read. And everything you tell him about me, I hear."

Snow went across the phone and off it. Dane didn't move. Pike let the line run quiet.

"Then it's Giglio, Deputy."

"Copy," Dane said, and the call ended.

He held out his good hand. She gave him the phone and slid the notebook back inside her own coat against her ribs, and he thumbed the phone dark and put it away inside his coat, the wet running off its case and down his wrist while he did the button up over it.

* * *

The ham sat on the board by the open mess oven, heated through, and the heat off the oven door lay along her shins. The little oven ran off the boat's generator, and the steel room had filled with clove and hot sugar while the sleet ran down the deckhouse windows.

Dane worked one-armed and gave the orders. "Diamonds. Across one way, across the other, so the glaze has somewhere to sit."

She had her own knife out. She scored the fat in long lines down its length, a finger apart.

"Those are lines."

"Lines hold it the same."

He spooned the glaze on with his good hand, and the lines held it.

"Ned laid that in for the crew's Thanksgiving," she said.

"The ham is eleven pounds, and there are three of us."

"That's a load."

"It's lunch."

He cut the first slice off the end and held it out to her on the fork. It was too hot. She ate it anyway, standing in the oven's heat with her shins warm through Kenny's jeans, the glaze running at the edge, a clove driven into it, the salt underneath. Outside the weather kept on at the steel, and in here it smelled like somebody's kitchen on a holiday. Under the deck plates the pump ran on, and for once the boat lay quiet against the pier and asked nothing of her. She took a second piece off the board with her fingers and didn't apologize for it.

Dane set out three plates and frowned at the third. "Does the captain sit for this, or does he eat on his feet?"

"He'll sit. He eats anything that holds still. Fridays he has supper at Tess's."

"Every Friday." Dane set a fork by each plate, the third one last.

"Every Friday. And every New Year's he asks her to marry him."

"What does she say?"

"'Ask me sober, Haskell.'"

"How long's he been sober?"

"Twenty-two years."

Dane laughed into his good shoulder, and she nearly went with him. "He'll ask her this year."

"He asks her every year."

He had glaze at the corner of his mouth. She reached over and took it off with her thumb, and her thumb was slow coming away. The thing she wanted to ask him was ready, about after the barge. She put the knife down on the board and picked it up again.

"When—" She looked at the board. "When's it go back in?"

"Ten minutes," he said.

The deckhouse door banged, and Ned came in stamping, snow on the shoulders of the green jacket. He stopped at the sight of the ham.

"Wind's backing," he said. "Somebody expecting company?"

Dane was already carving him a slab off the end one-handed, the fork pinning it and the knife going through the glaze, sliding it onto the third plate while Ned let himself down onto the bench hip first, still in his jacket, his eyes still on the size of the thing.

* * *

The chart table was cold through her sleeves. The squalls had thinned, the wind backing northwest, the light going early. Snow-light lay flat on the wheelhouse windows. The *Margaret Ann* lay to her new mooring with the town landing square in front of her bow, and along the Harbor Road the store's windows were yellow on its generator.

"Here's how this goes," Dane said. "The night low, the fish house. The shore's dry from about eight."

In the lee of the pier wall, where the wind hadn't scoured it, one line of footprints still came down the pier road to the riprap. They ran back over the north road and down the cove road to the fish house door.

"Anybody can walk it backward," she said. "They burned Ashby."

"Then where?"

"Steel doesn't burn."

"Everything in it does. Your captain told me that the first night."

"Tonight the stern's watched, I'm awake, and the outside ladder's my way down."

She turned from the window. "Somebody tells them where I am tonight. So here's who. Tess logged my light on the back steps that first Friday night and sat on it. She sat on the county's call about the landing too. She's got hundreds in the green ledger against the night fuel. *Ashby*, on her hand, Tuesday morning. The truck keys hang on the hook and the town ring sits by the safe, behind a curtain that doesn't shut. Every midday she's out on her rounds with the store shut behind her. And Arsenault stood at that counter and told her, 'Store like this lives on the boat,' and Tess laughed."

She left the seal out. Her envelope had ridden over in Tess's bag, and the teller, as she read it, had written down the boat's.

"That's a threat," Dane said.

"To Tess."

"You read water better than anyone I've met," he said. "Who has a key when she's out?"

"Ned."

"Haskell."

"Ned."

"This isn't water," he said quietly.

"Not Ned."

She took the notebook out of her coat and opened it on the chart table at a clean page past his last line. She drew the pencil out of the spine. She wrote *Teresa Marr* in her own hand, in the square capitals the island school had taught her, the whole name, which she had never once said out loud. She turned the book round and pushed it across to him.

"For Pike."

The notebook lay open on the chart table between them, turned his way with her pencil uppermost on the page, and his good hand rested on the table beside it and didn't move to take it.

* * *

At dusk the high water had lifted the boat till the brow lay nearly level with the pier. The snow was over. The sky was clearing hard out of the northwest, and the cold came down out of it with the first stars. Down the pier the bollards wore caps of ice.

Beth had her bare hand on the brow's rail. The new ice on it was white under her fingers.

Ned came along the deck in the green jacket, going ashore. He had combed his beard. There was a clean collar under the jacket, buttoned to the top.

"Send Russ along the riprap after dark," she said. "Tell him the constable's badge goes on the outside of his coat tonight, where it shows. And not Tess. Not tonight."

Ned looked down the length of the brow. "Rail'll freeze hard by nine." He turned his collar up. "Not Tess," he said.

He went past her to the head of the brow. "Mind the ice, Bethie."

He went down the brow, and its wheels ground once on the granite. Then he was on the stone and going up the pier road toward the lit store, big and slow in the dusk, his breath going up white. He took the frozen ruts with care, the bad hip leading. Up the road the store's windows were yellow and steady, and smoke stood straight up off Tess's chimney beyond them. She stayed at the brow head with her eyes on the green jacket the whole way up. At the top of the pier road her hand was already coming up off the rail for his wave.

He went on round the corner of the Harbor Road toward the store, and her hand was up in the cold for nobody.

He didn't look back.
