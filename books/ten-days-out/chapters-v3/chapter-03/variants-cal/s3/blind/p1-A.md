They waited out the afternoon in her kitchen with the door locked. The north road went up over the spine of the island in the dark, and the snow squeaked under their boots. The Ashby key was cold through her glove. At the top she stopped and looked back at the harbor she'd left. Below, the houses were black except where a generator kept a window yellow, and out on the guest mooring the *Margaret Ann* lay lit end to end, as she had all through the blackout.

Then every deck light on the *Margaret Ann* went out at once, and the outer harbor went black below the hill.

They walked faster. He had moved to her harbor side, between her and the long drop to the shore, and his hand had gone to his hip and stayed there, and she didn't move him off it the rest of the way.

Ashby came up at the end of the road, a big shingled shape with water on three sides and the generator shed on its pad below the house. She dragged the shed door open on its frozen track, primed the engine by feel, knocked on the tank, which rang near empty, and put the cord in his hand. "Long and even, and don't yank it."

He pulled, and the engine coughed once and quit. He pulled again and got nothing but the rasp of the cord going home into the housing, and their two breaths hanging in the beam of the flashlight he'd stood on the fuel can.

"Third one," she said.

"Is that a rule?"

"It is for this one."

On the third pull it caught. It coughed and coughed and ran, ragged and then even, and the shed filled with the hammer of it. He grinned like a boy with a fish on. He stood with the cord handle still in his fist and his other hand pressed flat to his chest, as if he could feel the engine in there. Through the open door, up the slope, the big house's windows came up yellow behind the sheets.

"Is that what you look like when something works?"

He looked at her, a man who talked to anybody who'd stand still, and had nothing ready.

She had wanted that mouth since the razor this morning, and she was done standing at the stove about it.

She put her back to the engine housing. It was warm already through her coat and getting warmer, and it shook against her with every stroke. She took his jacket in her fist.

"Down here."

He came down as far as her fist brought him and stopped an inch off. He waited on the rest.

"Kiss me," she said.

His mouth was cold for a second and then warm. He kissed her slowly and kept it slow, as if the shed were his and he meant to spend the night in it. He tasted of the store's terrible coffee. She caught his lower lip in her teeth, and the breath went out of him through his nose, long.

"Beth," he said against her mouth, and nothing else.

She stripped off a glove and laid her bare hand along his jaw. Smooth still, from her father's razor. Hers now, cheek to chin, and she took her time over it.

"Your hands," she said.

He brought them up and stopped short of her zipper. They waited there.

"Where?"

"Under the wool."

She opened her own coat. The zipper stuck halfway on the wool, and she said a word to it, and he waited. His hands went in under the sweater and the thermal to the bare small of her back. They were so cold she swore into his mouth. He laughed, and she felt it against her teeth. He kept his hands where they were till they warmed.

The engine beat at her back. Stroke, and stroke. His hands moved up her spine and round over her ribs, and the old hauler's ridge across his palms caught on her skin.

"Higher."

He went higher and stopped with the thermal bunched on his wrists.

"Here?"

"Yes." She took the hem herself and shoved it up, sweater and all. The cold off the door went over her skin and drew it tight.

He looked. She let him look, in the yellow light from up the slope, with the door open on the dark. Nobody had looked at her like this in a long time, as if she were the thing that worked.

"Your mouth," she said. "Will you?"

"Yes."

He bent his head, and his mouth was hot after the air. She heard herself make a sound, and the engine took it. Then his mouth closed on her nipple, and she put her hand on the back of his head and held it there.

"Harder," she said, and he gave her that.

The housing shook at her shoulder blades. Out the door the snow went past the flashlight's beam and on into the dark. She had a hand in his belt and didn't know when she'd put it there.

She took it out. She found his wrist and brought his hand down over her stomach to the button of her jeans, and held it there.

"Will you?"

He lifted his head and looked at her face. "Do you want me to?"

"Yes. Open it."

He opened it one-handed. The zipper went down against the engine's beat, a tooth at a time. His hand was warm now, all of it. It went in under the denim and found her wet. He said her name, and it had a question in it.

"That," she said. "Keep on it."

He kept on it. Stroke. Stroke. The housing drove at her back. His fingers kept its time. Then she rocked into his hand until they kept hers.

Cold at the door. Heat at her back. His breath on her throat, short now, and nothing else out of him at all.

Up the slope the yellow windows swam and steadied.

She kept her eyes open. She took his face in her hand and turned it up from her throat, so that he would see her, and he saw. She let him. She came against the shaking steel with her heel braced on the pad. The sound that left her went into the engine's noise. He heard it anyway; she saw him hear it.

For a while she only breathed. The housing shook, and so did she. His hand lay quiet where it was. His forehead came down against hers, and he said nothing at all.

She wanted the rest of it. She got her fingers in his belt again and pulled him in.

He leaned in, and his jacket swung against her hip, and something in the inside pocket pressed into her through everything, square and hard. *Log daily*, and the other thing, the one he couldn't tell her.

She took her hands back, and he stopped at once. His hand came out of her jeans, and the cold went in after it.

"That's your notebook."

"Yes."

She pulled the thermal down. Her fingers shook on the button of her jeans, and it took her two tries, and she did it herself. She stepped back off the housing and zipped her coat to the chin.

"I'd rather be kissed by a man I can check. I read it this morning, Dane Reyes, and I didn't ask you twice. I'm asking now. What you write every day, and what's in it that I don't get told."

He stepped back a full pace, so the engine's noise stood between them.

"Don't write me down," she said.

He took it without arguing. "Ms. Calloway." He gave it a moment. "No. And I'm not going to keep talking till it sounds like yes."

The engine hammered on between them. Up the slope the yellow windows stood lit for nobody.

"That boat went dark," he said. "I have to call it in."

She shut the shed door on the engine and went up the slope ahead of him with Ned's key in her hand, and unlocked a house that smelled of mothballs and cold wallpaper, where the furniture stood about under sheets.

At the door he stopped. "North end. That's what I'll tell them, and nothing closer."

He went out onto the porch, to the top step. She would sooner watch his mouth than stand behind any sheet in that house, so she went out after him, pulled the door to, and stood on the step beside him in the snow.

He had the phone to his ear and his face turned up. Down the slope the generator hammered in its shed, and his voice went into it low; she could see his breath go up and come apart in the snow. Past him the water lay black round the point, and nothing showed on it.

He turned his head once in the middle of it and found her at his shoulder. His mouth stopped. Then he stepped round her on the step, so that he stood between her and the water, and went on talking, and she moved up the last few inches until her arm was against his.

"North end," he said into the phone. Below them the generator missed, and caught, and she lost the rest.
