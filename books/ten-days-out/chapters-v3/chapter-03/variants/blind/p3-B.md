# Chapter Three: The Generator Shed

They sat the rest of the night out behind the chimney, and nobody came back.

At first light she took the kettle out onto the back step for snow, and the print was at the foot of the steps, frozen into the slush: a clean right foot, and a left one with the heel dragged out behind it in a groove. She filled the kettle beside it.

The stove was lit at last, and the kettle had the window over the sink running with steam.

Dane stood at the counter drawing a thumb along his jaw.

She opened the cabinet over the sink, took down her father's razor, the heavy safety razor with the knurled handle, and set it on the drainboard without a word. She took down his shaving mirror too and propped it in the window frame where he used to prop it.

He poured kettle water into the basin and bent to the mirror. "Your father didn't want to see much of himself."

"Enough to shave."

He shaved slowly. He tipped his head back for the throat, and she watched the long line of it go taut under the lather, and the razor come up it in short strokes, and the cords in his forearm move when he rinsed the blade and tapped it twice on the edge of the basin, which was what her father had done, and which nobody had shown him. His sleeves were shoved up past the elbow, and his forearms were paler than his hands.

A man was in her father's kitchen, at her father's sink, being careful, and she was standing by the stove with a mug going cold in her hands and looking at his mouth when he drew his upper lip down for the razor. She told herself she was watching so he wouldn't nick himself on a blade she'd have to clean. That lasted until he turned his jaw to the light and ran his thumb along it to check, and she wanted it to be her thumb.

"He tapped it twice too," she said. "On the basin."

The razor stopped on his jaw. "I can quit doing that."

"Don't."

He wiped the razor dry, set it back on the drainboard, and went out to the yard with the phone. She didn't go to the window.

He came back in, set the phone face up on the table, lit, and went to the stove for the pot.

She picked it up and turned it to the window. Last night the same glow had been under his chin on the front step. One message was open on it.

*Pike: Q's request approved. Log daily. Do not disclose to witness.*

She pressed the arrow once with her thumb, and the next line came up.

*Firm at thirty-one. Saturday noon.*

He turned with the pot and saw it in her hand, and he shut his eyes for a second.

"Who's paying you thirty-one, and what don't I get told?"

"Nobody pays me but the government. The thirty-one's mine. The other one I can't tell you about. And I won't lie to you about whether there's something."

"Q."

"Homeland Security. Portland."

That was all, and she didn't ask again. A log, then: where they kept her and when, and the things about the case they never told a witness. She set the phone on the drainboard beside her father's razor and left the two of them there.

He turned to the stove to pour, his back to her, and stayed that way longer than the pouring needed.

"Then don't sit with your back to me."

She took the chair facing the door, the one he'd have taken, which left him the one with the door behind it. He stood with a mug in each hand and looked at it, and she watched him decide to sit in it.

* * *

Marr's was full, because in a storm everybody on Cutter came to the store, and out back the generator roared flat through Tess's office.

Dane had his coffee and his back to the hardware shelf. "Anybody know a man who drags his left heel? Leaves a groove in the slush."

Russ Whitcomb was leaning on the counter. "That narrows it to about forty of us."

"That's thirty-nine more than I was hoping for. What about the bridge cable?"

"Somebody cut that cable?" Russ asked the urn. "Somebody'd have to beat the rust to it. That tower's been on the district's list since Labor Day. I'll run her across to Pruett on the *Second Wind* the minute it lays down. Somebody ought to offer, so I'm offering."

"Thank you. Any boat in this harbor could be theirs, I can't read this water, and whoever runs her across leaves a wake the whole bay can follow."

"Suit yourself," Russ said, and went back to his coffee.

The bell went. Lyle Arsenault, who skippered the *Margaret Ann*, came in out of the snow in a good Musto jacket, clean-shaven, the only clean-shaven man on a dragger in the bay. A step behind him came a man with a black hood up, who stopped just inside the door and stood there with his hands in his pockets and snow melting on his shoulders. He didn't come to the counter. Nobody said his name.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having." Arsenault counted change onto the counter before Tess said the price.

"Put that away, Lyle. It's on your account." Tess pushed the coins back and laughed too loud, the laugh you could hear from the fuel float.

Arsenault turned to Beth, kind. "I bought fuel off your father when you were about this high. Good man. Bad luck with banks."

"Banks weren't luck."

Over the door the clock's hands came together at noon. Dane looked at it, then at Arsenault, and stayed where he was. The call he'd have had to make was out under the sky, and he wasn't leaving her in a room with the dragger's skipper. Beth watched him let the boat go.

The man at the door hadn't moved. Beth couldn't see his face. She could see the hood turned toward her, his hands in his pockets, the puddle spreading under his boots.

Arsenault tucked the Golds into his jacket. "Tess, this store's the heart of the island. I'd hate to see it have a hard winter." He went out.

The hooded man turned and went after him, and on the wet boards by the door his left heel dragged.

"Him," Beth said.

Dane was across the floor before the bell stopped, and Russ set his cup down and went after him.

Beth went to the gap in the office curtain, where Tess's window looked down the store dock. Going past, she saw the hook with the two keys for Tess's Ford, and the island's phones charging on the shelf under it. Down the dock Arsenault was handing himself into an aluminum skiff, and the hooded man took up the oars and pulled for the moorings. Dane stood at the head of the float with Russ and didn't go down onto it.

Behind her Tess was snapping a yellow tag through the zipper of the morning's deposit bag and talking the whole time. "Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color. Calloway, get out of my office."

Beth came back to the counter, and Tess leaned both hands on it.

"Net had you aboard," she said, low, which Tess never did. "Your back steps had you home, twenty-two forty." She held up the back of her left hand, and there it was in ballpoint among the gallons, *22:40*, and there again in the storm log open between them, in her square capitals. "I can see your steps from right here, Beth. Didn't put you on the air."

The store had always looked up at the back of her house.

Then, fast and bright: "You'll sleep at mine. The cot's made up, the stew's on, and the marshal can eat his on the porch."

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

Beth's eyes went down the night's column and stayed there a second too long, and Tess's voice went sharp. "Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

The bell went, and Dane came in with Russ behind him, snow on his shoulders, and Beth's palm was flat on the page, over 22:40, before she'd decided anything. She hadn't given him Ned's name last night, and she wouldn't give him Tess's hand now, and it was the same reason: they were hers, and you didn't put your own people in a marshal's book.

"He rowed," Dane said. "Didn't start the outboard till he was past the co-op."

"You could've had him on the float," Russ said.

"One of me against a dragger crew on the water is a body count, and I don't get to pick whose." He stopped at her elbow. "What am I looking at?"

"Nothing in it. Soup for Ida and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk."

He handed her the milk.

Tess's phone went in her vest. "Ida Dyer." She listened. "Pilot's call. I know it is." She dropped the phone back. "LifeFlight won't lift her. Nobody's flying off this rock."

"What is your job, exactly?" Dane said.

"Emergency management director. I was slow putting my hand down."

Beth found Ned outside, round the side wall of the store, with a cup from the urn.

"Ashby," she said.

The Ashby place was a Connecticut family's summer cottage on the north point, shut for the winter, with water on three sides and one road in, and Ned started its generator every storm. Her father's fish house had the cove over its sill and would till Monday.

He worked a key off his ring and put it in her glove. "Nobody but—"

"Me and God. I know."

Russ came round the corner and leaned on the shingles beside them, with Dane a step behind him. "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

* * *

The north road went up over the spine of the island in the dark, and the snow squeaked under their boots. The Ashby key was cold through her glove. At the top of the spine she looked back.

The harbor lay black below them, all but the *Margaret Ann*, lit end to end on her mooring as she had been all through the blackout. Then, while Beth watched, every deck light on the dragger went out at once, and the harbor was dark where it had been brightest.

Dane had stopped beside her. He looked at the place as long as she did. Then they went on, faster.

Ashby came up at the end of the road, a big shingled shape with water on three sides and the generator shed on its pad below the house. She dragged the shed door open on its frozen track, primed the engine by feel, knocked on the tank, which rang near empty, and put the cord in his hand. "Long and even, and don't yank it."

He pulled, and the engine coughed once and quit. He pulled again and got nothing but the rasp of the cord going home into the housing, and their two breaths hanging in the beam of the flashlight he'd stood on the fuel can.

"Third one," she said.

"Is that a rule?"

"It is for this one."

On the third pull it caught. It coughed and coughed and ran, ragged and then even, and the shed filled with the hammer of it, and she was watching his face when it happened. He grinned like a boy with a fish on. He stood with the cord handle still in his fist and his other hand pressed to his chest, as if he could feel the engine in there, and maybe he could. Through the open door, up the slope, the big house's windows came up yellow behind the sheets.

"Is that what you look like when something works?"

He looked at her, a man who had talked all day to Russ and Tess and the urn and the snow, and now he didn't say a word.

She put her back to the engine housing. It was warm already through her coat and getting warmer, and it shook against her with every stroke. She took his jacket in her fist and brought him down to her.

His mouth was cold for a second and then warm. He went still, the whole length of him, and then he kissed her back slowly, as if he had thought about this and meant to take his time over it. He tasted of the store's terrible coffee. She put her hand along his jaw where she'd watched the razor go that morning, and it was as smooth under her fingers as it had looked from the stove.

"Beth," he said against her mouth, and nothing else.

"Your hands," she said.

He brought them up and stopped an inch off her zipper, and they waited there.

"Where?"

"Under. Not over."

She opened her own coat. His hands went in under the sweater and the thermal and onto the bare small of her back, and they were so cold she swore into his mouth. He laughed, and she felt it against her teeth, and he kept his hands where they were, which was what she wanted.

Then they warmed and moved, up her spine and round over her ribs, a hauling callus at the base of his fingers catching on her skin, and the engine beat at her back and the cold came in at the door and the house stood lit up the hill, and she had both hands in his belt, pulling him in, and she noticed she was pulling and hadn't decided to, and that her mouth was asking for things before she'd thought them.

"Do you want—"

"Yes." He said it into her hair. Then, quieter: "Do you?"

"Yes."

He leaned in, and his jacket swung against her hip, and something in the inside pocket pressed into her through everything, square and hard: the phone. *Log daily*, and the other thing, the one he couldn't tell her.

She took her hands back, and he stopped at once. His hands came out from under the wool, and the cold went in after them.

"That's the phone," she said.

"Yes."

She stepped back off the housing and zipped her coat to her chin. "I'd rather be kissed by a man I can check."

Her voice had gone quiet. "I read it this morning, Dane Reyes. I put it down by my father's razor, and I didn't ask you twice. I'm asking now. What you write every day, that I don't get told. Am I in it?"

He didn't answer, and the engine hammered on between them.

"Don't write me down."

He took it without arguing. "Ms. Calloway." He said it slowly. "I can't give you that. I know what it costs you. I'm not going to make it smaller by talking."

He looked past her at the door, toward the south end of the island and the harbor they couldn't see from here. "That boat went dark. I have to call it in."

She shut the shed door on the engine and went up the slope ahead of him with Ned's key in her glove, and let them into a house that smelled of mothballs and cold wallpaper, where the furniture stood about under sheets. Somebody from Connecticut had tacked a sheet over the front window too, against the summer sun.

At the door he stopped. "North end. Not the house. That's what they'll get."

Then he went out onto the porch, to the top step under the open sky, and she took the edge of the sheet and held it back from the glass.

Snow came down between them through the porch light. He had the phone to his ear and his face turned up to the sky, and his mouth was moving, and she couldn't hear one word of it over the generator down the slope. She watched his breath go up and come apart in the snow.

North end, not the house: that was what he'd said he would say, and she had nothing to check it against but his mouth. South of them the dragger lay on the black harbor with every light out.

He turned once in the middle of it and looked straight at the window, at her, and went on talking. He was still looking when she let go, and the sheet dropped back over the glass between them, out of her opening hand.
