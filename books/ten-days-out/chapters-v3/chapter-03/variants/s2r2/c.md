
Two cot blankets sat folded by the till, and Beth knew they'd been there since before she came in.

Dane stopped at the door. "I'll be on the dock. I can see this counter from there."

Russ leaned on the door and let the snow in. "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

The bell rang them out.

Tess held her left hand up across the counter, the time in ballpoint on the knuckles. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home, and a light going on them. Twenty-two forty. I can see your steps from right here, Beth. I didn't put you on the air. If I can see them from my counter, so can anybody on that dock, or on any boat in this harbor. I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one. Cot's made up. You'll sleep at mine."

Through the front window, Dane stood at the dock rail with his back to the harbor.

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

Tess's mouth was open on the next thing when the bell went.

Arsenault came in stamping snow, Dane behind him. Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, the dragger lit all through the blackout.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having," Arsenault said.

"Coffee," Dane said.

Arsenault paid for both, and Tess went bright as she rang it.

The clock over the door came to noon. *Saturday noon* had been the Webbers Cove. Dane looked at the clock, then at Arsenault. The phone worked only outdoors, and outdoors meant leaving her with the dragger's skipper. He stayed and drank the coffee Arsenault had bought him.

Arsenault took his cigarettes, nodded to her and went out, and Dane went after him to see which way he walked.

Tess had the store bag out before the door shut, and yanked the yellow tag through the zipper so hard the bag jumped.

"Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color, and I won't have you in a house I can't see."

"I'm not your money, Tess."

"No. My money I can count. I put you to bed the winter your mother got on the Bangor bus."

"Then you know I don't go where I'm put."

Through the window, Dane was back at the rail.

Beth's eyes went down the night's column in the open log by the till and stayed.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

Tess took the bag through the curtain to the safe and left the curtain hanging open.

Through the gap, past Tess at the safe, Beth could see the back window and the propane cage beyond it. A man in a hood stood by the cage, facing in.

He turned away down the slope, his left foot coming after him a beat late.

Beth went to the front door and opened it. "Him," she said.

Dane came in off the rail running, Russ behind. "Russ, stay on her," Dane said going by, and went out the back.

Beth got as far as the back step. Russ took the office doorway behind her, in the generator's roar, with the lane and the counter both in sight. Below, the fish houses stood on their posts in the top of the tide. The hood went down the lane between two of them, sure of every plank. Dane went in after him, and past the lane's end the sea heaped up green on the ledge.

"Sea coming," Beth called down. "Stand still."

He stopped. He got an arm round a corner post, and the sea came up the lane white, went through to his knees and sucked back out. The man stepped up onto a sill he knew and was gone.

Dane stayed where she'd put him, black to the knee, both arms round the post.

Tess was back at the counter when Dane came through the curtain breathing hard, the sea running out of his boots.

Dane looked at the water spreading across her boards. "I owe you a floor, Ms. Marr. Put it on the government's account."

"Get a look at him? Didn't, did you," Russ said.

"He went in between them like he'd built them," Dane said. "I went in like a man looking for the restroom."

Tess laughed. "Restroom's for customers."

Beth put her palm flat on the open log, over the night's column. With her other hand she knocked the snow off his shoulder and turned his wet collar down. She hadn't given him Ned last night, and she wasn't giving him Tess now.

"What am I looking at?" Dane said, his eyes on her hand.

"Nothing in it. Soup for the shut-ins and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk," Beth said.

He handed her the milk.

Beth and Dane went out the front, round to the side wall out of the wind, and Russ stayed in. Ned was there with a cup from the urn. Dane kept the corner, with her and the moorings in sight.

"I need the Ashby key, Ned," Beth said.

"Bethie." Ned worked a key off his ring and put it in her glove. "I run her generator every storm anyhow. Nobody'll look twice at a light."

"Nobody but—" Ned said.

"Me and God. I know," Beth said.

She came round the front with the key in her glove. Through the window Tess took the two folded cot blankets off the counter and put them back under it, one and then the other. She didn't look up.
