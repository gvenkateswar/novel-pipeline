
Two cot blankets lay folded by the till, and they had been there before Beth came in.

Dane and Russ were at the door, going down to the store dock to look for the heel's track.

"Where you sleeping tonight?" Russ said from the doorway. "Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

The bell went behind them.

Tess held her left hand up across the counter, the time in ballpoint over the knuckles. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home, light and all. Twenty-two forty. I can see your steps from right here, Beth. I didn't put you on the air."

She turned the storm log round so it lay open between them in her writing. "Anybody on the dock can see what I see. Anybody on a boat in this harbor. I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one. Cot's made up. Marshal can eat on the porch. You'll sleep at mine."

"Can't," Beth said. "Not tonight, Tess."

Tess's mouth was open on the next thing when the bell went.

Arsenault came in a step ahead of Dane and Russ, stamping snow off good boots, his face shaved close. Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, the dragger that had stayed lit through the blackout.

"Pack of Golds," Arsenault said, "and whatever the gentleman's having."

"Coffee," Dane said.

Tess's face came up bright, and Arsenault paid for both.

Noon came up on the clock over the door. *Saturday noon*, the screen had said, for the Webbers Cove. The call needed open air, and open air meant leaving her at the counter beside the man who'd bought his coffee. Dane stayed where he was and drank it.

Arsenault nodded to Beth and went out.

Over Tess's shoulder, through the office curtain and the back window, a man in a hood stood by the propane cage, facing in. He turned away down the slope, and his left boot came after him through the snow a beat late.

"Him," Beth said.

"Russ. Stay on her," Dane said, and he was through the curtain.

Beth got as far as the back step, Russ at her shoulder in the generator's roar. Below, the top of the tide was in among the fish houses. The hood went down the lane between two of them, sure of every plank. Dane went in after him running, and past the end of the lane the sea heaped up green on the ledge.

"Sea coming," Beth called down the slope. "Stand still."

He stopped. He got an arm round a corner post, and the sea came up the lane white, went through to his knees and sucked back out at him. The man stepped up onto a sill he knew and was gone through a door.

Dane stood in the lane where she'd put him, jeans black to the knee, chest heaving, his face turned up the slope to her.

Russ took the office doorway as Beth went back in, where he could see the lane and the till, inside the roar.

Tess had the store bag in her hands and was talking before the curtain fell shut.

"That's what's out back of my store." Tess yanked the yellow tag through the zipper so hard the bag jumped. "Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color, and I won't have you in a house I can't see."

"I'm not your money, Tess," Beth said.

"No. My money I can count." Tess held the sealed bag up between them. "I put you to bed the winter your mother got on the Bangor bus."

"Then you know I don't go where I'm put," Beth said.

Tess went past Russ to the safe, threw the bag in, slammed it and came back. Beth's eyes had gone down the night's column in the open log.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does," Tess said.

The back door banged. Dane came in past Russ with snow on his shoulders and the sea running out of his boots.

"I owe you a floor, Ms. Marr," Dane said. "Put it on the government's account."

"Get a look at him?" Russ said from the doorway. "Didn't, did you."

"He went in between them like he'd built them," Dane said. "I went in like a man looking for the restroom."

"Restroom's for customers," Tess said.

Beth put her palm flat on the log, over the night's column. With her other hand she knocked the snow off his shoulder, and then her fingers found his wet collar and turned it down. She hadn't given him Ned last night, and she wasn't giving him Tess now.

"What am I looking at?" Dane said.

"Nothing in it. Soup for the shut-ins and who's short on propane," Beth said. "Hand me the milk."

He handed her the milk. Tess rang it up.

Beth and Dane went out the front and round to the side wall. Russ stayed in. Ned stood there with an urn cup steaming, and Dane kept the corner, where he could see her and the moorings.

"I need the Ashby key, Ned," Beth said.

"Bethie." Ned worked a key off his ring with his thumb and put it in her glove. "I run her every storm anyhow. Nobody'll look twice at a light."

"Nobody but—" Ned said.

"Me and God. I know," Beth said.

She came round the front of the store. Through the window Tess took the two cot blankets off the counter and put them back under it, one and then the other. She didn't look up.
