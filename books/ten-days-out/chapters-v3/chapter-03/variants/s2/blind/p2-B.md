# Chapter Three: The Generator Shed

They sat the rest of the night out behind the chimney, and nobody came back.

At first light she took the kettle out onto the back step for snow, and her boot came down beside the print at the foot of the steps, frozen hard in the slush: a clean right foot, and a left with the heel dragged out behind it in a groove.

Inside, the stove was lit at last, and the kettle had the window over the sink running with steam.

Dane stood at the counter drawing a thumb along his jaw. He had come over with nothing but what was on his back.

She opened the cabinet over the sink, took down her father's razor, the heavy safety razor with the knurled handle, and set it on the drainboard without a word. She took down his shaving mirror too and propped it in the window frame where he used to prop it.

He poured kettle water into the basin and bent to the mirror. "Your father didn't want to see much of himself."

"Enough to shave."

He shaved slowly. He tipped his head back for the throat, and she watched the long line of it go taut under the lather and the razor come up it in short strokes.

When he rinsed the blade, the cords in his forearm moved, and he tapped it twice on the edge of the basin, which was what her father had done and nobody had shown him. His sleeves were shoved up past the elbow, and his forearms were paler than his hands.

A man was in her father's kitchen, at her father's sink, being careful, and she was standing by the stove with a mug going cold in her hands and looking at his mouth when he drew his upper lip down for the razor. She told herself she was watching so he wouldn't nick himself on a blade she'd have to clean. That lasted until he turned his jaw to the light and ran his thumb along it to check, and she wanted it to be her thumb.

"He tapped it twice too," she said. "On the basin."

The razor stopped on his jaw. "I can quit doing that."

"Don't."

He wiped the razor dry on the dish towel, set it back on the drainboard, and went out to the yard with the phone. She didn't go to the window.

He came back in with snow melting on the screen, set the phone face up on the table to dry his hands, and went to the stove for the pot.

She picked it up and turned it to the window. Last night the same light had been under his chin on the front step while somebody stood in her mudroom, and now one message was open on it.

*Pike: Q's request approved. Log daily. Do not disclose to witness.*

She pressed the arrow once with her thumb, and the next line came up.

*Firm at thirty-one. Saturday noon.*

He turned with the pot and saw it in her hand, and he shut his eyes for a second.

"Who's paying you thirty-one, and what don't I get told?"

"Nobody pays me but the government. The thirty-one's mine." He gave her the rest without hurrying. "The other one I can't tell you about. And I won't lie to you about whether there's something."

"Q."

"Homeland Security. Portland."

He offered nothing past that, and she didn't ask again. A log, then: where they kept her and when, and the things about the case they never told a witness. She set the phone on the drainboard beside her father's razor and left the two of them there.

He turned to the stove to pour, his back to her, and stayed that way longer than the pouring needed.

"You're out of milk," he said, still pouring. "That much I can tell you."

"Then don't sit with your back to me."

She took the chair facing the door herself, the one he'd have taken. The other faced hers across the table and put the door behind him. He stood with a mug in each hand and looked at it, and she watched him decide to sit in it.

* * *


They were halfway down the path from her back steps to the store, Dane a stride ahead, when she heard it on Tess's back landing: a step, and then a drag, wet rubber pulled across planks.

She stopped. He stopped because she had.

A man stood by the propane cage at the foot of the landing, hood up, hands in the pouch of it, his face to the office door.

Beth put her hand out toward him and said nothing at all.

Dane looked where her hand went. "Stay," he said, low. "Right here."

The hood turned. The man went off the end of the landing and down onto the shore stones with his left heel scraping after him, and then Dane was running, and she was running after him, because it was her shore and he didn't know where the stones quit.

The tide was at the top. The sea had come up among the fish houses between the store dock and the co-op pier, thick with slush, and the lanes between the sheds were half water and half ice. The hood went into the second lane like a man going into his own kitchen. Beth, at the head of the lane, heard the next sea before she saw it, the long hiss of it drawing back off the ledges.

"Sea coming. Stand still."

He stood. He threw both arms round the corner post of the nearest fish house, and the sea came up the lane in one gray shove and went through him to the knee and sucked back out hard enough to roll the stones from under his boots. At the far end of the lane the hood stepped up onto a sill he knew, dry-shod, and went round the corner and was gone.

Dane stood where she'd put him, his jeans black to the thigh, his chest heaving, snow in his hair.

At the store's side wall Ned stood with a cup from the urn. He'd come up from the boat and seen them run. Dane went past him and in at the office door, dripping.

"Bethie," Ned said.

"I need the Ashby key, Ned."

He set the cup on the woodpile and worked the key off his ring. "I run her every storm anyhow. Nobody'll look twice at a light."

"Nobody but—"

"Me and God. I know."

She put it in her pocket and went in. In Tess's office the hook by the back door hung crooked from Dane's shoulder, and the two keys for Tess's Ford lay on the charging shelf on their MARR'S tag; she hung them back with her knuckles in the cords, among the island's phones, Ned's in its rubber band.

Out front Dane stood dripping on Tess's floor, and Russ leaned on the cooler beside him.

"Lost him, did you? Somebody'd have to have played in those houses as a boy."

"He went in between them like he'd built them, and I went in like a man looking for the restroom."

Tess laughed and put a coffee in front of him without asking.

Russ looked at Beth. "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

"She's sleeping at mine," Tess said.

Russ looked at her. "Is she, though? Doesn't look it."

"Russ." Dane picked up the coffee. "I'd like to see which boat he rows home to."

They went out the front, and the bell swung behind them.

Tess was on her before it stopped. "You ran after him. On my ice." She was stuffing envelopes into the morning's store bag. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home. Twenty-two forty." She held up her left hand. The time was on it in ballpoint, and in the storm log open between them. "I can see your steps from right here, Beth. I didn't put you on the air. And if I saw your light, who else did? You'll sleep at mine. There's the cot. The marshal can eat on the porch."

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

"I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one."

Tess zipped the bag and yanked the yellow tag through the pull so hard the plastic sang. "Your pay rides to Pruett in my bag every other week. Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color, and I won't have you in a house I can't see." The bag went into the safe, and she kicked it shut.

Beth's eyes had gone down the night's column.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

The bell. Dane came in from the front with snow on his shoulders and his jeans dark to the knee.

"Nothing rowing. He's gone to ground."

Her palm went flat on the log, over the night's column, before she had decided anything. Her other hand went up and knocked the snow off his shoulder, and stayed at his wet collar a beat longer than snow needed. She hadn't given him Ned last night, and she wasn't giving him Tess now.

"What am I looking at?"

"Nothing in it. Soup for the shut-ins and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk."

He reached into the cooler and handed her the milk. At the till, Tess had seen both hands.

The bell again. A tall man in a good jacket came in, shaved clean. Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, the dragger that had lain lit end to end through the whole blackout.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having." His money was on the counter, counted out, before Tess had turned to the rack.

"Put that away, Lyle. It's on your account."

He left it where it was. "Long winter for a store, no boat. Hard one, this looks like."

The clock over the door came up on noon. Dane looked at it, then at Arsenault, and stayed; the call would have meant the yard, and he wasn't leaving her at a counter with the dragger's skipper.

Arsenault took his cigarettes. "Calloway." A nod, and the bell.

The minute hand went past twelve. Dane stood at the counter, wet to the knee, and lifted the coffee to his mouth, and didn't go out. Arsenault's money lay counted on the counter where Tess hadn't touched it.


* * *

The north road went up over the spine of the island in the dark, and the snow squeaked under their boots. The Ashby key was cold through her glove. At the top she stopped and looked back at the harbor she'd left. Below, the houses were black except where a generator kept a window yellow, and out on the visitors' mooring the *Margaret Ann* lay lit end to end, as she had all through the blackout.

Then every deck light on the *Margaret Ann* went out at once, and the outer harbor went black below the hill.

Nobody said anything. They walked faster. He had moved to her harbor side, between her and the long drop to the shore, and his hand had gone to his hip and stayed there, and she let him walk there the rest of the way.

Ashby came up at the end of the road, a big shingled shape with water on three sides and the generator shed on its pad below the house. She dragged the shed door open on its frozen track, primed the engine by feel, knocked on the tank, which rang near empty, and put the cord in his hand. "Long and even, and don't yank it."

He pulled, and the engine coughed once and quit. He pulled again and got nothing but the rasp of the cord going home into the housing, and their two breaths hanging in the beam of the flashlight he'd stood on the fuel can.

"Third one," she said.

"Is that a rule?"

"It is for this one."

On the third pull it caught. It coughed and coughed and ran, ragged and then even, and the shed filled with the hammer of it, and she was watching his face when it happened. He grinned like a boy with a fish on. He stood with the cord handle still in his fist and his other hand pressed flat to his chest, as if he could feel the engine in there. Through the open door, up the slope, the big house's windows came up yellow behind the sheets.

"Is that what you look like when something works?"

He looked at her, a man who had talked all day to Russ and Tess and the snow, and now he didn't say a word.

She put her back to the engine housing. It was warm already through her coat and getting warmer, and it shook against her with every stroke. She took his jacket in her fist and brought him down to her.

His mouth was cold for a second and then warm. He went still, the whole length of him, and then he kissed her back slowly, and kept it slow. He tasted of the store's terrible coffee. She caught his lower lip in her teeth, and the breath went out of him through his nose, long and slow. She pulled off a glove and put her bare hand along his jaw where she'd watched the razor go that morning, and it was as smooth under her fingers as it had looked from the stove.

"Beth," he said against her mouth, and nothing else.

"Your hands," she said.

He brought them up and stopped an inch off her zipper, and they waited there.

"Where?"

"Under. Not over."

She opened her own coat. The zipper stuck halfway on the wool, and she said a word to it, and he waited. His hands went in under the sweater and the thermal and onto the bare small of her back, and they were so cold she swore into his mouth. He laughed, and she felt it against her teeth, and he kept his hands where they were, which was what she wanted.

Then they warmed, and moved, up her spine and round over her ribs, the old hauler's ridge across his palms catching on her skin, and the engine beat at her back and the cold came in at the door and the house stood lit up the hill, and she had both hands in his belt, pulling him in, and she hadn't decided to, and her mouth was asking for things before she'd thought them.

"Do you want—" she said.

"Yes." He said it into her hair. Then, quieter: "Do you?"

"Yes," she said.

His hands spread over her ribs and stopped there, his thumbs still, waiting on her. She put her own hand over one of them through the wool and held it to her.

He leaned in, and his jacket swung against her hip, and something in the inside pocket pressed into her through everything, square and hard. *Log daily*, and the other thing, the one he couldn't tell her.

She took her hands back, and he stopped at once. His hands came out from under the wool, and the cold went in after them.

"That's the phone."

"Yes."

She stepped back off the housing and zipped her coat to the chin.

"I'd rather be kissed by a man I can check. I read it this morning, Dane Reyes, and I didn't ask you twice. I'm asking now. What you write every day, and what's in it that I don't get told."

He stepped back a full pace, so the engine's noise stood between them.

"Don't write me down," she said.

He took it without arguing. "Ms. Calloway." He let that stand a moment. "No. And I'm not going to keep talking till it sounds like yes."

The engine hammered on between them. Up the slope the yellow windows stood lit for nobody.

"That boat went dark," he said. "I have to call it in."

She shut the shed door on the engine and went up the slope ahead of him with Ned's key in her hand, and let them into a house that smelled of mothballs and cold wallpaper, where the furniture stood about under sheets.

At the door he stopped. "North end. Not the house. That's what I'll tell them."

He went out onto the porch, to the top step under the open sky. She would sooner watch his mouth than stand behind any sheet in that house, so she went out after him, pulled the door to, and stood on the step beside him in the snow.

He had the phone to his ear and his face turned up. Down the slope the generator hammered in its shed, and his voice went into it low; she could see his breath go up and come apart in the snow. Past him the water lay black round the point, and nothing showed on it.

He turned his head once in the middle of it and found her at his shoulder. His mouth stopped. Then he stepped round her on the step, so that he stood between her and the water, and went on talking, and she moved up the last few inches until her arm was against his, and stayed there in the snow while he told Portland, in words she couldn't hear, where she was.
