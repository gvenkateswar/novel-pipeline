They were halfway down the path from her back steps to the store, Dane a stride ahead, when she heard it on Tess's back landing: a step, and then a drag, wet rubber pulled across planks.

She stopped. He stopped because she had.

A man stood by the propane cage at the foot of the landing, hood up, hands in the pouch of it, his face to the office door.

Beth put her hand out toward him and said nothing at all.

Dane looked where her hand went. "Stay," he said, low. "Right here."

The hood turned. The man went off the end of the landing and down onto the shore stones with his left heel scraping after him, and then Dane was running, and she was running after him, because it was her shore and he didn't know where the stones quit.

The tide was at the top. The sea had come up among the fish houses between the store dock and the co-op pier, thick with slush, and the lanes between the sheds were half water and half ice. The hood went into the second lane like a man going into his own kitchen. Beth, at the head of the lane, heard the next sea before she saw it, the long hiss of it drawing back off the ledges.

"Sea coming. Stand still."

He stood. He threw both arms round the corner post of the nearest fish house, and the sea came up the lane in one gray shove and went through him to the knee and sucked back out hard enough to roll the stones from under his boots. At the far end of the lane the hood stepped up onto a sill he knew, dry-shod, and went round the corner and was gone.

Dane stood where she'd put him, his jeans black to the thigh, his chest heaving, snow in his hair.

At the store's side wall Ned stood with a cup from the urn. He'd come up from the boat and seen them run. Dane went past him and in at the office door, dripping.

"Bethie," Ned said.

"I need the Ashby key, Ned."

He set the cup on the woodpile and worked the key off his ring. "I run her every storm anyhow. Nobody'll look twice at a light."

"Nobody but—"

"Me and God. I know."

She put it in her pocket and went in. In Tess's office the hook by the back door hung crooked from Dane's shoulder, and the two keys for Tess's Ford lay on the charging shelf on their MARR'S tag; she hung them back with her knuckles in the cords, among the island's phones, Ned's in its rubber band.

Out front Dane stood dripping on Tess's floor, and Russ leaned on the cooler beside him.

"Lost him, did you? Somebody'd have to have played in those houses as a boy."

"He went in between them like he'd built them, and I went in like a man looking for the restroom."

Tess laughed and put a coffee in front of him without asking.

Russ looked at Beth. "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

"She's sleeping at mine," Tess said.

Russ looked at her. "Is she, though? Doesn't look it."

"Russ." Dane picked up the coffee. "I'd like to see which boat he rows home to."

They went out the front, and the bell swung behind them.

Tess was on her before it stopped. "You ran after him. On my ice." She was stuffing envelopes into the morning's store bag. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home. Twenty-two forty." She held up her left hand. The time was on it in ballpoint, and in the storm log open between them. "I can see your steps from right here, Beth. I didn't put you on the air. And if I saw your light, who else did? You'll sleep at mine. There's the cot. The marshal can eat on the porch."

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

"I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one."

Tess zipped the bag and yanked the yellow tag through the pull so hard the plastic sang. "Your pay rides to Pruett in my bag every other week. Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color, and I won't have you in a house I can't see." The bag went into the safe, and she kicked it shut.

Beth's eyes had gone down the night's column.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

The bell. Dane came in from the front with snow on his shoulders and his jeans dark to the knee.

"Nothing rowing. He's gone to ground."

Her palm went flat on the log, over the night's column, before she had decided anything. Her other hand went up and knocked the snow off his shoulder, and stayed at his wet collar a beat longer than snow needed. She hadn't given him Ned last night, and she wasn't giving him Tess now.

"What am I looking at?"

"Nothing in it. Soup for the shut-ins and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk."

He reached into the cooler and handed her the milk. At the till, Tess had seen both hands.

The bell again. A tall man in a good jacket came in, shaved clean. Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, the dragger that had lain lit end to end through the whole blackout.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having." His money was on the counter, counted out, before Tess had turned to the rack.

"Put that away, Lyle. It's on your account."

He left it where it was. "Long winter for a store, no boat. Hard one, this looks like."

The clock over the door came up on noon. Dane looked at it, then at Arsenault, and stayed; the call would have meant the yard, and he wasn't leaving her at a counter with the dragger's skipper.

Arsenault took his cigarettes. "Calloway." A nod, and the bell.

The minute hand went past twelve. Dane stood at the counter, wet to the knee, and lifted the coffee to his mouth, and didn't go out. Arsenault's money lay counted on the counter where Tess hadn't touched it.
