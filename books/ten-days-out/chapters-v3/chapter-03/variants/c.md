# Chapter Three: The Generator Shed

They sat the rest of the night out behind the chimney, and nobody came back.

At first light she took the kettle out to the back step to pack it with snow, and set her boot down beside the print at the foot of the steps: a clean right foot, and a left one with the heel dragged out behind it in a groove, frozen hard where he'd stood.

Inside, she lit the stove at last, and the kettle had the window over the sink running with steam.

Dane stood at the counter drawing a thumb along his jaw. His bag in the front room was the size a man packed for one night.

She opened the cabinet over the sink, took down her father's razor, the heavy safety razor with the knurled handle, and set it on the drainboard without a word. She took down his shaving mirror too and propped it in the window frame where he used to prop it.

He poured kettle water into the basin and bent to the mirror. "Your father didn't want to see much of himself."

"Enough to shave."

He shaved slowly. He tipped his head back for the throat, and she watched the long line of it go taut under the lather, and the razor come up it in short strokes, and the cords in his forearm move when he rinsed the blade and tapped it twice on the edge of the basin, which was what her father had done, and which nobody had shown him. His sleeves were shoved up past the elbow, and his forearms were paler than his hands.

A man was in her father's kitchen, at her father's sink, being careful, and she was standing by the stove with a mug going cold in her hands and looking at his mouth when he drew his upper lip down for the razor. She told herself she was watching so he wouldn't nick himself on a blade she'd have to clean. That lasted until he turned his jaw to the light and ran his thumb along it to check, and she wanted it to be her thumb.

"He tapped it twice too," she said. "On the basin."

The razor stopped on his jaw. "I can quit doing that."

"Don't."

He wiped the razor dry on the dish towel, set it back on the drainboard, and went out to the yard with the phone. She didn't go to the window.

He came back in, set the phone face up on the table with the screen still lit, and went to the stove for the pot.

She picked it up and tipped it to the gray light from the window. Last night the same light had been under his chin on the front step while somebody stood in her mudroom, and now one message was open on it.

*Pike: Q's request approved. Log daily. Do not disclose to witness.*

She pressed the arrow once with her thumb, and the next line came up.

*Firm at thirty-one. Saturday noon.*

He turned with the pot and saw it in her hand, and he shut his eyes for a second.

"Who's paying you thirty-one, and what don't I get told?"

"Nobody pays me but the government. The thirty-one's mine." He gave her the rest without hurrying. "The other one I can't tell you about. And I won't lie to you about whether there's something."

"Q."

"Homeland Security. Portland."

He gave her nothing past that, and she didn't ask for it. A log, then: where they kept her and when, and the things about the case they never told a witness. She set the phone on the drainboard beside her father's razor and left the two of them there.

He turned to the stove to pour, his back to her, and stayed that way longer than the pouring needed.

"Then don't sit with your back to me," she said.

She took the chair facing the door herself, the one he'd have taken. The other faced hers across the table and put the door behind him. He stood with a mug in each hand and looked at it, and she watched him decide to sit in it.

* * *

Marr's was full, because in a storm everybody on Cutter came to the store, and out back the generator's roar came through Tess's office and under the counter into the soles of Beth's boots.

Dane had his coffee and his back to the hardware shelf. "Anybody know a man who drags his left heel? Leaves a groove in the slush."

Russ Whitcomb was leaning on the counter. "That narrows it to about forty of us."

"That's thirty-nine more than I was hoping for. And the bridge cable. I've been thinking about it since it went over my head."

"Somebody cut that cable?" Russ asked the coffee urn. "Somebody'd have to beat the rust to it. That tower's been on the district's list since Labor Day." He turned his cup a quarter on the counter. "I'll run her to Pruett on the *Second Wind* the minute it lays down. Say the word."

"Thank you. I can't, because any boat on this island could be theirs, and I can't read this sea well enough to know a bad one when I'm standing on it, and whoever takes her across leaves a wake the whole bay can follow."

Russ nodded at that as if it were the price of bait and went back to his coffee. He didn't look put out. Beth watched him not look it.

The bell went. A tall man came in out of the snow in a good Musto jacket, clean-shaven, the only clean-shaven man on a dragger in the bay. Lyle Arsenault, who skippered the *Margaret Ann*, the Gloucester boat that had stayed lit through the blackout.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having." He counted coins onto the counter before Tess had said a price.

"Put that away, Lyle. It's on your account." Tess pushed the coins back across and laughed, too loud, the laugh you could hear from the fuel float.

He turned to Beth, kind. "I bought fuel off your father when you were about this high. Good man. Bad luck with banks."

"Banks weren't luck."

Over the door the clock's hands came together at noon. Dane looked at it and stayed where he was, against the shelf, with his coffee. Any call he made would have to be made outside under the sky, and he wasn't leaving her in a room with the dragger's skipper. Beth had read the screen that morning. She watched him let the boat go.

The curtain to Tess's office hung half open behind the counter, and past Tess's shoulder Beth could see straight through the office to its back window. A man in a hood stood out there by the propane cage at the foot of the back step, hands in his pockets, facing in. Waiting. Then he turned away along the cage, and his left heel dragged a groove in the snow.

"Him."

Dane was through the curtain before Tess could open her mouth. Beth got as far as the office doorway: the hook with the two keys for Tess's Ford on their MARR'S tag, the phones charging on the shelf under it, the back door banging open on the wind, and the back step empty. Then Dane was gone round the cage after the groove.

Russ set his cup down and went out the front.

Arsenault tucked his Golds into the Musto. He didn't hurry. "Tess, this store's the heart of the island." He pulled the door open on the snow. "I'd hate to see it have a hard winter."

The bell swung behind him.

Tess was already sealing the morning bag, snapping a yellow tag through the zipper, fast, talking to Beth, to the bag, to the empty store. "Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color. Calloway, you look like a wet cat."

Then she leaned both hands on the counter.

"Net had you aboard." Low, which Tess never was. "Your back steps had you home, twenty-two forty." She held up the back of her left hand. There in ballpoint among the gallons was *22:40*, and there it was again in the storm log open on the counter between them, in her square capitals. "I can see your steps from right here, Beth. Didn't put you on the air."

Beth looked past her, out the front window and up the hill. Her kitchen window looked down on this store, and the store looked up at the back of her house, and it always had. Every night of her life somebody behind this counter had known when her light went off.

"You'll sleep at mine. The cot's made up, the stew's on, and the marshal can eat his on the porch."

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

Beth's eyes went down the night's column in the log and stayed there a second too long, and Tess's voice went sharp as a gaff. "Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

The back door banged. Dane came through the curtain with snow to the knee and his breath going hard, and Beth's palm was flat on the page, over 22:40, before she'd decided anything. She hadn't given him Ned's name last night, and she wouldn't give him Tess's hand now, and it was the same reason: they were hers, and you didn't put your own people in a marshal's book.

"Lost him in the fish houses on the co-op side." He stopped at her elbow. "He went between them like he'd been doing it since he was six. Everybody on this island walks that shore like that except me." He looked down. "What am I looking at?"

"Nothing in it. Soup for Ida and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk."

He handed her the milk.

Tess's phone went off in her vest. She took it on her shoulder and rang up a can of soup with her free hand. "Pilot's call, I know it is. EMT stays with her, then." She dropped it back in the vest. "LifeFlight won't lift Ida Dyer. If they won't fly for Ida, nobody's flying off this rock."

Dane looked at her over his cup. "What is your job, exactly?"

"Emergency management director. I was slow putting my hand down."

Ned was round the side wall of the store with a cup from the urn and nobody in earshot.

"Ashby," Beth said.

The Ashby place was a big summer cottage on the north point that belonged to people from Connecticut, shut up for the winter, with water on three sides and one road in. Ned started its generator every storm, so a generator running out there would only look like Ned. Her father's fish house had the cove over its sill and would till Monday.

He worked a key off his ring and put it in her glove. "Nobody but—"

"Me and God. I know."

Russ came round the corner and leaned on the shingles beside them, with Dane a step behind him. "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

* * *

The north road went up over the spine of the island in the dark, and the snow squeaked under their boots. Ned's key was cold through her glove. Along the east shore below them the houses were black except where a generator kept a kitchen window yellow, and past them the ledges showed white where the sea broke.

She heard it before she found it: an outboard, throttled down, somewhere inside the ledges, where nobody ran at night in this. Then she had it. A skiff with no light, a darker thing on dark water, coming up the east shore close in, its wake pale behind it. It ran past the mouth of Calloway Cove and on toward the north point, and off the point it slowed, and the engine dropped to almost nothing.

Dane had stopped on her left, his hand at his hip. "I see it," he said, low. "It's slowing. It's looking at water, not at the road. Stand still and we're two fence posts."

The skiff lay off the point for the length of three seas on the outer ledge. Then the engine came up, and it swung and went back down the shore the way it had come, without a light, until the snow had the sound of it, and then the wake.

She walked on faster, and he kept up.

Ashby came up at the end of the road, a big shingled shape with every window dark. The generator shed stood on its pad below the house. She dragged its door open on the frozen track.

She primed it by feel and knocked on the tank, which rang near empty, and put the cord in his hand. "Long and even, and don't yank it."

He pulled, and the engine coughed once and quit. He pulled again and got nothing but the rasp of the cord going home into the housing. Under the rasp she was listening for an outboard.

"Third one," she said.

"Is that a rule?"

"It is for this one."

On the third pull it caught. It coughed and coughed and ran, ragged and then even, and the shed filled with the hammer of it, and she was watching his face when it happened. He grinned like a boy with a fish on. He stood with the cord handle still in his fist and his other hand pressed to his chest, as if he could feel the engine in there. Up the slope the big house's windows came up yellow behind their sheets.

"Is that what you look like when something works?"

He didn't say a word.

She put her back to the engine housing. It was warm already through her coat and getting warmer, and it shook against her with every stroke. She took his jacket in her fist and brought him down to her.

His mouth was cold for a second and then warm. He kissed her back slowly, as if he had thought about this and meant to take his time over it. He tasted of the store's terrible coffee. She put her hand along his jaw where she'd watched the razor go that morning, and it was as smooth under her fingers as it had looked from the stove.

"Beth," he said against her mouth, and nothing else.

"Your hands," she said.

He brought them up and stopped an inch off her zipper, and they waited there.

"Where?"

"Under. Not over."

She opened her own coat. His hands went in under the sweater and the thermal and onto the bare small of her back, and they were so cold she swore into his mouth. He laughed, and she felt it against her teeth, and he kept his hands where they were, which was what she wanted.

Then they warmed and moved, up her spine and round over her ribs, the old callus at the base of his fingers catching on her skin, a hauler's callus, and the engine beat at her back and the cold came in at the door, and she had both hands in his belt pulling him in, and she noticed she hadn't decided to, and that her mouth was asking for things before she'd thought them, and she let it.

"Do you want—"

"Yes." He said it into her hair. Then, quieter: "Do you?"

"Yes."

He leaned in, and his jacket swung against her hip, and something in the inside pocket pressed into her through everything, square and hard: the phone. *Log daily*, and the other thing, the one he couldn't tell her. In a minute it would have a skiff in it, and this road.

She took her hands back, and he stopped at once. His hands came out from under the wool, and the cold went in after them.

"That's the phone," she said.

"Yes."

She stepped off the housing and zipped her coat to the chin before she said anything else.

"I'd rather be kissed by a man I can check." Her voice had gone quiet and exact. "I read it this morning, Dane Reyes. I put it down by my father's razor, and I didn't ask you twice. I'm asking now. What you write every day. Am I in it?"

He didn't answer.

"Don't write me down."

He took it without arguing. "Ms. Calloway." Slow, and very polite. "I can't give you what you're asking for. I know what that costs you. I won't make it smaller by talking."

He looked past her, out the door toward the point. "That skiff came up and had a look at the north end and went home. I have to call it in."

She shut the shed on the engine and let them into the house with Ned's key, into mothballs and cold wallpaper and furniture under sheets. Somebody from Connecticut had tacked a sheet over the front window too.

At the door he stopped. "North end. Not the house. That's what I'll tell them." He nodded at the sheet. "You can watch me from there."

He went out onto the porch, to the top step under the open sky. The edge of the sheet hung by her hand. She went out the door after him instead, and pulled it to behind her, and stood on the step beside him in the snow.

He had the phone to his ear and his face turned up. Down the slope the generator hammered in its shed, and his voice went into it low; she could see his breath go up and come apart in the snow, and not one word of what went out on it. North end, not the house. She had his word for that.

He turned once in the middle of it to look at the window and found her at his shoulder instead. His mouth stopped. Then he went on talking, and she moved up the last few inches until her arm was against his, and stayed there in the snow while he told Portland where she was.
