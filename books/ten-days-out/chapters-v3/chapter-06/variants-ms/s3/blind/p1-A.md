Glass flashed on the *Margaret Ann*'s wheelhouse, far down over the low ground. Once. Then again.

Dane had the phone at his ear and his back to the wind. The wind came over the ledge with nothing in its way and took every word before it reached her.

"Down," Beth said.

He went down on one knee below the line of the rock and finished the call crouched there, writing on his knee. The glass didn't flash again.

"Pike?"

"Pike. Weather."

He gave her the rest going down off the granite, a hand on the wet rock between sentences. "Coast Guard's sending a lifeboat at first light tomorrow to the town float, because it's the only lee in this swell, and their helicopter's offshore on a case. The inspector rides over on her for the ferry, you ride back with them, and the landing's cleared with the county."

Beth stopped on the last of the granite. The county would ring the island. The island had one person to ring.

"You told the county?"

"Portland did. Before they told me."

She stepped down onto the shore under the bank. The ebb had drawn off the ledges, and the weed lay flat on them, shining, all the way toward town.

"Going aboard," she said.

"That's the most watched boat in this harbor."

"I crew her. I don't leave her without telling her master."

He walked a few steps on the weed. "You tell Haskell you're going. Here's who knows the hour. Nobody. Not Tess, not Russ." A few more steps. "Nobody. Not Ned."

"Nobody," she said.

Where the shore turned, the low ground opened onto the inner harbor, and the bank fell away from over them. The *Margaret Ann* was off her guest mooring. She lay on a new one, closer in, with the town landing square in front of her bow. A man came aft along her rail, the left heel dragging, and stopped. He stood facing the shore.

"Moorings swing," Dane said.

"That isn't swinging."

Glass winked at the man's face.

Dane's hand closed on her sleeve. "Back under the bank. We sit in the cove till the night low and come round in the dark."

Beth stood at the water's edge and looked at it. Behind them lay a mile and a quarter of open weed back to Calloway Cove. If they turned now, the glass came with them, every step to her father's door. If they went flat on the rock, that was what the man was standing on the rail to see. Ahead lay the riprap and the stone pier.

"Walk," she said. "Hands out of your pockets. Don't look at her."

"Walking." He came up beside her, easy, his voice low and even. "Hands out. Eyes on the weed. I'm a man who's been warned about the weed and took it to heart, which is true."

They went along the open shore at an islander's pace, upright, in full view of the dragger. Beth kept her eyes on her footing. Each time she lifted them, the man on the rail had come round with them, a little at a time, the way a compass card follows a boat's head.

The last ledge before the road was all weed. Dane's hand came under her elbow and stayed there till she had rock.

"Tomorrow we go down to look," Dane said. "Anything wrong at the ramp head, we turn round."

"I'll know before you do."

"You will."

Ned had the brow out at the stone pier. From the top of it Beth looked across the harbor once. The man was still on the dragger's rail, square to the pier now, and he watched her step aboard the *North Reach*. Dane went straight up the outside ladder to the wheelhouse to watch the pier.

Beth heard the forepeak before she saw it. The pump had quit. The boat had gone quiet under her boots.

Below the open hatch the water was over the floor plates and working at the second rung. Ned stood in it to his shins with the old impeller in his hand, its vanes chewed to stubs.

She went down. At the second rung the cold came through her boots and kept coming. The forepeak smelled of rust and cold paint.

"Glass is up," Ned said. He looked at the water round his shins. "So's this."

"Pump's not."

"New one's in the mess." He handed her the dead one and went up the ladder a rung at a time, both fists on the rails.

At the top he stopped, his head and shoulders in the light of the hatch, and looked back down at her. Then he was gone.

The cold got into the bones of her fingers and ached down to the wrist.

Ned came down with a box. Marr's price sticker was on the end of it.

He knelt in the water at the housing. She held the new impeller in place, both hands on the cold steel, while he turned it to seat it.

"She's eaten three since Sunday," Ned said. "Crack's no worse. Inspector'll want her dry when he comes."

He got the first bolt in.

"That unit was dark Thursday," Beth said.

"He always runs his unit. Thermo King, he runs it continuous for live product, every Thursday since the Fourth of July two years back, you can hear it from the wheelhouse."

Beth kept her hands where they were. The water moved against her shins. He got the second bolt in.

"Crates come open now and again," Ned said. "You know they do. A year back I started seeing too many pieces to the crate for the weight. Figured it was shorts. Boxes rode heavy, and I never gave that a thought. That contract's the winter boat. Lose it, the district runs her three days a week. Somebody gets laid off, and it wouldn't be me. So I never said."

He set the wrench down on the housing. "I thought it was shorts, Bethie."

She took her hand off the impeller and put it on his arm, on the wet green wool, a second. Then she picked up the wrench and gave it back to him.

He threw the switch. The pump coughed, caught and ran. The water started down the rung.

Under the bank, not an hour back, she had said Nobody, and the marshal had heard her say it. Asking Ned was asking the boat about the water. Ned's the boat.

"Is the float workable on the ebb?"

"Which tide?" Ned said.

"Tomorrow's first."

He sat back on his heels in the water. "Glass'll be falling again by morning. There's a second blow behind this one, and the swell comes in ahead of it." He wiped his hands down his jacket and reached past her for the tide table tacked on the bulkhead. "Early in the ebb she rides fine. Bottom of it, the inshore end sets down on the mud and that ramp stands up like a ladder. Ledges under the bank go dry behind it, all but the last one." He ran a wet finger down Thursday's column. "That one's still deep in the dark."
