Wednesday's first sun lay flat on the ledge with no warmth in it worth the name.

The phone went off in Dane's jacket. He answered it with his back to the wind, and the wind took every word before it reached her.

Past his shoulder the *Margaret Ann* sat on her guest mooring. Glass flashed on her wheelhouse, once, then again.

"Down," Beth said.

He finished the call on one knee, below the line of the rock.

"Pike?"

"Pike. Weather." He gave her the rest going down off the rock. "Coast Guard's sending a lifeboat at first light tomorrow to the town float, because it's the only lee in this swell, and it's bringing the inspector over for the ferry, and you go back aboard it, since the district's helicopter is offshore on a case. The landing's cleared with the county."

On the weed she stopped. The county would ring the island. The island had one person to ring.

"You told the county?"

"Portland did. Before they told me."

Tess had been slow putting her hand down at town meeting. Beth put that away and went on.

"Boat first," she said. "Ned hears I'm going, and he hears it from me."

Dane walked beside her on the dry rock. "He can hear that much."

"Here's who knows the hour," he said. "Nobody. Not Tess, not Russ." He walked a few steps. "Nobody. Not Ned."

"Nobody," she said.

Where the shore turned, the low ground opened onto the inner harbor, and they were in the open. The *Margaret Ann* was off her guest mooring. She lay on a new one, closer in, with the town landing square in front of her bow. A man came aft along her rail with the left heel dragging. He stopped there, facing the shore.

"Moorings swing," Dane said.

"That isn't swinging."

"Then we back up under the bank," Dane said, "find a dry rock, and wait for him to go below."

Beth stood at the edge of the water. Back under the bank was back toward the cove and the fish house, every yard of it under that glass. A man who saw two people turn round would want to know where they went. A man who saw them get down behind a rock would know why.

"Walk," she said. "Slow. You've lived here all your life."

She went on along the tide line upright, at the pace Russ used going down to his skiff, and Dane came on the seaward side of her. Where the weed was worst his hand came under her elbow and stayed till she had rock.

"I'm walking like a man who owes the store money," he said, low. "He's turning with us. Still turning."

The man on the stern turned with them all the way along the riprap to the stone pier.

"Tomorrow we go down to look. Anything wrong at the ramp head, we turn round."

"I'll know before you do."

"You will."

Dane went straight up the outside ladder to the wheelhouse to watch the pier. From the gate Beth could see the man on the dragger's stern, his face to the *North Reach* now.

Beth heard the forepeak before she saw it. The pump had quit.

The hatch stood open. Below it the water was up over the floor plates and working at the second rung of the ladder. Ned was down in it to his shins with the old impeller in his hand, its vanes chewed to stubs.

"Glass is up," Ned said.

She went down the ladder. The cold came through her boots at the second rung and kept coming. The forepeak smelled of rust and cold paint.

"New one's in the mess." Ned put the dead impeller in her hands and went up the ladder a rung at a time, both fists on the rails.

At the top he stopped, his head and shoulders in the light of the hatch, and looked back down at her. Then he was gone.

The cold got into the bones of her fingers.

He came down with a new impeller in a box with Marr's price sticker on the end.

"You seat it." He knelt in the water at the housing and held the cover off. "You put both hands on her and feel for the key."

She got both hands on the cold steel and worked the impeller in until it went home.

"Ten seconds of southwest when she comes off the slip," Ned said, threading the first bolt by feel, "and she sets down on the north dolphin. You know that gust."

"I know it."

"District'll want a name by spring."

"That unit was dark Thursday," she said.

"He always runs his unit. Thermo King, he runs it continuous for live product, every Thursday since the Fourth of July two years back, you can hear it from the wheelhouse with the door shut."

Beth kept her hands where they were.

He got the first bolt in, then the second.

"Crates come open now and again," Ned said. "You know they do. A year back I started seeing too many pieces to the crate for the weight. Figured it was shorts. Boxes rode heavy, and I never gave that a thought. That contract's the winter boat. Lose it, the district runs her three days a week. Somebody gets laid off, and it wouldn't be me. So I never said."

He set the wrench down on the housing. "I thought it was shorts, Bethie."

She took her hand off the impeller and put it on his arm, on the wet green wool, a second. Then she picked up the wrench and gave it back to him.

He threw the switch. The pump coughed, caught and ran. The water started down the rung. Ned sat back on his heels in it and listened. Then he looked at her and waited on the name.

She had said Nobody to the marshal on the shore, not an hour back. A question to Ned stayed aboard. Ned's the boat.

"Is the float workable on the ebb?"

Ned looked at the water. "Which tide?"

"Tomorrow's first."

He wiped his hands down his jacket and reached for the tide card tacked to the bulkhead. "Wind goes round west of north by midnight. Swell comes round the point and dies in the lee of the co-op pier, and the float lies quiet there the first of the ebb. Inshore end sets down on the mud a little before nine. Shore under the bank dries out behind the ebb, all but the last ledge, and that one's still deep in the dark." "You read me the forenoon off that card."
