# Ten Days Out: what happens in the first three chapters

On a Thursday night in November, Beth Calloway, eleven years a deckhand on the Cutter Island ferry, puts her bare hand on a lobster dealer's refrigerated truck and finds it isn't cold. A crate lid springs during tie-down, and under the lobsters are sealed bricks of pills. The driver sees her see them. So does a hooded man loading crates, who limps off the boat onto her island. She presses the lid shut ("Lid's sprung. You'll lose your count."), re-straps it, and walks to the wheelhouse as if nothing happened. At one in the morning she calls it in from her kitchen.

At 7:40 the next morning she's on the bow line at the mainland ramp when a deputy US marshal in the wrong boots tells her she was told not to work. Dane Reyes wants her in his car now. She offers him a deal: she works the boat back to the island and the two o'clock over again, and she's his on the ramp at quarter to three. He says no. His phone buzzes in the middle of the argument and he checks it. "Am I keeping you?" He gives her the worst true thing he has: the last witness who knew her own town better than he did is buried in it. She casts off anyway. The ramp starts to rise and he jumps, badly, and she hauls him aboard by the front of his jacket. Neither of them lets go right away.

On the way across he asks her, grudgingly, what a used thirty-four-foot lobster boat is worth. "To who?" "To me." "Less than you're paying." He has a viewing in a boatyard at 3:30 and a broker who wants thirty-one thousand by Saturday noon. She tells him the bank sold her father's boat to a man from away who made it a picnic boat. "You want a working boat to sleep on." "I want a boat I can't be called off of." Back on the island she reads the wind and tells him the two o'clock won't run. At 1:15 the captain cancels it on the radio. "There goes my boat." "It was never your boat."

He moves her aboard the ferry for the night. At nine the storm surge tears the landing bridge loose, and a parted cable whips across the deck. Dane takes her down flat under the rail with her fist in his collar, and the bridge comes down on the bow. Her captain, Ned, looks at the wreck: "Well. That's the bridge." "Does it do that a lot?" "Just the once." The boat is holed and won't carry anyone until the Coast Guard has seen her. Dane slips Beth home in the dark. On the way off the boat she tells Ned, and only Ned, where she'll be. With no fire and no lamp, they sit against the chimney under one blanket and whisper about everything wrong with his boat. Below the house the storm-bound dragger's deck lights burn, and someone on her afterdeck is facing the hill. At five past eleven a pickup with no headlights climbs the harbor road, idles under the house, and goes on. At 11:20 Dane steps out to the yard to make a call. The mudroom door, which has knocked in the wind all night, goes quiet. At 11:40 someone lifts the sticking latch. Dane goes to the door, and whoever it was runs. "Who knew you were here?" "You."

In the morning he comes in from the yard and sets his satellite phone on the table to pour coffee. The screen is still lit: "Log daily. Do not disclose to witness." Under it: "Firm at 31. Sat noon or it goes." She picks it up and reads both lines to his face. Who's paying him thirty-one, and what doesn't she get told? The money is his, he says, and the other thing he can't tell her, and he won't lie about whether there is one. At the island store her oldest friend Tess mentions she saw Beth's flashlight on the back steps last night, and Beth keeps that from him. At noon Dane looks at the clock, then at the dragger's skipper buying cigarettes a few feet away, and doesn't step outside to take his call. Beth watches him let the boat go. They move to an empty summer house on the north point. In its freezing generator shed she gets the engine running, and then they're kissing against the warm housing with his cold hands under her layers. She stops it, and it turns into the fight they didn't finish that morning. She tells him she'd rather be kissed by a man she can check. He goes out on the porch and makes his call where she can watch him do it.
