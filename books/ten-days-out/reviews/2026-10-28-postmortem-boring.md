# Postmortem: why *Ten Days Out* is boring

**Trigger:** the author, October 28, 2026: "This was boring too." That came after three drafts, three outside rounds (4.0–4.2 of 5, "ready after a proof pass"), a simulated buy-yes and a pull curve that never dropped below 5 of 10. The general lessons are in `docs/postmortem-boring.md` and `docs/changelog.md`. This file covers what was specific to this book, from its own reader reports (`reviews/pull-curve.md`, `reviews/sample-read-sample-mark.md`, `reviews/manuscript-audit.md`) and the outside panels.

## 1. The mystery was over by Chapter 3

The sample reader had Tess at 15% (the late laugh on the check-in call) and was sure at 23% ("Tess knew I stayed"). Every misdirect after that bounced off. From then on the suspense plot is the reader waiting for Beth to catch up, and every revision made it worse: we added Beth suspecting Tess early (draft 2) and her admitting it (draft 3), which made the waiting explicit.

## 2. The hero was never a live suspect

Chapter 2 opens in Dane's head and shows him honest. So the notebook in Ch 7, the book's dark moment, could not make a reader doubt him. A romantic-suspense book whose one twist is "the hero was keeping notes" and whose reader already trusts him has no second engine.

## 3. Chapter 1 has no hero

The genre map now requires both leads in a room with spark inside Ch 1. The v1 Ch 1 is Beth alone on a car deck with a dead reefer. It is the best-written chapter and it is a thriller opening, not a romance one.

## 4. The romance was supervised by paperwork

Reporting, check-ins, authorizations, credibility logs, consequences, who hears which call. Every fix for "the repair is too quick" added more procedure. Ch 8 became three long relayed reports in one chapter (pull 5), and the outside panels called it "an administrative negotiation."

## 5. The heat was short and polite

Three scenes at 300–400 words, the first at about 50%, each followed by a suspect list or a phone call. The front note promised "steamy." The sample reader at 28% had "one breath at her temple in a generator shed."

## 6. Texture became the content (again)

Binders, tide tables, radio channels, sightlines, three caretaker houses, a propane cage, a padlock and a gauge. Each was accurate. Together they were most of the sample. The reader skimmed exactly those passages.

## 7. The register was one temperature

The bible's samples were restrained, so the book was restrained: short sentences, withheld action at every scene end, hands that stop an inch short. The voice passes reduced the tics and kept the temperature.

## 8. What worked (keep as events)

The reefer lid on the car deck; the 11:40 latch and "Who knew you were here?" / "You."; the generator shed; the bar crossing and her fist in his belt; "It's a very disappointing kit"; the condoms at Tess's counter; the landing ambush; Tess's two words on channel 9; the car deck in the dark, socks on steel, the ring buoy; "The ham is eleven pounds." Readers named these and nothing else.

## What v2 must do differently

- A mystery that stays open past the midpoint: at least one other suspect with real motive, means and access whom a fair reader can believe, and clues that point two ways.
- Dane must be a live suspect for the reader, not only for Beth. Either no Dane POV until he is cleared, or a Dane POV that hides something true.
- Both leads in a room with spark in Ch 1. First kiss by 35%. Heat 3–4 with full-length scenes, the first before the midpoint.
- No relayed phone calls as scenes. Consequences happen on the page or not at all.
- One or two texture details per scene.
- Style samples at two temperatures.
