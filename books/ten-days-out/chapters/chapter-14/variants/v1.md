# Chapter Fourteen: The Wheel

By Friday night Beth Calloway could hear her own boat breaking itself against the pier through the town office wall.

Steel on stone, a shriek and a thud each time the sea lifted the ferry and dropped it. She had her coat half on before she'd decided anything.

"I'm taking it off."

In the back room Dane ran a thumb under each of the kid's cable ties and added a fresh one. The kid kept his face to the wall, as he had since Thursday.

Beth thought of that slow thumb on her knee on Tuesday, and made herself look at the door.

Tess held out the shotgun. "It's loaded, Marshal. I loaded it."

He broke it open, looked, closed it and handed it back. "We'll be on the ferry till light."

Tess put the island radio in her pocket, on top of her keys.

At the pier the ferry rolled on bare granite, shrieking. The floating dock between steel and stone had gone in the first storm.

"By light it's holed, and a holed boat takes nobody to Portland. I'll hold it off inside the harbor till the wind goes round."

"That steering's never had the sea on it," Dane shouted. "Out there it's rocks in the dark. Here it just grinds."

"Here it's holed for certain. Out there it might hold."

He didn't tell her no. "What do you need?"

"Two hands on the ropes that know what they're doing."

"I worked the back of a lobster boat two summers. I'm good at exactly this."

She put the flashlight in his hand, and Ned's knife after it. "Tonight you hold the light."

He looked at the flashlight longer than a flashlight needed.

The rod from the wheel down to the steering blade under the boat was back in, as straight as two days at the co-op vise could make it. At the stair she said, "It might stick with the sea on it."

She climbed to the cabin and took the wheel. In eleven years she had never had charge of it alone. Her father had stood at a wheel every working day of his life, and her hands went to this one as if they knew it.

She called the ropes off one at a time, and Dane threw each clear. The last held the whole boat, so tight it hummed. In the first storm a rope like that had parted like a gunshot.

"It won't come off the post," Dane shouted up. "Cut it?"

One second.

"Cut it."

He stood clear of the rope's line and cut it with Ned's knife. The end whipped back across the empty pier. She watched him do it right.

The stone fell away, the shriek stopped, and the boat swung free into the dark. Dane stood on the car deck with the knife in one hand and her flashlight in the other, looking up at her window.

* * *

An hour in, the wheel went heavy in Beth's hands at one point in its turn.

Snow hissed on the windows, and each sea came at the boat's nose out of the black. The heaviness came again at the same place, a drag she felt in her palms before she understood it.

Then the nose swung off the wind, toward the rocks below the harbor road, where the lobster boats had gone up in the first storm. She couldn't see them. She turned to bring it back.

The wheel stopped.

It wouldn't come back. The rod she'd straightened had stuck, with the blade under the boat turned all the way to one side, so the boat kept turning. Slowly at first. Then faster. Toward the rocks.

"Come on," she said. "Come on, damn you."

She threw her whole body on the wheel, and the rod came free all at once. Nothing held the blade then. The sea shoved it, and the wheel spun back through her hands with it. She held on as it spun, and it took the skin off both palms. The white rope scar across her left palm opened with the rest. She could have let go. She held on until it stopped, and then she held it there.

Her breath went out of her white. Her hands were hot and wet, and she didn't look at them.

Dane was in the doorway. He'd felt the boat turn wrong through his feet and come up the stair. He looked at her hands and said nothing.

A white light came on along the shore.

It swung across the snow and stopped on the rocks. Behind it stood the red fire truck on the harbor road. The rocks were far closer than she'd guessed, and the boat was still swinging toward them.

If she turned all the way, the rod might stick again. She turned the wheel most of the way with her raw hands and held it. The boat came round slowly. The rocks went by in the white light a few yards off, black and shining.

Tess. She had put the light on the rocks for Beth, and she still wouldn't say one word to her on the radio.

"Who's on the prisoners?" Dane said.

"Russ. She'll have given him the shotgun."

Dane took off his coat, then his shirt, and put the coat back on over his bare chest. The first-aid box was empty. He held the shirt up to her without a word.

She took her left hand off the wheel. "Hold it here. Don't turn it."

He held the spoke where she had put it. He stood on one sleeve, split the shirt down its seam, and wrapped her left palm, then her right, while she steered.

"That was my good shirt."

"You don't have a good shirt."

"Open and close them for me. Every few minutes, or they'll set."

"Stop," she told him, and he stopped.

His coat hung open over bare skin in a cabin cold enough to show her breath. She looked at the hollow of his throat and thought about her mouth there, and she was angry enough to keep both hands on the wheel.

Later, when he turned to watch the light on the rocks, she opened and closed her wrapped hands on the wheel, as he'd told her, where he couldn't see.

* * *

At first gray, past the white water at the harbor mouth, the big boat lay off the point showing one light, and Beth Calloway could see it was closer in than last night.

Neither of them said what it was. On the harbor road the fire truck's light went off, and the truck drove away up the hill. All night Tess had never once swung the light up to the cabin.

Her hands sat on the wheel in his split shirt, gone stiff and brown. The snow was thinning, and the wind had started back round. Dane stood at her shoulder in his open coat, his right knee locked from the cold.

He propped his phone on the shelf under the front windows and called his boss on speaker. "The boat off the point came in closer overnight."

"Your Hal Mercer comes back to nobody," his boss said. "There's no license out of Gloucester in that name. That's a lie to a federal officer, and in this sea it still doesn't put a boarding team aboard. Nothing flies before Tuesday. The Coast Guard's on the storm's distress calls. If that boat comes at you, that's a distress, and they'll come from an hour off. And a text from a phone in nobody's name won't arrest that lawyer."

"The harbor mouth is white water till Sunday evening," Dane said, "and the next storm's in before light Monday. Sunday night or not at all."

"Sunday," Beth said.

"And the hold stands for Monday," his boss said.

Beth said it to Dane's face, with her wrapped hands on the wheel. "Cancel it."

He looked at her hands. Then he spoke into the phone. "Withdraw it. Tell the prosecutor this morning. She doesn't get held, and she doesn't go into the program unless she says so. Write that down."

"You asked for it Wednesday," his boss said.

"I was scared, and I called it the job." He took a breath. "And I'm involved with her. Write that down too."

The line went quiet. The sea knocked under the boat. Then his boss said, "Written."

"Then she's on her own after Monday."

"Not all of her. I've put in for the Maine office."

He held the phone out to her.

"Say it to me," Beth said.

"After Monday nobody from my office stands between you and those people. Do you understand what you're choosing?"

"I know what I'm choosing. I'm the one standing here."

The call ended. The wheel creaked under her hands.

Dane stayed where he was and didn't touch her. "I still think you should go. It was never mine to say. Last night you took it off the stone when I'd have left it there, and you held that wheel with the skin coming off your hands, and you were right both times."

"Then you ask me. Every time. Even when you're sure. And you never go round me to anybody again."

"Yes."

"What if somebody's shooting at us?"

"Then ask fast."

She looked out at the one light. "Wednesday I said, 'And so do we.' For both of us. Same thing I hated you for."

"I went first," he said, and looked at her hands.

It was five days since he'd said he loved her at the lighthouse and she'd told him to ask again in five. He didn't ask. She looked at him in his open coat with nothing under it and wanted to be asked.

"What do you want, Dane?"

"I'll tell you on the other side. When nobody's paying me to stand here."

The wind came round. She brought the boat's nose toward the pier, and he went down to take the ropes without being told.

* * *

Dane Reyes had the ham in the town-office oven by three, because that was the one thing on Cutter he could still fix.

The back room smelled of cloves. He wore Russ's flannel shirt, short in the arms, because his own was round her hands in pieces. He shut the window over the sink on the one light off the point.

That morning the ferry board gave Beth the boat for tonight, over his phone. Her palms were in black electrical tape.

Beth stood in the doorway, where Russ and Tess could both see her.

"Russ. Tess. He's going to ask me something, and I'm going to say yes. I'm telling you myself, so nobody ever says it for me."

Tess's eyes came up off the desk to Beth's face and stayed there.

"You just told them the answer," Dane said.

"Ask anyway. Even when you're sure."

He took the oven mitt off. "Can I kiss you? I'm asking."

"Yes." She didn't come to him yet. "And what you said to me at the lighthouse. Yes to that too. I love you. I have since the rock, when you opened your hand."

Dane didn't move. On the couch Russ set his glass down.

"Come here," she said.

He went. She couldn't put her palms on him, so she hooked her wrists over his shoulders and held her taped hands up behind his neck. He kept his own hands at her waist, clear of her palms. She kissed him slowly, after four days of not, and her mouth tasted of coffee, and he had a sentence going about how this would sound, and it never finished, and he thought her name, and her mouth, and nothing else.

Tess stood with the shotgun under her arm. "I'll be on the steps. Somebody ought to watch the harbor." She went out past Beth without a look, and the door shut hard.

Beth watched it shut over his shoulder and didn't let go of him.

From the couch Russ said, "Well."

* * *

By eight it was full dark, and Dane lay on his back under the bait truck in the smell of old herring.

Behind the back wheel he found a cheap phone in a plastic bag, taped to the frame. It lit when he touched it.

Friday night the car deck took spray till morning, and everything on it wore ice. This bag was dry, the battery near full. Somebody came aboard Saturday.

All Saturday the ferry sat empty at the pier while he held the light for her at the co-op vise. That one was his. Ned and the kid were tied up all day. Anybody else could have walked down.

He took it up the stair to Beth. "It tells them when we leave. Can I switch it off?"

"Off."

"When it goes dead, they'll know we're moving."

"Then we go dark. Let them find us by ear."

He held the button through the bag till the screen went out.

Ned looked down at the life jacket Dane buckled on him as if it were somebody else's.

At the hatch Beth ran her thumb along the rod to Saturday's bright mark. "If it sticks, I'll feel it first."

In the cabin she said, "Bring Ned up here for the ledges. He hears breaking water before anybody sees it."

"I don't like it. He's the man who sold you to them."

"Down there he's an arm's length from the kid."

"It's your call. I'll bring him." He cuffed Ned to the rail by her left hand and dropped the key in her coat pocket.

"We go at ten," she said.

"Why ten?"

"High water's an hour after. I want every foot of it under us at the harbor mouth."

"That boat's watched us get ready all day. Do we go?"

"Go. If it comes at us, I run inside the ledges, close in to the point."

At her word every light aboard went off. "It's against every rule."

Dane's hand went to the cabin switch out of habit.

"Touch that and you swim."

The ropes came off clean, and at ten she took it across the black harbor by feel.

At her shoulder he watched the pale tape on her hands and thought about his mouth on the back of her neck.

At the mouth she slowed. White water roared on both sides. Ned turned his head toward it, listening.

Then, late, under their own engine, Dane heard a bigger one come out of the dark off the point. It had found them.

Her taped hands stayed where they were on the wheel. "Tell me where it is."

Dane took the rifle to the back window.
