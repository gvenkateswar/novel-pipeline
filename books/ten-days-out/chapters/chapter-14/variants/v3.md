# Chapter Fourteen: The Wheel

By Friday night Beth Calloway could hear her own boat breaking itself against the pier through the town office wall.

A long shriek, then a thud, each time the sea lifted the ferry and dropped it. She had her coat half on before she'd decided anything.

"I'm taking it off."

Dane ran a thumb under each of the kid's cable ties and added a fresh one. The kid kept his face to the wall.

Tess held out Russ's shotgun. "It's loaded, Marshal. I loaded it."

He checked it anyway. "We'll be on the ferry till light." Tess put the island radio in her coat pocket, on top of her keys.

Beth watched his thumb go under the ties, slow and careful, and thought about those hands on her. She made herself look at the door.

At the pier the ferry rolled on bare granite. The float, the floating dock between steel and stone, was gone. Past the harbor mouth the snow opened on one light, low under the point, and shut again.

"By light it's holed," Beth said. "I'll hold it off the pier till the wind goes back round."

"That steering's never had the sea on it," Dane shouted. "Out there it's the rocks in the dark. Here it only grinds."

"Here it's holed for certain. Out there it might hold."

He didn't tell her no. "What do you need?"

"Two hands on the ropes that know what they're doing."

"I worked the back of a lobster boat two summers. I'm good at exactly this."

She took the flashlight out of her coat and put it in his hand, and Ned's knife after it. "Tonight you hold the light."

Two days she'd had the rod in the co-op vise, the one that ran from the wheel down to the blade under the boat that steered it. "It might stick with the sea on it," she told him.

In eleven years she had never had this wheel to herself. Her father had stood at a wheel every working day of his life, and her hands went to this one as if they knew it.

She called the ropes off one at a time, and Dane threw each clear. The last one held the whole boat, so tight it hummed. In the first storm a rope like that had parted like a gunshot.

"It won't come off the post," Dane shouted up. "Cut it?"

One second.

"Cut it."

He stood to the side of its line and cut it with Ned's knife, and the end whipped back across the pier. She watched him do it right.

The boat swung free into the dark, and the stone fell away from its side. Dane stood on the car deck with the knife in one hand and her flashlight in the other, looking up at her window.

* * *

An hour in, Beth Calloway felt the wheel go heavy at one point in its turn. It was in her palms before it was in her head.

The white water at the harbor mouth was at her back, and past it, off the point, one light she couldn't see and didn't need to. Below the harbor road on her right were the rocks where the lobster boats had gone up in the first storm, and she couldn't see them either.

The nose began to swing off the wind, and she turned to bring it back.

The wheel stopped.

It wouldn't come back. The rod she'd straightened had stuck, with the blade under the boat turned all the way to one side, so the boat kept turning. Slowly at first. Then faster. Toward the rocks.

"Come on," she said. "Come on, damn you."

She threw her whole body on the wheel, and the rod came free all at once. Nothing held the blade then. The sea shoved it, and the wheel spun back through her hands with it. She held on as it spun, and it took the skin off both palms. The white rope scar across her left palm opened with the rest. She could have let go. She held on until it stopped, and then she held it there.

Dane was in the doorway. He had felt the boat turn wrong through his feet and come up the stair. He looked at her hands and said nothing.

A white light came on along the shore.

It swung across the snow and stopped on the rocks, and behind it was the shape of the island's red fire truck on the harbor road. The rocks were far closer than she had guessed, and the boat was still swinging toward them.

If she turned all the way, the rod might stick again, so she turned the wheel most of the way with her raw hands and held it. The boat came round slowly. The rocks went by a few yards off.

Tess had put the light on the rocks for her, and she still wouldn't say one word to her on the radio. That was Tess. She'd save your life and charge you for the gas.

"Who's on the prisoners?" Dane said.

"Russ. She'll have given him the shotgun."

The ferry's first-aid box was empty since Russ's leg. Dane took off his coat, then his shirt, and put the coat back on over his bare chest. He held the shirt up to her without a word.

She took her left hand off the wheel. "Hold it here. Don't turn it."

He held the spoke exactly where she'd put it. He stood on one sleeve, split the shirt down its seam, and wrapped her left palm and then her right while she steered.

"That was my good shirt."

"You don't have a good shirt."

"Open and close them for me. Every few minutes, or they'll set."

"Stop," she told him, and he stopped.

His coat hung open over bare skin in a cabin cold enough to show her breath. She looked at the hollow of his throat and thought about her mouth there, and she was angry enough with him to keep both hands on the wheel.

Later, when he had turned to watch the light on the rocks, she opened and closed her wrapped hands on the wheel, as he'd told her to, where he couldn't see her do it.

* * *

In the first gray Beth Calloway saw the big boat past the white water at the harbor mouth, off the point, showing its one light. It lay closer in than last night, close enough now that the light had a long dark hull under it.

Neither of them said what it was. They had both known since Thursday.

Her hands were on the wheel in his split shirt, gone stiff and brown. On the harbor road the fire truck's light went off, and the truck drove away up the hill. All night Tess had never once swung the light up to the cabin.

Dane propped his phone on the shelf under the front windows and called his boss on speaker. He told her the boat had moved in.

"Your Hal Mercer comes back to nobody," his boss said. "There's no license out of Gloucester in that name. That's a lie to a federal officer, and in this sea it still doesn't put a boarding team aboard."

Dane's hand went to the shelf beside the phone and held on. A man who'd lie about his own name to a marshal would lie about anything.

"Nothing flies before Tuesday. The Coast Guard's on the storm's distress calls. If that boat comes at you, that's a distress, and they'll come from an hour off."

"The harbor mouth is white water till Sunday evening," Dane said, "and the next storm's in before light Monday. Sunday night or not at all."

"Sunday," Beth said.

"And the hold stands for Monday."

Beth said it to his face, her wrapped hands on the wheel. "Cancel it."

He looked at her hands. Then, into the phone: "Withdraw it. Tell the prosecutor this morning. She doesn't get held, and she doesn't go into the program unless she says so. Write that down."

"You asked for it Wednesday."

"I was scared, and I called it the job. And I'm involved with her. Write that down too."

The line went quiet a long time.

"Written."

"Then she's on her own after Monday."

"Not all of her. I've put in for the Maine office."

He took the phone off the shelf and held it out to her.

"Say it to me," Beth said.

"After Monday nobody from my office stands between you and those people. Do you understand what you're choosing?"

"I know what I'm choosing. I'm the one standing here."

The call ended.

He said the rest standing, a step off from the wheel. "It's worse than I thought, and I still think you should go. It was never mine to say. Last night you took it off the stone when I'd have left it there, and you held that wheel with the skin coming off, and you were right both times."

"Then you ask me," Beth said. "Every time. Even when you're sure. And you never go round me to anybody again."

"Yes."

"What if somebody's shooting at us?"

"Then ask fast."

She looked at the floor, then at him. "Wednesday I said, 'And so do we.' For both of us. Same thing I hated you for."

He held her eyes. "I went round you first."

It was five days since he'd said he loved her on the lighthouse and she'd told him to ask again in five. He didn't ask. She looked at him in his open coat with nothing under it and wanted to be asked.

"What do you want, Dane?"

"I'll tell you on the other side. When nobody's paying me to stand here."

The wind had gone round. She brought the boat's nose toward the pier, and he went down to take the ropes without being told.

* * *

Dane Reyes had the ham in the town-office oven by three, because that was the one thing on Cutter he could still fix.

The back room smelled of cloves. Out the window over the sink, low under the point, the one light hadn't moved since dawn yesterday. That morning Beth had called the ferry board on his phone and got the boat for tonight.

Beth stood in the doorway where Russ and Tess could both see her.

"Russ. Tess. He's going to ask me something, and I'm going to say yes. I'm telling you myself, so nobody ever says it for me."

Tess's eyes came up off the desk to Beth's face and stayed there.

"You just told them the answer," Dane said.

"Ask anyway. Even when you're sure."

He took the oven mitt off. "Can I kiss you? I'm asking."

"Yes." She didn't come to him yet. "And what you said to me at the lighthouse. Yes to that too. I love you. I have since the rock, when you opened your hand."

Dane didn't move. On the couch Russ set his glass down.

"Come here," she said.

He went. She couldn't put her palms on him, so she hooked her wrists over his shoulders and held her taped hands up behind his neck. He kept his own hands at her waist, clear of her palms. She kissed him slowly, after four days of not. He thought about how this would look, a marshal kissing his witness with that boat out there watching, and then he stopped asking. He thought her name, and her mouth, and nothing else.

Tess stood with the shotgun under her arm. "I'll be on the steps. Somebody ought to watch the harbor." She went out past Beth without a look, and the door shut hard. Beth watched it shut over his shoulder and didn't let go of him.

From the couch Russ said, "Well."

* * *

By eight it was full dark, and Dane Reyes lay under the bait truck with his flashlight in the stink of old herring, looking for a man or anything a man had left.

Up behind the back wheel he found a cheap phone in a plastic bag, taped to the frame. Its screen lit through the plastic when he touched it.

Friday night the car deck had taken spray till morning, and every chain and truck on it had worn ice. This bag was dry, with no ice on it, and the battery was near full. Somebody had walked aboard on Saturday.

All Saturday the ferry had sat empty at the pier while he held the light for her at the co-op vise. That one was his. Ned and the kid had been tied up all day. Anybody else on Cutter could have.

Two days he'd watched that boat, and it had been watching him from under a bait truck.

He took it to Beth on the steel stair. "It tells that boat when we leave the pier. Can I switch it off?"

"Off."

"When it goes dead, they'll know we're moving."

"Then we go dark. Let them find us by ear."

He held the button through the bag till the screen died, and pocketed it.

Both prisoners got life jackets. Ned had never worn one in thirty years on this boat. Russ's handheld radio went on Dane's coat.

"At the ledges I want Ned beside me," Beth said. "He hears breaking water before anybody sees it."

"I don't like it. He's the man who sold you to them."

"Down there he's an arm's length from the kid."

"It's your call. I'll bring him." In the cabin he cuffed Ned to the rail by her left hand and put the key in her pocket.

"Why wait till ten?" he asked.

"High water's an hour after. I want every foot of it under us at the harbor mouth."

"Do we go?"

"Go. If it comes at us, I run inside the ledges, close in to the point."

Every light aboard went off at her word. Dane's hand went to the cabin light switch out of habit.

"Touch that and you swim."

At ten she took it off the pier and across the black harbor by feel. At her shoulder he watched her taped hands, and on any other night he'd have put his mouth to the back of her neck.

At the mouth she slowed. Ned turned his head toward the white water on both sides, listening.

Under their own engine, late, Dane heard a bigger engine come up out of the dark off the point.

Her taped hands stayed where they were. "Tell me where it is."

Dane took the rifle to the back window.
