# Chapter Fourteen: The Wheel

The kid wouldn't say what boat, and by Friday night Beth Calloway had stopped asking, because her own was breaking itself against the pier.

The sound came through the town office wall from the pier. Steel grinding on stone, a long shriek and then a thud, each time the sea lifted the ferry and dropped it. She was on her feet, coat half on, before she had decided anything.

In the back room the kid lay with his face to the wall and his wrists in cable ties. Dane ran a thumb under each tie and added a fresh one. "They've got a boat," the kid had said on her kitchen floor yesterday, and not one word since. Ned sat in Russ's cuffs at the pipe, listening to his boat grind on the stone.

Beth watched Dane's thumb under the kid's ties, the same careful hands that had dressed her knee on Tuesday, and she wanted him. She had ended it on Wednesday. She hadn't stopped wanting him for one hour since.

Russ lay gray on the couch, shot through the thigh in her own doorway. Tess held his shotgun out to Dane. He broke it open, looked, closed it and handed it back.

"It's loaded, Marshal. I loaded it." Tess didn't look at Beth.

"We'll be off the pier, inside the harbor, till light," Dane told her.

Tess said nothing. Her keys lay on the desk beside the fire truck's radio.

At the pier the ferry rolled against bare granite in the blowing snow, because the float, the floating dock that used to sit between boat and stone, had gone to pieces in the first storm.

"By light it's holed," Beth said. "Then nothing crosses Sunday, and Sunday's the one night the sea lets anything out. I'm taking it off till the wind goes back round."

"You've had the steering in pieces two days," Dane shouted. "If it goes out there, you're on the rocks in the dark. Here it only grinds."

"Here it's holed for certain. Out there it might hold."

He didn't tell her no. "What do you need?"

"Two hands on the ropes that know what they're doing."

"I worked the back of my mother's cousin's lobster boat two summers. I'm good at exactly this."

She pulled the flashlight out of her coat and put it in his hand.

At the hatch she pushed her gloves into her coat pocket and dropped down into the dark where the steering lived. The flashlight came down after her in his hand.

* * *

She lay on her back on cold steel with a wrench, in a space the size of a closet. Every wave boomed through the hull a foot from her head.

"Lower," she said. "There. Don't move it."

He held the light exactly there, crouched behind her because there was nowhere else to be, his breath on the back of her neck.

"What does that do?"

"The wheel up top turns a gear. The gear pushes this rod. The rod turns the rudder, the big steel blade under the back that steers the boat. The first storm bent the rod. There's no new one this side of Pruett, so I straightened it in the co-op's vise. It's as straight as I can get it. It might bind when the sea's on it."

"And if it does?"

"Then I'll find out at the wheel. On that stone it's holed by light."

His chest was almost at her back, and she wanted him so plainly that her hands slowed on the wrench. She wanted to turn over in the dark and pull him down by his coat.

At the last bolt she said, "Hold it still, Marshal."

They both heard it. She hadn't called him that since Wednesday.

"You called me Marshal," he said quietly.

"It's what you are."

"You only say it one way."

"Hold the light."

She pulled the bolt tight. He held the light.

She climbed the steel stair on the stiff knee and put her hands on the wheel. In eleven years she had never once been in charge of it with nobody beside her.

Her father had stood at a wheel every working day until the bank took his boat, and she had carried the papers out to the bank man's truck. Her hands went to this wheel as if they already knew it.

She called the ropes off one at a time from the cabin window. Down on the car deck, the open deck where the trucks parked, Dane lifted each off its post and threw it clear. She kept the last one on so the boat could swing out on it, off the stone. It held the whole boat now, so tight it hummed.

In the first storm a rope like that had parted like a gunshot, and Ned had dragged her clear. Her hands wanted to leave the wheel and go down there.

"It won't come off the post," Dane shouted up. "Cut it?"

One second.

"Cut it."

He stood to the side of the rope's line, because a cut rope under that much pull comes back like a whip. Two summers on a lobster boat had taught him that. He cut it with Ned's knife, and the end whipped back across the pier, nowhere near him.

She watched him do it right.

The boat swung free of the pier into the dark and the snow, and the stone fell away from its side. Dane was still on the car deck with the knife in his hand, looking up at her window.

* * *

She had held the boat's nose into the wind for most of an hour. Off to the right, below the harbor road, were the rocks where the lobster boats had gone up in the first storm, and she couldn't see them.

The wheel went heavy.

Only at one point in its turn, for a second, and she felt it in her palms before she understood it. Then the nose began to swing off the wind, and she turned the wheel to bring it back.

The wheel stopped.

It wouldn't come back. The rod she'd straightened had bound, with the blade under the boat turned all the way to one side, so the boat kept turning. Slowly at first. Then faster. Toward the rocks.

"Come on," she said. "Come on, damn you."

She threw her whole body on the wheel, and the rod came free all at once. The sea pushing on the blade spun the wheel back through her hands. She held on as it spun, and it tore the skin off both palms, and the white rope scar across her left palm opened with the rest. She could have let go. She held on until it stopped, and then she held it there.

Dane was in the doorway. He had felt the boat turn wrong through his feet, down among the trucks, and come up the stair. He looked at her hands and said nothing.

A white light came on along the shore.

It swung across the snow and stopped on the rocks, and behind it was the shape of the island's red fire truck on the harbor road. The rocks were thirty feet off. She had guessed far more, and the boat was still swinging toward them.

Turning all the way could bind the rod again, so she turned the wheel most of the way with her torn hands and held it. The boat came round slowly. The rocks went by in the white light a boat's length off.

Tess. She had put the light on the rocks and hadn't said one word on the radio.

"Who's on the prisoners?" Dane said.

"Russ. She'll have given him the shotgun."

The ferry's first-aid box was in the town office, empty since Russ's leg. Dane took off his coat, then his shirt, and put the coat back on over his bare chest. He held the shirt up to her without a word.

She took her left hand off the wheel. "Hold it here. Don't turn it."

He held the spoke exactly where hers had been. She gave him her left hand, and he tore the shirt with his teeth and wrapped her palm with his free hand, quick and sure, as he'd done her knee. Then the right.

Later, with the boat steady, he said, "In an hour they'll stiffen. Keep the fingers moving."

"The cold's helping. It slows the bleeding."

Later still: "That was my good shirt."

"You don't have a good shirt."

"Stop," she told him, and he stopped.

His coat hung open over bare skin in a cabin cold enough to show her breath, and his hands had been steady and careful on hers. She wanted him. She was angry with him. Both were true.

Hours on, the light was still on the rocks.

"Dollar says it holds till light," Dane said.

"I don't bet on—"

She stopped, because she couldn't see any of it: the rod, the wind, the morning.

* * *

In the first gray, past the white water at the harbor mouth, a big fishing boat lay off the point, out of the wind, showing one light.

Her hands were wrapped on the wheel in his torn shirt, gone stiff and brown.

"That's not from here," she said.

Nobody from here lay off the point in November, and she knew every boat on this coast by its shape. This one was nobody's. "They've got a boat," the kid had said on her kitchen floor. "It comes in from offshore," Ned had said on the east road. Here it was.

On the harbor road the fire truck's light went off, and the truck pulled away up the hill. Tess never once looked at the boat.

Dane's phone sat on the shelf under the front windows, on the harbor's one bar. He called it in on speaker, beside her: a big fishing boat off the point with one light, watching the harbor mouth.

His boss gave him two things.

"The Coast Guard is out on the storm's distress calls, and it won't come for a boat that hasn't done anything. If that boat comes at you, call them, and they'll come from an hour off."

Dane waited.

"Marine Patrol's boat on your stretch is the one with the hole in it, and the state won't send its next one into a shooting. If she isn't in the room on Monday, Cole goes free at two."

Marine Patrol was the state police on the water.

"The harbor mouth is white water till Sunday evening," Dane said, "and the next storm's in before light Monday. Sunday night or not at all."

"Sunday," Beth said.

Dane turned from the phone and said it to her face.

"I decided your life for you because I was scared, and I called it my job. I still think you should go. It was never mine to say."

To the phone: "She doesn't go into the program unless she says so. Write that down."

The program was the new name in the new state, and he had just taken it off the table.

"Then she's on her own after Monday," his boss said.

"Not all of her. I've put in for the Maine office." Then: "Tell her yourself. She's right here."

The boss spoke to Beth for the first time. "After Monday nobody from my office stands between you and those people. Do you understand what you're choosing?"

"I know what I'm choosing. I'm the one standing here. Say it to me."

The call ended.

On Monday, up on the lighthouse, she had told him to ask her again in five days. This was the fifth day, and he didn't ask. She noticed. She noticed, too, that she had wanted him to. She wanted him, in his open coat with nothing under it, and she wanted to be asked.

"What do you want, Dane?"

"I'll tell you on the other side."

Past the white water, off the point, the one light hadn't moved.
