# Chapter Eleven: Somebody's Boy

The kid wrote the way kids wrote, all small letters and no names, and by half past two in the morning Dane Reyes could write it too.

Besides the leak's thread, the phone held a few short ones to numbers with no names: *cant*, *ill*, a period after most of it, nothing about Cole or the ferry or Beth.

At a quarter past two he had rung the leak's number from his own phone, and it was off. Like the kid's, it came on only to send or to wait for an answer. So the one way to get it switched on, in somebody's pocket, in a room with them, was to give it something to answer.

He set the kid's phone to silent, so nothing from the other number could ring in the dark.

Beth sat on the daybed with her bad leg straight.

"Nobody on this island lets a boy bleed to death in the snow," she said. "Say he's shot. Say he can't walk."

Dane wrote it the boy's way and held the screen out to her.

He fetched the second quilt and rolled it long. "This goes on the daybed. You go in the back bedroom."

"No." She didn't move. "Whoever comes will kneel and put a hand on him. A quilt doesn't breathe. I want to be the one they find."

He threw the quilt back through the bedroom door. "You're right. The first hand on it would know, and they'd be gone before I could stop them."

"Whoever walks in here is one of yours," he said, "and they'll come because they're kind."

"I know. That's why it'll work."

"This is the kid's cove. He's got a pistol and no boat. If that door opens on him, you go out the front when I say go."

He went and drew the front door's bolt and left the door shut.

"If it's the kid, the plan is I shoot him. If it's Tess, the plan is you explain."

"If it's Tess, the plan is run."

Beth leaned over and shut the stove's damper, and the red behind its door began to go dull.

Dane stood the kid's rifle in his own corner behind the back door, out of reach of anybody kneeling at the bed.

Then he crouched with the phone's light on her knee. The tape had held. He put his mouth on her knee above it, once. Her hand came down on his head and stayed there until he stood up.

At the kitchen window, where the one bar was, he texted his boss with the screen turned so Beth could read it.

*The leak's phone is off. I want to bring the leak here with a text from the kid's phone. Beth plays the boy on the daybed. I take the door. I need your go.*

He waited.

*Go.*

He sent the bait.

*marshal shot me. bleeding bad. cant walk. east cove the house up the hill. back door open. come alone. bring something for blood.*

Four minutes later the kid's phone lit in his hand.

*coming.*

At a quarter past, that phone had been off. Now it was on, like a man waking to look on the half hour.

Dane pulled the chair out from under the back door's knob. The storm leaned on the door, and cold came in around its edge. He stood the chair in his corner, laid his gun on it, and set the kid's phone face down beside the gun. Its light showed only at the edges.

* * *

By three the stove was dead, and the kitchen was as cold as the yard.

Beth lay on the daybed under the dentist's quilt the way a hurt man would lie, bad leg straight. Dane stood in the corner behind the back door, on the hinge side, so the door would swing in and hide him. The whole black kitchen lay between them. The only light was a thin line around the edge of the phone on the chair.

The cold came up through his boots, and the cut in his scalp ached with it. Wind moaned down the chimney into the dead stove, and below the house the sea went in and out of the cove.

He held his gun at his side and listened for the road.

Something knocked on the stones below the house. Then a long scrape, wood over rock.

He was at the cove-side shutter with his gun up before the scrape stopped. Across the kitchen Beth had quit breathing. He could hear that she had.

It came again.

"The skiff," Beth said from the dark.

It came again, and again, in time with the sea: the kid's skiff, half full of water, lifting on the tide and dropping back onto the stones.

Dane listened until he was sure it kept time with the sea and nothing else. Then he went back to his corner.

Neither of them said it was nothing.

"Talk to me," Beth said into the dark, quietly.

He started talking.

"There's a boat I want to buy," he whispered across the kitchen. "An old wooden lobster boat, about the size of the one I worked on at sixteen. They always want thirty-some thousand dollars for one, and they always tell you it only needs a little work."

"Does it?"

"It needs everything. First I'd fix the floor at the back, where you stand to haul. On an old boat it's always soft. You put your boot down, and it gives."

"It'll leak," Beth said.

"They all leak."

He kept on in a whisper about the soft boards, and he was giving her the price of a boat in the dark so she'd have his voice to hold onto, and under every word of it was how much he loved her, and how whoever was coming down that road was somebody she loved too, and how nothing he knew how to do would make it anybody else. He talked anyway. It was all he had to give her, and it was a boat.

Through the gap at the road-side shutter, where one hook didn't seat, a light showed.

Far off on the east road, it came slowly along and turned down the dentist's lane.

Dane stopped talking.

Beth came off the daybed on her good leg to the gap, and her shoulder came against his.

The light dipped and rose and dipped again, on every other step, the way a lantern swings for a big man who favors one hip.

A lit lantern on that road was a light anybody in those woods could see.

"I know whose it is," Beth said.

She went back to the daybed, lay down the way a hurt boy would and pulled the quilt over her head. Dane stepped into his corner behind the door with his gun down along his leg.

Outside, a boot came down on the back step.

* * *

The door swung in and stopped a hand's width from his face.

Snow blew in across the floor. Then light, low and orange, from a lantern. Then Ned's voice.

"Son? It's all right, son."

Through the crack at the hinges Dane saw a green wool jacket white with snow, the lantern, the ferry's first-aid box, and a blanket. Ned was breathing hard from an hour of road. He went straight to the daybed.

He set the lantern on the floor, and every shadow went up the walls. He knelt by the daybed slowly, minding the bad hip, and talked the whole way down.

"I've got the box off the boat, son. Gauze and tape. It's all right now. You're cold, that's all, so I brought my good blanket." He shook it out over the shape and smoothed it down. "There. Let's have a look at you."

Snow melted off his jacket onto the dentist's floor. The kitchen smelled of kerosene.

His hand went to his belt, to the rope knife every man on the ferry wore for cutting a rope in a hurry. The blade came out over Beth.

He meant to cut a sleeve off a wound. Dane's gun came up anyway.

Beth sat up out from under both blankets.

"Hello, Ned."

Ned's hand stopped on the knife.

Dane shut the door behind him.

Ned turned his head. He saw the marshal and the gun, and his face went straight back to Beth.

From then on Ned looked at Beth before every answer, and only at Beth.

Dane picked the kid's phone off the chair and rang the leak's number. A man walking an hour to a hurt boy kept his phone on, in case the boy wrote again.

Ned's coat buzzed.

"Take it out," Dane said.

Ned took out a cheap phone. It was lit and shaking in his big hand, with the kid's number calling.

Dane took the knife out of his other hand in one move and held out his own for the phone. Ned gave it to him. Dane pocketed both and stepped back to the door.

Dane talked through everything, and now he stood by the door of a stranger's kitchen with his mouth shut, because this was hers, and he hated watching her do it, and he would not take one word of it from her, and at the back of his head Cole's lawyer was already asking twelve people from Maine to picture an old man on his knees with a blanket. He held his tongue. He was bad at it.

Beth sat with her bad leg straight out and one hand flat on the daybed beside her.

"Russ's porch," she said.

"Wind's going round to the west."

She waited.

"That was me."

"The north-side house."

"Me."

"The beach at three."

"Me."

Her hand stayed flat on the daybed, pressed until the fingertips went white.

"The Dyer phone," she said.

"I could see his light from there. I told Tess so. I wanted Russ off that porch, and I didn't want him hurt."

Tess had said those words across her counter on Saturday. Now they had Ned's voice.

"You stood in the water," Beth said.

"I did that too."

"And tonight you walked an hour in this. What for?"

"He's somebody's boy, Bethie."

Beth pulled his blanket off her legs, folded it square and put it in his arms. He set it on the chair by the daybed.

"Ned," Dane said, "you're under arrest." It came out rough. He liked the man.

Ned held his wrists out together. He held them out to Beth.

Dane's cuffs were at the bottom of the bay.

Beth got up off the daybed, took the length of rope from around the kindling in the woodbox and came back. She knelt on her good knee in front of him and tied his wrists with the knot he had taught her on the car deck when she was nine, the one that only pulls tighter.

"You'd walk through a storm for a boy you never met," Beth said. "And you sold me."

Whatever Ned said next would take the last of him away from her, and Dane could not stand in front of it.

Ned looked at the dead stove.

Beth held the end of the rope she had tied him with, and she asked him, "Why?"
