# Chapter Five: Half Tide

Her jeans and socks hung steaming on the stove rail, and the wet wool had begun to cook. Beth sat in Dane's fleece with the sleeves past her hands, his red tag still knotted over the left cuff, and her bare feet on the oven door. The heat came back into her legs from the outside in, shins first, thighs last. Dane crouched at the firebox in a summer family's kitchen and fed it one split, then another, his sleeves pushed up, and she let herself look.

Between squalls the Monday air went dead. From the window, cheek to a gap in the sheet, she could see the smoke go up off the roof in one gray stalk the whole island could read.

"Smoke," she said.

"You were blue an hour ago." He put on another split and shut the door on it. "It stays lit till you aren't, and from where I'm crouched you're still a color I don't like."

By noon the squalls had thinned. A truck came over the spine on the north road, propane rack catching the light, and stopped at the top facing Ashby, exhaust smoking white.

She knew that truck, and who drove it, and what would be on its seat when the rounds were done, and where Tess wrote things down.

It went on down the east-shore fork.

Over the roof the smoke still stood straight up. Beth took her jeans off the rail, stiff and dry, and began to put them on.

* * *

The bell went over the door at half past one, and Tess didn't look up from the ladle.

Two cot blankets lay folded on the counter by the till, squared at the corners. The storm log lay open beside them. Tess stood at the big pot on the woodstove ladling chowder into two pots, and the steam had gone white on the cold front window. Out back the generator roared through the office curtain. The store was empty. Everybody on Cutter was home feeding a stove.

"Generator's Ned's," Tess said. "Chimney smoke's people."

Beth stood at the counter with her coat zipped. Out front, past the steam, Dane waited on the store dock. She had walked the north road in daylight to ask Tess to hide her, and she had put him on the one dock in the harbor the *Margaret Ann* could see from her mooring.

"Second one's Haskell's," Tess said, and knocked the ladle on its rim. "His phone lives on my shelf. Only place on the island it's a phone, and does he come up for it? He hasn't come off that boat since he brought you the diesel. I carry it down." She jerked her chin at the curtain.

Through the gap Beth could see the booster's antenna on its shelf and the charger cords hanging off it, and nothing else. The booster ran off the store generator. Inside about fifty feet of it a phone worked like a phone, and past the end of the dock it was a brick. Nothing went up the north road that Tess didn't hear about by supper, and two cans of diesel had gone up it Sunday.

"You keep Ashby off the net," Beth said.

Tess put the ladle down. "Friday night, after the bridge. A flashlight on your back steps. Ten seconds on the ice, and off." She held up her left hand, back out.

Across the knuckles, in ballpoint, under three days of other people's gallons and names: 22:40. Tess turned the log round on the counter and paged it back to Friday. *Calloway — home 22:40.*

"Twenty-two forty," Tess said. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home. I can see your steps from right here, Beth." She tapped the counter twice. "I didn't put you on the air."

The counter looked straight up the slope at Beth's back steps. It always had.

Tess wiped the counter round the log, hard, with her fingerless glove. "Sat on it since Friday, Calloway. And you let me find you by your smoke."

"Had my reasons," Beth said, and held out her hands for the near pot.

Tess didn't give it to her. "I walked you home from every dance on this island," Tess said. She put her hand down on the blankets. "You'll sleep at mine."

"Can't," Beth said.

Tess picked the ladle back up and went at the pot, brisk now, and brighter. "I know where every dollar on this island sleeps tonight, Calloway. Every gallon, every can, every cord of wood that's owed for. I'll know where you do."

"I'm not your money, Tess," Beth said.

"No. My money I can count," Tess said.

Tess set the ladle across the pot. "I put you to bed the winter your mother got on the Bangor bus."

A cot in Tess's back room, a wool blanket rough at her chin, and her own feet, nine years old, a foot short of the end of it.

"Then you know I don't go where I'm put," Beth said.

Beth put two fingers on the corner of the log to turn it forward to today's page, to see whether Ashby was on it.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does," Tess said, and shut it under Beth's fingers before a page could turn.

Through the top of the window Dane hadn't moved from where she'd put him. He stood on the dock facing the harbor, his back to the window, and the generator took every word before it reached him.

Tess took the two cot blankets off the counter and put them back underneath it, one and then the other, without looking up.

She lidded both pots, gathered them into her arms and came round the counter, and her hip was on the door and the bell already going. "Truck's out front. I'm going up anyhow, and Haskell's on the way back. Marshal can ride in the back with the cylinders. He's good at staying where he's put."

* * *

Near ten, Tess's chowder had come back to a simmer on the Ashby stove, and they ate it out of the Ashbys' good bowls by the light the generator made behind the sheets. Under the floor the hum came up through the boards into Beth's feet.

Dane stirred his bowl. "I've been through this twice looking for the clam," he said, "and I'm starting to think the clam was a rumor."

"It's mostly potato on purpose," Beth said.

The hum stopped.

The lights went out behind the sheets. In the window on the shed side, orange came up. Beth was at the window with the sheet bunched in her fist before her chair had finished falling.

Below the house the shed sat on its pad, already gone, fire out the door and up through the roof, and the light of it ran up the drive. In the drive, lights off, a shape stood against the glow. The plow frame. The rack. A dark cab. She had ridden up the north road in that cab at two with a pot between her boots.

Nobody was hiding anymore. They had come up the one road in a truck the whole island knew and left it where the fire would show it. She let go of the sheet.

Dane had the gun out and was at the other window. "I've got the shed and the truck, and I don't have the cab or anything past the bend in the drive or the trees on the left, which is where I'd be," he said. "Nothing's moved by the truck since the light came up. You stand at the pantry end of that wall, away from the window, and low."

"Back door," Beth said. "Water."

He looked round at her.

"Road's theirs," she said. "Bar's mine."

He came away from the window and took his jacket off the chair.

They went out the back and down onto the shore path along the ledge below the house, with the fire throwing their shadows ahead of them on the rock. Behind them something in the shed went with a bang. Beth didn't turn round.

The flood had come halfway up the bar. She went in without stopping, and the cold came in over the tops of her boots and filled them, and Dane came in after her, a stride inside her on the side away from the drop.

Half tide, knee-deep, the gut a stride to her left and his belt in her fist, and she wasn't thinking about the gut, and that was the most dangerous thing on the bar.

The cobbles knocking under the flood. The water working at the drop on her left. The fire far behind, a crack and a long sigh. His breathing, and nothing else from him.

Dane didn't talk. Nobody had told him to. It was the first time she'd known him quiet with something burning at his back.

Halfway a sea came in off the drop and slopped up her thigh, and she stopped dead. He stopped with her. She stood in it and listened to the water decide, and it drew back off the stones with a long suck, and she went on.

Ashby's light came over their shoulders and lay on the water ahead of them, red on black, so she could see the flood pour between the stones. Then the bar rose under her and the water let go of her knees, and the cove was in front of them.

The cove had gone back out of the fish house. The sill was wet under her hand when she found it, and the planks inside gave damp under her boots, and the one door faced the land behind them. Dark. Cold. Old bait.

She found the matches by feel on the shelf where her father had kept them, and knelt at the oil-drum stove she'd relined in September, and opened its door on the cold. She struck the match.

* * *

It caught on the first match. The kindling went up with a small fierce sound, and she fed it shavings off the bench and then two splits of her father's spruce and shut the door, and sat back on her heels. In a minute the drum began to tick. In five the cold had gone down to the floor and lay along the planks, and the walls began to give up their smell as the boards warmed, the pine tar coming out of them sweet and dark, and she shut her eyes and breathed it in and let it be hers.

She lit the lantern from a sliver out of the stove and turned it low.

They got their boots off sitting on the planks by the stove. Hers had filled at the bar and came off with a suck, and she tipped the cove out of them at the door. Then the wool, wrung and hung on the wire along the stovepipe, where it began at once to steam. They didn't talk. It was work. Dane wrung his second sock out over the coal hod and kept on wringing it, and when she looked over he opened it and peered inside, as if there might be a fish.

On the shelf over the bench her father's red Pelican case sat where it had always sat, square in the lantern light.

She sat on the cot. He sat on the floor with his back against it and his feet to the stove, and set the gun on the planks by his right hand. Ashby's smoke was in both of them, in her braid and the wool of her father's sweater. Close by her knee the gray had come in above his ear.

She wanted to ask him something. For once she knew what.

"Is it still a not yet?" she said.

He didn't answer straight off. The stove ticked. Then he turned so he could see her, one arm along the cot by her hip.

"Not anymore," he said. "When this is over, I report this."

She had been waiting since Friday for him to lie to her about something, and he had picked this to tell her straight.

"I know what you are," she said. "I'm choosing anyway."

He came up onto his knees by the cot, and his face was level with hers, and he kissed her. It was slow and it tasted of the chowder, and she put her hand on the back of his neck and kept him there.

His hand came to the hem of her father's sweater and stopped there. It waited. She could feel it waiting, the warm backs of his fingers against her hip through the wool, and she let it wait, and then she took his wrist and moved his hand under.

"Yes," she said. "You?"

"Yes," he said, against her mouth.

Then he stopped, and said, "Hold on," and went to his jacket on the nail. She heard the zip of the trauma kit. He went through it honestly in the lantern light, every pouch, and she lay back on the cot and listened to him do it: tape, gauze, shears, something that crackled, a tourniquet he set aside. He zipped it up again.

"It's a very disappointing kit," he said.

She laughed out loud. It came up out of her and filled the fish house, and he stood by the nail with the kit in his hand and let her, and that made it worse.

"Hands, then," she said. "And mouths. Come here."

He came back to her. She pulled the sweater off over her head herself and the shirt after it, and the cold off the planks came up over her bare back and shoulders, and the stove put its heat along her ribs, and she was two temperatures at once.

"Your shirt," she said. "Off."

He took off the fleece and the shirt under it and dropped them on the planks. The red tag rode on his bare wrist, where he'd slid it off the cuff when she gave him back the fleece.

Then his mouth was on her breast, and his hand on the other, and the stubble rasped her skin. She put her fingers in his short hair and held his head where she wanted it.

"There," she said. "Like that. Slower."

He went slower. His tongue went over her nipple and away and back, and her head was against the boards of the wall and her eyes were shut, and the stove ticked, and outside the cove slapped at the ledge, and she was working out how high the tide would come tonight and whether the sill would stay dry, and then she lost the sum halfway, and let it go, and she heard her own voice asking him for something and couldn't have said what, and she noticed she wasn't steering any of it.

She got her hands on his belt. Her fingers were stiff from the bar and the buckle beat her twice, and he laughed against her ribs, and she said, "Don't help," and he didn't, and on the next try it came.

He was hard in her hand. She closed her fingers, and he went still against her throat.

"Beth," he said. "Lie back?"

She lay back. The cot gave a long iron complaint, and she said, "It's my father's, it's allowed," and he kissed her through the laugh.

He got her jeans down, wet and stubborn, and hung them on the wire by feel without looking, which she'd remember. Then his mouth was on the inside of her thigh, and higher, and the callus across his palms was rough on the backs of her knees, and her own hard hands were in his hair.

She was wet and he found it out, and the sound he made went through her. On the wire their wool steamed. A coal shifted in the drum.

"Don't stop," she said, and then, because he was a man who did what she told him on a bar in the dark, "There. Stay there."

He stayed. His tongue went slow and then less slow, and there was the stove at her shoulder and the cold under the cot, and the red tag on his wrist dragging across her belly where his hand lay, the one thing about him she could check, and she didn't, and there was the tide, and Ashby burning across the bar, and she came with her heel hard in his back and her hand over her own mouth, and then took the hand away, because there was no one in the world to hear her out here, and let the sound go.

He came up the cot to her and she pulled him down alongside her and got her hand round him again.

"Show me," she said. He put his hand over hers and showed her, and then took it away and let her.

The man who had talked to her through the whole of Friday night had nothing to say now. He breathed, hard. "Like that?" she said, and he said "Yes," and it was the last word he had, and then he came in her hand with his face in her neck and a sound she meant to keep.

After, the stove ticked. She got the blanket off the foot of the cot over both of them, her father's, gray, and lay with her back to the drum and Dane along the front of her, and cold came up through the canvas from the planks. Ashby's smoke was in his hair where her face was. He was quiet a long time, and she kept her face where it was.

Then he asked it, in the voice he used for easy questions.

"Who had Ashby," he said, "besides me?"

* * *

"Tess saw my light on my back steps Friday night," Beth said. "She wrote it on her hand at twenty to eleven, and in her book, and kept it off the air."

The stove was down to coals, and she had turned the lantern almost out. Outside, the tide was near the top and working on the ledge under the seaward wall, the one she'd shingled in October, and the wind had found the landward wall she hadn't got to. Dane lay on his back beside her under the gray blanket and didn't move.

He waited, one hand open on his chest.

"I told Ned 'Home,'" she said. "Myself, at the *North Reach*'s stern, when you'd walked off to the road end. I didn't give you either one of them Friday night. I knew it when I said *you*."

Her thumb had found the hawser scar across her left palm and was going along it, end to end, and back. Ned on the stern of the *North Reach* in the sleet. Ned, who had run that boat since she was in diapers. She had put him in the same breath as Tess, out loud, to a man with a notebook, and the planks under her hadn't moved, and the cove went on working at the ledge, and nothing had happened except that she had said it.

There was a third thing. She didn't want to say it. She said it.

"Tess's truck. The keys hang on an open hook in her office, hers and the spare. Anybody at the counter could lift them, or anybody at the back door."

Dane turned his head toward her. "Three things you didn't count," he said, and made nothing more of it.

Under the blanket his hand found hers, the one with the scar, and stopped her thumb.

"Keep her name out of what you send," she said.

"I'll call it a store vehicle," he said. "No name."

His jacket hung on the nail with the notebook in it, and he didn't get up for it.

"And this place," she said. "My father's."

"I'll do what I can," he said.

She had heard men say yes. She slid her hand out from under his and put her thumb back on the scar.

He looked up at the roof boards. "Monday's the twenty-third," he said. "You're in Portland in front of the grand jury, and between here and there I'd like every day planned before we're standing in it. Which tide, which door, who's awake while you sleep and which wall I'm against while they are, because I've run out of houses, and I've got one shed left, and it's your father's, and I'd like to give it back to you with the roof on."

"Fish house," Beth said.

She sat up in the coal light, and the blanket came off her shoulders and the cold found them. She had been hiding since Friday. The cottage, with the doors shut and no lamp. The boat she was supposed to be on. Ashby, with Ned's key. Here. Every place she'd gone, somebody had known, and every time she'd gone somewhere smaller. She put her bare feet down on the cold planks.

"You like the plan first," she said. "Here it is. In the morning I go to the store and buy what I need, and I watch who watches me buy it."
