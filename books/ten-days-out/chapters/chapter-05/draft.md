# Chapter Five: Good Till Next August

Dane Reyes stood behind Beth at the island store at nine in the morning while she bought condoms in front of Tess Marr, two lobstermen and God.

She set the box on the counter like a can of beans. The lobstermen looked at it, then at Dane.

Tess rang it up and told the whole store. "Four seventy-nine. Good till next August."

"I'd have asked for a receipt," Dane said.

He could already see Cole's lawyer in a good coat, asking these two men slowly what the witness had bought on Saturday morning and who was standing behind her. He wasn't sorry.

Last night in the net loft she had put her cold bare feet flat on his shins, and this morning she was buying these for tonight. He wanted tonight: a bed, a door that locked, the whole night with her.

Beth counted out four dollars and change and came up a penny short. "You owe me," Tess said. Beth owed nobody anything, on purpose. Her mouth went flat. She put the box in the zip pocket inside her coat.

"Cole Varney lent me the money for my new fuel tanks," Tess announced, "and you just put my store under, Calloway."

Then she came around the counter and hugged Beth hard, eyes shut, cheek on her shoulder.

The lobstermen left, and the bell rang behind them.

Dane took the chair by the woodstove with its back to the wall. Beth took the other without being asked, then went for two coffees from the urn.

"Tell me about the call last night," Dane said.

Tess squared a rack of lighters by the register that was already square. "Screen said Dyer. A man, talking low, like he had his hand over it. He said send Russ, he could see his light from there."

"I sent Russ because the man said a tree was through the roof," she said. "What was I supposed to do, ask for ID?"

"Your marshal looks at my hands like he's pricing them," Tess told Beth's back.

From the Dyer house on the point you would look straight across the black water at the one lit porch on the harbor road. Somebody had sat in Mrs. Dyer's dark house, watched Russ on that porch, and picked up her phone.

"A man covers the phone when you'd know his voice," Dane said.

"Well, I didn't," Tess said, too fast.

Her cell phone lay face up on the counter. It buzzed, and she had it before the first buzz was done. "Not now," she said low, her back half turned.

Beth came back with the coffees. Tess hung up and turned the phone face down. Through the window Dane saw Russ on the steps, putting his own phone away.

At the door Dane bent close to Beth. "Her phone rang while Russ had his at his ear."

"Russ talks to Tess every day of his life."

He opened the door. Russ stood on the steps with snow on his shoulders.

"Summer house up the east road has its back window out," Russ said. "I saw it at five, looking for you."

* * *

Russ drove them up the east road, three across the bench seat. Beth's thigh was against Dane's, warm through two layers of denim, and he wanted nothing on earth so much as to put his hand on it. He kept it on the door handle, because his gun hand was the one beside her.

Dane watched the road. "Who'd you tell she was sleeping at your place?"

Russ answered before he'd finished. "Nobody. Who'd I tell?"

His eyes went to the mirror, down the hill to the store roof. Dane watched who a man looked at when he answered. Nobody said anything more.

Russ stopped below the house. Every window was shuttered, the snow lay smooth on the front steps, and smoke was coming off the chimney of a house that ought to be dead cold.

Dane was afraid, and he let himself know it. The man who had looked in Russ's window before he fired was somewhere close, and Beth was in this truck.

"Engine running, doors locked," he told her. "If you hear a shot, you drive to the store and you don't stop."

Beth was quiet. "Go."

Dane took the front, Russ the back with the shotgun. Dane talked low the whole way in so Russ would know where he was. "Kitchen. Stove's going. Front room's clear. Back bedroom's clear." The house was empty.

The stove was warm under his palm. Cans of stew, a phone charger coiled on the table, the dishes washed and stacked, wet wool socks hung in a neat row by the stove. A kitchen chair stood at the front window, facing down the road they had come up.

Out the back door, boot prints went down through the snow toward the shore: one clean print, then the long smear the other foot leaves, the edges already softening under new snow.

The man had sat in that chair and watched them come, and then walked out the back.

Russ stood at the sink, looking at the dishes. "He washed up," he said. "I don't wash up."

Dane turned. A square of newspaper was pinned with a thumbtack to the wall by the stove, and there was a face on it. He knew the face before he was close enough to see it.

* * *

Beth was in the kitchen doorway. She had heard no shot, and he'd been in too long, so she had come to look. He should have known she would.

She saw the picture.

It was Beth at thirty, in her orange work coat at the wheel of the ferry, on the day she got her captain's license. The island newsletter had run it. She went quiet. Her hand came up toward it and stopped an inch short.

Dane put his hand flat at the small of her back. She leaned into it and let it stay. He wanted to put his mouth to her hair, here, with a stranger's socks drying by the stove, and he didn't. He kept his eyes on the back door.

"I look like I'm being arrested," Beth said. Her voice wasn't right.

Then she looked properly: a brown ring from a coffee mug across one corner, and four old pinholes.

"That's Tess's. Off the board by her register. She put it up the week it ran. Three years it's been up there. I never look at it. I hate that picture. I don't know when it went."

"That's the kid off Cole's boat," Russ said, his eyes on the picture. "Worked the back of it two summers for cash, and lived in that trailer of Cole's out the east road. It's been empty since Thursday. Everybody called him kid. I don't think anybody ever asked him his name."

No name, no pay stub, no paper. "Then my office has nothing to run," Dane said.

"I could sit on this house," Russ said.

"He watched your truck come up the road. He won't come back to tire tracks at the door." He'd pick another shut house. The island had plenty.

Dane stood at the window, gun out. "Nobody hears where you sleep tonight. Nobody."

Beth faced him. "Russ sat on his porch with a shotgun in a storm. The whole road knew where I was, and anybody could walk into the Dyer house. The loft's found, and there's no food in it. I can't do nine more nights without a key and groceries. If Russ wanted me dead, he had me asleep in his kitchen."

Dane had nothing to say to that. "Fair."

She was rude to him and right, which was the thing about her he liked best.

Beth turned to Russ. "You've got the key to the north-side house."

Russ was already working it off his ring. She took it in her bare palm, over the white rope scar.

Dane kept his voice polite and slow. "Then my boss hears it too. And I'm asking for all three of their phones."

* * *

The store was nearly empty when they came back in the early afternoon, and the urn ticked on the woodstove. Tess was in the back room filling a box from Beth's list. Ned Haskell stood at the counter, big and stooped, with both hands around a mug of coffee.

Dane watched the hands. They were steady, a ferry captain's hands.

Beth told him straight. "We're at the north-side house tonight. The one Russ looks after."

"She'll blow till Monday, Bethie," Ned said. "Russ keeps that place tight." He pushed his coffee across the counter to her, and she took it. To Dane he said, "She'll feed you. She won't be nice about it."

He went. At the door he turned once and lifted a hand, and the bell rang behind him.

Tess came out of the back with the box: canned beans, onions, a stick of butter, candles.

"North-side house," Beth said. "Russ's. Food for two."

"I put in candles."

On the cork board by the register, among the notices, was a pale square with a pinhole at each corner.

Beth looked at it. "When did my picture go off your board?"

"First week of September," Tess said. "I figured some summer kid wanted the island's lady captain."

"It's on a wall up the east road."

Tess's hands stopped on the box. "Beth."

All day it had been Calloway. Dane heard the difference.

Out on the steps the snow came flat off the harbor, and his phone found one bar. He called his boss on speaker. Beth stood beside him with the box on her hip and Ned's coffee in her other hand.

"Twice in one day, Dane," his boss said.

"The north-side summer house, tonight. Three islanders know it: Tess Marr, Russ Whitcomb and Ned Haskell. I want the phone records for all three."

"Those three are mine, Dane," Beth said, to his face.

"Then the records will say so, and I'll say I was wrong in front of Tess."

"The carriers take three days," his boss said, and hung up.

Beth was leaning on his arm on the cold step while she fought him, and she didn't seem to know it. He did.

He put the phone away. "That's four people who know where you'll be tonight."

"Three of them would die for me."

"Somebody wanted your face on a wall in September."

"In September I hadn't seen anything."
