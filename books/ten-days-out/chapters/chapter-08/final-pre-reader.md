# Chapter Eight: The Tower

Beth Calloway was elbow-deep in a dead generator when Dane Reyes told her he was in love with her.

She had dragged it out onto the back step under the eave, with sleet slanting past. The summer people had shut its fuel off for the winter and left the spark plug black. She wiped the plug on her jeans and screwed it back by feel, and now the starter cord dragged across her skinned palms with every pull.

"Seven."

Dane stood in the doorway with his gun down along his leg, watching the tree line. She'd been quiet ten minutes, so he was talking.

"My boss may think we're both dead, and I can't tell her different until my phone finds a tower, so I'm rooting for you, and I'm very good at it."

"Eight."

"I'm in love with you." His eyes stayed on the trees. "You can write that down, since you're the one keeping track."

"Nine."

She pulled again. On ten it caught. The engine coughed, then held one steady note, and inside the house the summer people's refrigerator began to hum.

She kept her hands on the machine. "Ask me in five days."

Portland was seven days off. Five days meant she'd answer him before it was over, and she meant him to notice. From the way he breathed out, he had.

She wanted to drag him down onto the cold step in the sleet, and that annoyed her, because she had an engine to finish. She wouldn't say it back with her hands in a machine. She wouldn't say no either.

"How far does that carry?" he asked the trees.

"Not past the wind." She left it running.

Inside, the stove coil went red. They had one phone between them now; hers had drowned on the rocks when its bag split. He had already opened the one tin of ham and cut it as thick as books, as if the whole lighthouse was coming to lunch.

She looked at the pan. "That ham is load-bearing."

"I cook for an apartment building," he said, and fried all of it. "It's the one thing I do that nobody has to rescue."

She ate the first slab standing at the stove, too hot to hold, salt and fat after a night of seawater. She burned her mouth and didn't care.

She took the tower key off its hook and went out the door with a second slab in her other hand. Dane followed, shrugging into his coat, phone in his fist.

* * *

At the tower door Dane took the key from her, unlocked it and went up first, testing every step with his weight before he gave it hers. The iron stair ached through her gloves.

"I had a boat to see on Saturday," he said, "before your phone call."

"What does a marshal want with a boat?"

"A witness told me once that everybody who doesn't know what they'd do instead wants a boat." He kept climbing. "What do you want?"

"Ned's job. He retires in June. I've got the license and eleven years on that deck, and I've never asked him."

"Why not?"

"Asking means saying out loud that he's old. And that I want something."

"I dare you to ask him. Out loud, in a sentence with a verb in it, and if he says no, you can blame me, I'm used to it."

"Keep climbing, Marshal."

He went up the ladder ahead of her, and the back of his neck was right there, red with cold where his hair was cut short. She wanted her mouth on it.

He shoved the hatch open, and they crawled out on their hands and knees.

Up here the wind had nothing in its way. Ice coated the walkway around the top and the railing, and the railing came only to her hip. Past it, a long way down, was the rock.

Beth went quiet. Above them ice cracked off the lamp glass in a gust and skittered over the edge.

"Stop. Hold the rail."

He held it with one hand and lifted the phone with the other. It found a signal and started going off.

A gust came, and a sheet of ice let go under his boot. His new boots had soles meant for a dry store floor. He went sideways into the railing, and it caught him at the hip, which was too low, and his weight tipped on over it toward the drop.

She got her fist into the back of his belt. She sat down hard on the ice and jammed both boots against the foot of the railing, and she held, while the belt cut through her glove into her raw palm and his weight hung out over the rock and the wind leaned on both of them.

All she could see was his back, the gray past it, and her own arm, straight as a rope.

"I'm on the rail," he said, low. "I've got one hand, and I've got the phone, and I'm coming back to you now, slowly, and you don't let go, and I'm coming."

She held on.

When the gust eased she felt it, the way she felt a sea go slack before the next one. She hauled with her back and legs, and he came back over the railing and down onto his knees on the ice.

Her fist was still in his belt. The phone was still ringing in his hand.

* * *

He answered crouched at the foot of the lamp, out of the wind, the phone on speaker between their heads.

"Dane," his boss said, and then nothing.

"We're both alive," he said, "at the old lighthouse on the north point, and nobody else knows."

The line stayed quiet a little too long.

"The constable told me there were two people in the house that burned," his boss said.

"We went out a pantry window," Dane said, "and she went first, which will look great in my report."

"The wind may drop tomorrow," his boss said, "any time from noon to four. The hospital helicopter won't fly in icing, and neither will the Coast Guard's, not for somebody who isn't dying. The state's sending a police boat. They want somebody on the island radio who can see the beach and tell them when to come in. Whoever runs emergencies out there."

"That's Tess," Beth said.

"Where does it come in, Dane?" his boss said.

"The north beach, right under us," Beth said. "The pier's a mile of open road through the village, and no boat that size can lie there in a sea. I can be on that sand in ten minutes."

Below them lay the north beach: sand between rocks, trees along the top, a heap of rocks at one end. Far off, the village chimneys smoked. A man waiting for her would wait in those trees.

"Forty yards of bare sand, and the trees are the only cover," Dane said. "Those rocks at the far end are the one place the trees can't see. You stand there and nowhere else."

"The judge set Cole's hearing for Monday the twenty-third at two," his boss said, "to decide whether there's enough to keep him locked up. The grand jury has to hear her that morning, or the prosecutor says Cole walks. Call me with the hour." She was gone.

"Tess has to know," Dane said. "The boat can't come in without her. Give her the boat and the beach, nothing else, and ask her to sit on it."

"She'll sit on it."

"Ask her anyway."

She dialed from memory. Tess picked up before the first ring was done.

"Tess. It's me."

Tess came on loud.

"I drove the fire truck up to that north-side house last night, and it was burned to the cellar, and Russ walked the shore till two, and the sea had taken every track, and I sat in the cab and thought I'd be washing you for your coffin, Calloway."

Her voice went. "Beth."

Beth put her free hand flat on the cold iron and kept her eyes on the chimneys. "I'm here. I'm fine. A police boat's coming for me tomorrow, to the north beach, and they want you on the radio to bring it in. Sit on it. Nobody else gets it."

"I'll raise them on the radio at the store," Tess said, already moving. "Don't go anywhere, Calloway." She hung up.

They waited two minutes. Ice ticked off the lamp glass. His knee was against hers and her hand was still in his belt.

Tess called back.

"Three o'clock. Top of the beach road. I'll have the radio, and you owe me a fire truck's worth of gas, Calloway."

* * *

The light had gone flat. Beth reached for the phone again. Dane kept it.

"Right now the man with the gun thinks you're at the bottom of the bay," he said, "which makes this the safest you've been since Thursday. Every person you call can tell him you aren't, and once you're alive to them, you're alive to whoever they tell. Nobody else needs to know."

"Ned and Russ."

"Ned knew the north-side house. So did Russ, who said nobody before I'd finished asking."

Her fist was still in the back of his belt. She meant him to feel it every time he argued: she had held him over that drop, she wanted him, and she was going to make these calls anyway.

"You've still got my belt," he said.

"Keep arguing. I'll keep hold of it."

"They walked the shore all night looking for my body. Tess was crying on that phone. If I leave this island with them thinking I burned in that house, or I treat them like suspects to their faces, Cole has already taken them from me. I may not see them for a long time. Tess knows now. The island knows by supper."

"I can't argue with supper," he said. "I've met Tess."

"I know what it costs. I'm the one paying it."

He gave her the phone.

She dialed Ned with the wind in the microphone. "Ned, it's me. I'm alive. A boat's coming for me at three tomorrow, to the north beach, and I want you there to see me off."

Ned gave her the weather first. "Wind's going round to the north. It ought to lie down by tomorrow noon, or near it." Then: "I'll be there, Bethie."

The job question was right there in her mouth, and she kept it. "Not on the phone," she told Dane.

Then Russ. "Beth. I walked that shore till two." When she told him the hour: "Leave the harbor road with a gunman loose? Who'd watch it? Nobody."

Dane took the phone back. "You made three phone calls on a frozen walkway, and now everybody you love knows where you'll be standing at three o'clock. Tell me which one of them you'd like me to stop trusting first."

"None of them, Dane Reyes. Watching them is your job. Calling them was mine."

At the hatch he stopped. "He won't come up a stone tower with one door and me at the top of it. He'll wait for us to stand on open sand."

He called his boss back himself. "Three o'clock, the north beach. Tess Marr, Ned Haskell and Russ Whitcomb have it. She told them herself."

His boss said the names back slowly, one at a time, writing.

"Tess Marr. Ned Haskell. Russ Whitcomb."

Then a pause, with the wind in it.

"And me," his boss said.
