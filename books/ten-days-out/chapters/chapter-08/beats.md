### Scene 1
**Setting:** The keeper's house, the back step under the eave, late morning, Monday. Sleet slanting past the eave. The small generator Beth dragged out of the back hall onto the step, because nobody runs one indoors. Sensory anchor: the starter cord dragging across her skinned palms (Ch 07).
**Characters present:** Beth, Dane.
**Surface action:**
- Open on the outline's line, as written: *"Beth Calloway was elbow-deep in a dead generator when Dane Reyes told her he was in love with her."* Then go back no more than one beat to show what she's doing. She has found what the summer people did to it for the winter: the fuel shut off, the little spark plug black. She has the plug out, wipes it on her jeans and screws it back by feel. **She's counting her pulls out loud,** "Seven." "Eight." That sets up his line and turns the counting around: for once she's the one keeping count.
- Dane stands in the back doorway with his gun down along his leg, watching the tree line, not her. He's talking, because a long silence from her makes him talk. Give his reason in one line inside it (say it twice from Ch 07): the man thinks they drowned, and Dane's boss may think so too, and he can't tell her different until the phone has power and a tower.
- On "Nine," he says it. The line as it stands: **"I'm in love with you. You can write that down, since you're the one keeping track."** He keeps his eyes on the trees.
- She pulls again. It catches on ten. The engine coughs, then holds a steady note. Inside, the summer people's refrigerator starts humming.
- Her answer, with her hands still on the machine: **"Ask me in five days."** Her interior says plainly why five: it's less than the seven left until Portland, and she means him to notice. The reader must never be left guessing what a line means (the Ch 07 "He didn't tell her why" complaint).
- His phone goes on the charger. The stove coil goes red. He has already opened the one tin of ham and cut it as thick as books, as if the whole lighthouse is coming to lunch. She looks at the pan. **"That ham is load-bearing."** He fries all of it anyway. Two or three sentences for the pleasure: hot salt fat after a night of seawater, eaten standing.
- She takes the tower key off its hook in the kitchen. She's eating a slab of ham out of her other hand as she goes out the door.

**Subtext:** He says it now, in daylight, with his eyes on the trees, because he plans to put her on a boat and doesn't want to leave it unsaid. It's the morning answer to the question she didn't answer last night. Don't quote that line; one clause at most. She wants him (say it in her head, in plain words, in a new shape: she wants to drag him down onto the cold back step, and she's annoyed with herself, because she has an engine to finish). She won't say it back to a man the first time he says it, with her hands in a machine, on a day she's supposed to be dead. And she won't say no either.
**Tension source:** Interpersonal: a declaration, and no answer back. Situational underneath: the noise of the generator. She says it in one line: the wind will eat the sound on this end of the island.
**Ends on:** Motion. The key in her fist and the ham in her mouth, out the door toward the tower, and Dane behind her pulling his coat on. **No joke to close it.** The joke is already spent mid-scene.
**Word count target:** **380.** It's short on purpose (Flag 1). Don't pad it with a warming-up or a recap of the night.

**Avoid here:** a second sock joke (Ch 07 had the socks), the dating app, any mention of last night's sex, "Fair," a laugh count ("Three laughs now"), and Dane's father stories.

### Scene 2
**Setting:** The lighthouse tower, from the one door at the bottom to the walkway at the top. Inside: an iron stair winding up, then a short iron ladder, then a low hatch they crawl through on hands and knees. Outside: the iron walkway around the lamp, with a waist-high railing, glazed with ice. Use the domain-checked words only: "the walkway around the top," never "gallery" or "catwalk." Sensory anchor: iron so cold it aches through her gloves; at the top, wind with nothing in its way.
**Characters present:** Beth, Dane.
**Surface action:**
- Dane takes the key from her at the door, unlocks it, and goes up first. He tests each step with his weight before he gives it hers. (Competence, shown and not said.)
- **On the stair, climbing (motion, not a sit-down talk), the boat exchange in two lines of backstory, no more.** She asks why a marshal was looking at boats on a Saturday. He says a witness once told him that everybody who doesn't know what they'd do instead wants a boat. No name, no story. He goes on climbing. That is all the Marisol there is.
- Then he turns it on her, and she answers with her own want: the captain's job in June, when Ned retires. She has the license and eleven years, and she has never once asked Ned for it. One plain clause on why: asking means saying out loud that he's old and that she wants something. **He dares her to ask him.** Don't use "Dollar says" here; save the bet. He dares her in plain words, in his long sentence with a dry turn at the end. She doesn't answer the dare.
- **Longing, a new shape:** he's on the ladder above her, and she's looking at the back of his neck, and she wants him in plain words. Fold it into the climb in one or two sentences, no "he wanted X and didn't do X" pattern.
- The hatch. They crawl out onto the walkway. **Give the fear its beat before it lands.** Wind slams them. Ice coats the iron. The railing is lower than it ought to be. Below them the rock, a long way down. Beth goes quiet. She hears ice cracking off the lamp glass in the gusts before he does. In two or three words: "Stop. Hold the rail."
- Dane holds the phone up, hunting for the one bar. It finds it, and the phone starts going off in his hand: missed calls stacking up, two days of them, buzz after buzz.
- **The slip.** A gust, and a sheet of ice lets go under his boot. The new boots from the fudge store have soles meant for a dry showroom floor (this is the book's middle boots mention; say it plainly, no joke). He goes sideways into the railing, and the railing catches him at the hip, which is too low, and his weight tips out over it.
- She gets her fist into the back of his belt and sits down hard on the ice. She jams her boots against the base of the railing and holds. **Long sentences here,** cause before effect, so a listener can follow: the belt cutting into her raw palm, his weight, the wind, the drop.
- **Talk against silence, in action:** he talks the whole time, low, so she knows he's still there ("I'm on the rail. I've got one hand. I'm coming back to you, slowly, don't let go"). She says nothing. She holds on. She hauls him back the way she hauled him out of the sea, timing it to the gust easing.
**Subtext:** She has just told him the one thing she wants and has never asked for, and a second later she is holding his whole weight over a drop. He was risking his footing for a signal so he could get her a boat off the island.
**Tension source:** Situational (the ice, the height, the wind) and physical. Her silence and his talk are the contrast that makes the scene.
**Ends on:** A physical image. He's down on his knees on the ice with her fist still in his belt, and **the phone is still ringing in his hand.** He never let go of it. Don't add a line after.
**Word count target:** **420.** The slip itself gets at least 150 words in real time.

**Avoid here:** a flashlight, a shooter, and "the first time in her life." The jump in Ch 02 can't be mentioned (no callbacks). She doesn't pull her gloves off with her teeth (a tic). She keeps them on. It's iron, and she doesn't take them off for this.

### Scene 3
**Setting:** The walkway, on the side out of the worst of the wind, crouched against the base of the lamp. Below and to the north, the north beach: sand between rocks, open to the sea, trees along the top, a heap of rocks at one end. To the south, the whole island in the gray, and the village's few chimneys smoking at the far end. Sensory anchor: the boss's voice tinny on speaker, his phone held between their two heads against the wind.
**Characters present:** Beth, Dane. Voices: Dane's boss, then Tess.
**Surface action:**
- **A new shape for the call (Flag 2).** He answers on his knees, still out of breath, with Beth's fist still in his belt. It's the boss, and this time she called him. **She says his name and then nothing.** He has to fill the silence (he talks when he's scared): both of them alive, at the lighthouse on the north point, nobody else knows. The silence goes on one beat longer than it should. Then her first line, and it's new: **"The constable told me there were two people in that house."** Then she's back to business. Don't use "Is she alive?" (Ch 05 used it). Don't use "Dane" and a hang-up (the Ch 05 reader called that pattern predictable).
- **The boss's business in four lines** (the bible's "Lines as they now stand" and CASE FACTS, plain):
  1. The wind may drop tomorrow, any time from noon to four.
  2. "LifeFlight won't fly in icing, and the Coast Guard won't send its helicopter up from Cape Cod through icing for somebody who isn't dying." Gloss LifeFlight on first use as the hospital helicopter. So a Marine Patrol boat is the only thing that can come.
  3. "Marine Patrol wants somebody on the island radio who can see the beach and tell them when to come in. That's your emergency director." / Beth: **"That's Tess."**
  4. The judge set Cole's hearing for Monday at two. Gloss it: a judge decides whether there's enough to keep Cole locked up. And the clock restated: **"The grand jury has to hear her by Monday morning, or the prosecutor says Cole walks."**
- **Beth picks the beach herself, out loud, with her reason in one line:** the north beach, right below them. The pier is a mile of open road through the village, and with the float gone no boat that size can lie there in a sea, while she can walk down to this sand in ten minutes.
- **Dane reads the beach (competence, and the plant for Ch 09).** From up here he looks at it the way a man with a rifle would. The trees along the top are the only cover. Forty yards of open sand. The rocks at the far end are the one place on it that can't be seen from the trees. He says she'll stand there, at the rocks' end, and nowhere else. Two lines.
- **Dane on Tess, briefly:** she has to know, because the boat can't come in without her, so call her, give her the hour and nothing else, and ask her to sit on it. Beth dials from memory. This is a short disagreement, not the fight. The fight is S4.
- **Tess.** She answers before the first ring is done (her habit). Beth says her name. Then Tess, loud, then breaking: she drove the fire truck up to the north-side house last night and found it burned to the cellar; Russ walked the shore with a light until two, and the sea had taken every track. "I thought I'd be washing you for the box, Calloway." Then **"Beth."** Use the first name once, the serious one. Beth's answer is short. She's a bad liar and she doesn't try. Keep Beth's interior to her body: her hand on the cold iron, her eyes on the village chimneys.
- Tess takes the job at once, and loud: she'll get the Marine Patrol on the base radio at the store. She hangs up.
- They wait in the wind. **The two minutes are the beat.** Make them physical: ice ticking off the lamp, his knee, her hand still in his belt.
- The phone rings. Tess: the Marine Patrol says three o'clock; she'll be at the top of the beach road with the radio.
**Subtext:** The island has already buried her. Hearing Tess cry is what Beth will carry into the fight. For the first time in the book Dane hears his boss go silent, and he doesn't know what to make of it. Neither narration nor Dane interprets it. Beth only notices it.
**Tension source:** Situational (the cold, the waiting, a voice going silent on speaker) and emotional (Tess). The disagreement over Tess is small and settled in two lines, and it builds toward the fight in S4.
**Ends on:** Tess's practical callback line, in her own voice (headlines, prices, surnames). For example: **"Three o'clock. Top of the beach road. I'll have the radio, and you owe me a fire truck's worth of gas, Calloway."** Then the click. No interior line after it.
**Word count target:** **480.**

**Avoid here:** Tess hanging up fast in a way that reads as suspicious (Ch 05 already did that, and the reader called it "neon"). Here she hangs up because she's going to work. Tess gets no line about the September photo (Flag 7). No radio procedure words ("copy," "over").

### Scene 4
**Setting:** The same walkway, then the hatch. The light is going gray and flat, and the wind is up. Sensory anchor: wind roaring across the phone's microphone between calls, and her fist in the back of his belt, which she hasn't let go of since the slip, and knows she hasn't.
**Characters present:** Beth, Dane. Voices: Ned, Russ, Dane's boss.
**Surface action:**
- Beth reaches for the phone again. Dane holds it a second before he gives it to her. **The fight, face to face, on the iced walkway.** Both of them are partly right. It's adult, and nobody wins clean.
  - **Dane, the cost, in plain words** (the caller's instruction): right now the man with the gun thinks she's at the bottom of the bay, which makes this the safest she has been since Thursday. Every person she calls is someone who can tell him she isn't, and once she's alive to them she's alive to whoever they tell. Tess had to know. Nobody else does.
  - **He argues against each call by name, evenly:** Ned knew the north-side house. So did Russ, and Russ told Dane "nobody" in his own truck. That's a reason, not an accusation, and it makes Russ's the sharper charge, as Flag 6 requires.
  - **Beth, in her own words, out loud, to his face:** they walked the shore all night looking for her body. If she leaves this island with them thinking she burned in that house, or treats them like suspects to their faces, Cole has already taken them from her. She may not see them for a long time. And once Tess knows, the island will know by supper anyway. Give her no list of why each of them is innocent. Ch 06 did that, and the reader called it rehash.
  - **Both partly right, and both of them say so:** she says she knows what it costs, and she's the one paying it. He can't refute the supper line, and he says that too. Don't use "Fair" (a tic).
  - **The longing inside the fight:** she keeps her fist in his belt the whole time, and she means him to feel it. Say it plainly in her head. This is new: earlier chapters used "she hadn't noticed; he had," and Beth knows exactly what her hand is doing.
- **She calls Ned.** Ned answers the phone on his own. His voice: the weather first, slow. Then: **"I'll be there, Bethie."** That's the whole of it: two lines, the same number Russ gets. He asks no questions. The narration doesn't linger on any pause, and Beth has no interior thought about him. She has the job question in her mouth and doesn't ask it. One clause in her head and one line to Dane: **"Not on the phone."** That sets up Ch 09, where she asks on the beach.
- **She calls Russ.** He answers a question with a question and answers it himself, his habit: he can't leave the harbor road with a gunman loose. "Who'd watch it? Nobody." Two lines. Ch 11 explains where he really is.
- **Dane's angry line** (bible angry line 4, walkway wording), after the last call: **"You made three phone calls on a frozen walkway, and now everybody you love knows where you'll be standing at three o'clock. Tell me which one of them you'd like me to stop trusting first."** Beth's answer is short and bossy. Write it fresh, along these lines: none of them, because watching them is his job, and calling them was hers. Don't give a long reply.
- **At the hatch** (B1, the outline's line, to her): **"He won't come up a stone tower with one door and me at the top of it. He'll wait for us to stand on open sand."** It makes plain why tonight in the keeper's house is safe and tomorrow on the beach isn't.
- **Dane reports the change at once, in front of her.** He calls the boss back himself, which is a different shape from the first call: he places it, and he's brief. Three o'clock, the north beach. Tess Marr, Ned Haskell and Russ Whitcomb have it. She told them herself. Beth hears every word. This is his honesty rule on the page: he reports the cost to his own side the minute it's paid.
- **The boss repeats the names slowly, writing them down,** one at a time. Then she adds a name.
**Subtext:** Beth is choosing love over safety, with her eyes open. Dane lets her make the call while hating it, the opposite of what he'll do in Ch 13 (bible character flag 1: this is the "before"). The boss putting herself on the list is either a professional being exact or something else. Nobody on the page interprets it.
**Tension source:** Interpersonal, their hardest fight yet. Situational: every call makes her findable. The boss's last words add a new unease.
**Ends on:** The boss's voice, writing: **"Tess Marr. Ned Haskell. Russ Whitcomb."** A pause. **"And me."** The line stays open, with the wind in it. **This is the chapter's new question:** why does the one woman Dane trusts put herself on the list? It leaves the reader holding four live suspects going into the beach (Ch 09: "So it's one of your three, or it's one of mine"). The outline's promise stands under it: three o'clock tomorrow, and will she be on that boat? **Add no line, gesture or interior after "And me."**
**Word count target:** **560.**

**Avoid here:** "polite and slow" as a description of Dane's anger (a recycled tag), and "Ms. Calloway" more than once (it's better not used). No narrated question at the end. The boss doesn't just hang up. Russ's cap doesn't go off and on. No callback to earlier chapters' lines beyond the one B1 line.

- Callbacks quoting earlier chapters (the coat in the snow, "you don't get to decide what I can hold," the twelve people from Maine, Cole's lawyer, the penny). One quiet nod to last night's unanswered question in S1 is the limit.
- Strings of one-word orders. Beth's "Stop. Hold the rail." is the only short burst in the chapter, and it's earned by fear.
- Both leads joking with the same rhythm. Beth's jokes are flat verdicts, conclusion first ("That ham is load-bearing."). Dane's are long sentences that turn dry at the end, at his own expense. If a quip could belong to either of them, it's wrong.
- A button line at the end of every scene. The four endings above are assigned so no two match.

## Rhythm note for the writer
The reader has called the book a metronome twice. **Long sentences are required** in three places: the slip (S2), Beth's interior during the fight (S4), and Tess on the phone (she runs on). Short sentences go only where fear cuts them (the hatch, "Stop. Hold the rail."). Let Dane's talking during the slip run on "and," in present tense.
