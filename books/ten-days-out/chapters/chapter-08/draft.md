At the top he stopped. The harbor lay below in first gray, pewter scored with white, and at the stone pier the *North Reach* had her work lights on, orange through the rain. He was already going down toward them.

*Day 8, first light*

Ned knocked twice on the companionway door. A big man's knuckles, unhurried.

"Wait," Beth said.

She unlatched the outside door, the one to the steel ladder, and kicked the wedge under it. The wind came in cold off the harbor and lifted the corner of the chart. The Icom was zipped in its bag inside her float coat. She took the fixed set's mic off its clip and backed toward the open door until the coiled cord ran out straight, and held it in her right hand with her thumb off the key. Sixteen. If he came across the room at her, the Coast Guard would hear it, and half the bay.

"Come in."

His key went into the lock. The door opened on wet wool and the burnt coffee from the pot below, and Ned stopped one step inside.

He looked at the mic. Then at the open door behind her, the ladder, the gray beyond it. His finger went up to his ear.

"Leave it on."

He lowered his hand.

"He doesn't always run his unit," she said. "I had my palm on that box. The panel was dark. And the Ashby shed. Transfer switch, three years of invoices, a sill gone to sawdust, ask Walt. Nobody asked you about the shed."

He put one hand flat on the chart table, the whole spread of it, as if the table had moved.

"A year," he said. "About. Unit off, weights wrong. Sternman from away loading, never the same one twice."

"What did they pay you?"

"Not a damn dime." The hand pressed down. "That dealer's the night freight. Night freight's the subsidy. No subsidy, no winter boat." He didn't say the rest. She had grown up on what the rest was.

"You sent me home."

"Tried to." He took the rag off the console and wiped the forward glass, hard, and left a gray smear the width of his arm. "I'd have worked it short."

She kept the mic where it was. "The Ashby place. Who did you tell?"

His face went dark red above the beard. "I told nobody where you were. Nobody but Tess. Tess doesn't count."

She didn't answer.

The fixed set hissed. Ned had never once in her life filled a silence, and he filled this one.

"She gets everything. Always has. Supper, then I go home." He folded the rag into a square and opened it again. "She's been running that pump after dark. For the loan. I knew. Her business." The rag went over the glass. "She's scared, Bethie. Since the storm she's scared all the time. I thought it was the loan."

He stopped wiping.

"Sleeps down the store now. Back office, in her chair by that booster, light on. Every night this week, sitting by that phone." His voice went lower. "I thought she was waiting on you to call. Morning net, she ticks your line. Every day since the lines went, and you've not called once."

Beth looked at the smeared glass. Through it the store was a bar of yellow across the harbor.

Ned went past her without being asked, down the companionway, and she heard his boots on the steel treads and a mug knock below. He came back up and set it on the chart table by her left hand, and stepped back to the door.

She didn't pick it up. She reached over and clipped the mic onto its hook.

He watched it go on. His finger went halfway to his ear and stopped there, and the coffee steamed by her hand.

He left it on.

*Day 8, twenty to seven*

Under her boots the bilge pump kicked in, and the deck plates hummed with it.

Beth took the Icom out of its freezer bag and switched it on. She turned the knob off sixteen, click by click, to nine.

Nobody. Their old channel. Tess on the store's set, Beth fourteen on the *Ruthie C*: *Tell your father his coffee's on, two sugars.*

She counted. At eleven she turned it back to sixteen. She hadn't keyed it.

On Saturday a phone had rung in the back office when no phone on the island could ring. *The booster's on the generator.* That had been true.

The spiral log open on the counter. *Calloway — home (phone).* The round hand. The first name the back door came for.

The green ledger. *Island Girl,* 3:52, then white lines to the foot of the page, and the float lit at nine with a red hull alongside. *Cash after hours isn't a crime.* That had been true too.

*Local emergency management.* She had split kindling and pictured a county desk in Pruett. On Cutter, local emergency management was a woman who'd been slow putting her hand down at town meeting and laughed about it at the register.

The bank bag. Her paycheck in it every other Friday with the island's deposit slips, and the seal tag through the zip pull with *TM* on it in green marker. She had read *via store bank bag* in his hand and gone straight past it to Saturday. Only one pair of hands closed that bag.

And Tuesday. Tess's hands on the register, on the ledger, holding the box out across the counter. Not once on Beth's arm. Not a tug at her braid. Tess had touched her every day of her life.

*Secondary location, B's family property, not disclosed.* He'd kept the fish house out of his own book. Every place that went up his phone had come back as a man with a gun. The one place that went nowhere had had nothing come to it in four days but weather. If it had been him, the fish house would have burned first.

It had never been the book. It was the counter, and Ned's supper, and the one phone that rang.

The day her mother left, Tess drove the store truck onto the boat with Beth in the passenger seat, nine years old, and at Pruett bought her a whoopie pie and didn't make her talk the whole way home.

Beth sat down in the helm chair because her legs decided it. She picked up Ned's mug. The coffee had gone cold. She drank it anyway, all of it, then switched the Icom off and bagged it.

Through the smeared glass a figure came down the pier road. One arm held against his ribs where she'd taped it. A bag on the other shoulder. He walked the line of the pier wall first, the way he took any space, then turned for the gangway. She knew him by the walk before the face.

He stopped at the head of the gangway. Below her the deckhouse door banged open, and Ned stepped out onto the aluminum brow and stood in his way.

*Day 8, seven o'clock*

From the top of the wheelhouse ladder she watched the two of them.

Ned stood in the middle of the brow in his oilskin with his arms folded. Each sea worked the boat against the fenders and shifted the aluminum under him, and it rang, and he rode it without unfolding them. Dane had stopped at the pier end, his gun holstered. He looked at Ned's hands once, then past him, up at the wheelhouse. At her.

He lifted his good hand, open, and waited.

The Iridium went off in his jacket.

He took the call there on the open stone, in the wind, with his eyes on her. She couldn't hear a word. She watched him listen. Then he turned his head and looked across the harbor at the store, the only lit building on the island, and held on it, and turned back.

She didn't need the word. She'd had it for twenty minutes.

"Let him on," she said.

Ned unfolded his arms and stepped down onto the deck by the gate, and stayed there. Neither man spoke as Dane came past.

In the crew mess wet gear steamed on the heater rail. Ned's green wool jacket lay balled at the end of the settee where he'd slept. Beth sat at the steel table, and Dane set the go-bag down and sat across from her.

She held out her hand. He took the notebook from his inside pocket and gave it to her, and she put it on the table under her palm.

"Who told you to write me down?"

"Quist asked. Pike ordered. Saturday." He didn't look away. "*She doesn't know* was you."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Telling you could be called coaching. Coaching ends your testimony."

"The money."

"Pike's office gave me the emergency management name at seven. Teresa Marr." He said all of it, the way it would go in the record. "The bag went from her hands to Ned's to the credit union. The teller found it under your slip. Under her tag."

Her hand stayed on the cover.

"I wrote *no indication* Sunday night. I believed you from then on."

"Sunday. The shed."

"Quist wanted the money asked about. I wanted to know you anyway." His eyes stayed on hers. "Both were true. One went in the book."

"Wednesday's line. What was under it."

He didn't pretend not to understand. "*Subject and I.*" He stopped. "That's as far as I got. I wasn't putting you in Quist's book that way. I said it out loud this morning, to Pike."

"You could have told me Saturday."

"The last time a woman asked me for something off the plan, I gave it to her. She died in a parking lot." His voice stayed level. "I didn't bend one after that."

She looked at the black tape on his sleeve. She didn't ask what the call had cost.

He told her anyway.

"I reported that you read the log. I reported us. Both by name. I'm off this detail when relief lands, and there'll be a referral to internal affairs." He set his good hand on the table. "It's in the record now. Moreau has to hand it to the defense. A lawyer can stand up at trial and put it to you under oath. I'd rather you get asked than have it found."

His name at seven matched hers at twenty to. He'd paid before she could ask the price. Beth took her hand off the notebook and slid it back across the steel to him.

Her left palm stayed on the table between them, scar up. His hand came across and stopped an inch short of it, and waited. She held his eyes and didn't look away.

Ned filled the doorway.

"Somebody's got to get Russ," he said. "I can walk the Harbor Road and nobody looks twice."

"The man who shot me is on this island with a radio," Dane said. "He knows your face."

"Nobody shoots the ferry captain on his own road in daylight."

"He's right," Beth said.

Ned took the green jacket off the settee, shook it out and shrugged into it. His boots went along the deck and onto the brow, and the aluminum rang under each one, and then the pier took them and the ringing stopped.

The notebook lay on the table between them. Neither of them picked it up.

*Day 8, one o'clock*

She woke in the lower bunk to gray light moving on the overhead. Two hours. The porthole on the outboard side looked straight at the pier's granite, wet and a few feet off, and above it a slot of sky ran past fast and pale.

*Day 8, half past three*

The glass had come up four since noon. Beth tapped it with one knuckle on her way past and went to the forward windows.

The cold came off the glass now that the wind had left it. Out on the harbor the northwest was laying the chop flat in long streaks. Across the water the store's windows were lit, and the *Margaret Ann* sat on her mooring with her deck lights burning again.

"They have to get to me before Sunday's boat," she said. "Once your relief lands tomorrow, they've lost. They know it."

Dane stood by the lighting panel on the aft bulkhead. "Go on."

"By morning the sea's down, and Arsenault can sail. The island end of it walks off with him. Whatever I say on nine gets passed on. It has to be my voice. Not Ned's. Not yours. Nobody else's would be believed."

"Saying what?"

"That I'm alone. On the boat. Tonight."

"Why the boat?"

"It's steel. I know it blind and they don't. Every ladder, every bin, every binder." She looked aft through the glass, down at the deck going blue in the last light. "From the foot of this ladder to the stern gate is thirty-four steps. I've walked them in the dark for eleven years."

"And?"

"And I pick the ground. Not them."

He didn't raise his voice. "You're the witness. Pike says I'm still the detail, and I'm taking her at her word. We lock that door with both of us behind it. Relief's here by noon." He held her eyes. "The last woman who asked me for something against the plan got it."

"If we sit tight and he sails, there's one person left on this island who can put him away." Beth looked across the water at the lit windows. "Tess. She's in that store tonight with whoever's in it with her. You think he sails and leaves her behind to talk?"

He didn't answer.

"Waiting kills Tess." She said it flat. "And this way you don't get it from Ned or a phone log. You get it from her mouth."

He looked across the water at the store.

"Pike hears it from me first," he said. "Before anything happens. Not after."

"Then go tell her."

Far off down the pier road two small figures were coming in the last light, one big and stooped, one wide in a ball cap.

Dane took the Iridium out of his jacket and went out the door onto the wheelhouse wing. The northwest wind took his collar. He turned his face up to the clearing sky, put the phone to his ear and began to report.

Inside the glass, Beth took the Icom out of its freezer bag and set it on the chart table beside the fixed set, its knob still on sixteen.
