**Warning first:** I would keep reading, but this is the chapter where I stopped trusting that Dane can do his job, and where the book's patterns started to show through the story.

---

## Where I got bored

- **The opening generator.** "She pulled again. On ten it caught. The engine coughed, then held one steady note." This is the fourth time I've watched Beth bring a dead machine back to life: Russ's generator choke (Ch 3), the loft heater (Ch 4), the keeper's generator that failed (Ch 7), and now this one. Ch 4 already told me "A dead machine, starting under her hands. Nothing in the world was better." Fine, I get it. This time it's just furniture for the "I love you" line, and I was waiting for the pulls to end.
- **The boss's logistics speech.** "The wind may drop tomorrow, any time from noon to four. The hospital helicopter won't fly in icing, and neither will the Coast Guard's, not for somebody who isn't dying. The state's sending a police boat..." It reads like a briefing being read to me. Then a second chunk about the hearing at two. My attention drifted there, on a frozen walkway right after a near-fatal fall.
- **The second phone argument.** "Ned knew the north-side house. So did Russ, who said nobody before I'd finished asking." I had this argument in Ch 5 ("Those three are mine") and again in Ch 6 (the "would never hurt me" list). Ch 8 replays it a third time with the same moves. Dane objects, Beth says they're hers, Dane gives in. Nothing new is learned until "And me."

## Where I got confused

- **"I cook for an apartment building."** I read it twice. Does he mean he always cooks too much? Did he grow up cooking for a building? It doesn't land, and "It's the one thing I do that nobody has to rescue" comes too quickly after it to clear it up.
- **"Ask me in five days."** Portland is seven days off, and the narration tells me "Five was less than seven, and she meant him to notice." I still don't know what five days is. Is it before the grand jury? Is it some private deadline? The narration explains that it means something without telling me what.
- **Cole is locked up?** "The judge set Cole's hearing for Monday the twenty-third at two, to decide whether there's enough to keep him locked up." I didn't know Cole had been arrested. In Ch 2 the truck was clean and the case was only "her eyes." If he's in custody, who is running the kid? Is it the insider? If so, say so. Right now I'm unsure about the basic situation.
- **"The constable told me there were two people in the house that burned."** How is Russ talking to Dane's boss? Dane said he reports to her and nobody else. Did Russ call the Marshals himself? Did the boss call him? This feels like a clue, but I can't tell whether it's a clue or just loose plumbing.
- **The boss still has no name after eight chapters.** She's a suspect now, and she's still just "his boss." Either that's deliberate or it's a gap. Either way it bugged me.
- **Why does Tess have to be told at all?** Dane's phone works at the top of the tower. The state boat could be talked in by the marshal standing on the beach. "Whoever runs emergencies out there" reads like the plot's way of forcing Beth to tell an islander.

## Where I stopped believing

- **Dane lets her broadcast the extraction.** He says, correctly, that being thought dead "makes this the safest you've been since Thursday." Then, a page later, he hands her the phone, and she tells all three suspects the exact place and hour she'll be standing on open sand. He gives in after one speech about supper. He did the same in Ch 3 (going into the house), Ch 5 (the key, "I hate it, and it's fair") and now here. His pattern is to object, say "Fair," and give in. A marshal with forty-one witnesses would not do this. I'd rather he lost the argument because he couldn't stop her than because he agreed.
- **Dane falls again.** In Ch 2 he jumps badly. In Ch 4 his boots go on the ice. In Ch 6 he warns her he's "terrible at this." In Ch 7 the wave takes him. In Ch 8 he goes over the railing, and the boots get the blame again: "His new boots had soles meant for a dry store floor." The boot joke was charming in Ch 1. By Ch 8 he has needed rescuing so often that "I'm very good at my job" reads as irony the book doesn't intend.
- **Why is he standing on an iced gallery with a railing at hip height, holding the phone up with one hand?** Beth says "Hold the rail," and he holds it with one hand. The fall feels arranged so she can catch him.
- **The belt.** She keeps her fist in the back of his belt through the boss call, the Tess call, the argument and three more phone calls. That's several minutes of crouching. Then the narration explains it for me: "She meant him to feel it every time he argued: she had held him over that drop, she wanted him, and she was going to make these calls anyway." I would have believed the gesture. The explanation is what made it feel staged.
- **No reaction to "I'm in love with you."** In the same scene she saves his life by the belt. Then nobody says anything about either thing. They go straight to the phone. Maybe that's restraint, but it reads as if the book forgot the declaration happened.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **The chapter opening formula.** "Beth Calloway was elbow-deep in a dead generator when Dane Reyes told her he was in love with her." Every chapter opens the same way: full name, an action, then a twist. Ch 2: "Beth Calloway woke wanting the marshal..." Ch 3: "Dane Reyes lost the argument..." Ch 4: "Beth Calloway ran Dane Reyes..." Ch 6: "Beth Calloway was frying onions... when she smelled smoke." Ch 7: "Dane Reyes did exactly what she did, right up until..." It's eight for eight. I noticed it at Ch 4. By Ch 8 I could predict the first sentence.
- **Exact repeated reassurance lines.** "I'm coming back to you now" appears here and also, word for word, in Ch 3. Dane narrating his position out loud ("I'm on the rail... I've got one hand... I'm coming") is a tic from Ch 2's "I'm right here, Beth. I'll keep talking," Ch 3's "I'm still here" and Ch 4's "Still here." It was a character trait. Now it's a macro.
- **Counting as a motif.** Her pulls are counted ("Seven." "Eight." "Nine."). He says "You can write that down, since you're the one keeping track." Earlier the book counted witnesses ("Forty-one"), kisses ("One. I'm keeping count"), laughs ("That's one laugh," "Two laughs now"), and seconds ("He counted. He counted everything."). It's too much.
- **"She wanted" statements.** "She wanted to drag him down onto the cold step in the sleet." "She wanted her mouth on it." This book states desire flat, in nearly every chapter, from both heads ("He wanted her," "She wanted him," "I want you"). Once was bold. Now it's wallpaper.
- **Over-explained beats.** "Five was less than seven, and she meant him to notice. From the way he breathed out, he had." and "She wouldn't say it back with her hands in a machine. She wouldn't say no either." Both lines tell me what the dialogue already showed.
- **Neat kicker endings on every scene.** "Her fist was still in his belt. The phone was still ringing in his hand." Then "you owe me a fire truck's worth of gas, Calloway." Then "And me." Every section ends on a button. The book never lets a scene simply stop.
- **The jokey safety line in danger.** "I dare you to ask him. Out loud, in a sentence with a verb in it, and if he says no, you can blame me, I'm used to it." Dane's banter is all run-on, self-deprecating and clause-stacked. So is "which will look great in my report" and "which tells you about my day" in Ch 7. It's one rhythm, used over and over.

## What I want to know next

- **"And me."** Is the boss the leak? Or is she just being scrupulous about the list? This is the strongest pull in the chapter, and it's the reason I'd turn the page.
- **Which islander tells the kid?** Ned (afraid in Ch 1, "you didn't see it," gives the weather "first"), Russ (answers "nobody" too fast, now won't come to the beach, and apparently talked to the boss), or Tess (her picture, the loan, the phone that rang when Russ's did, and now she controls the radio and the boat's timing). Tess picking "Three o'clock" herself makes me most suspicious of her.
- **Ned can't swim and won't wear a life jacket.** That was planted in Ch 2, and he's coming to a beach in a sea. I'm braced for it.
- **Will Beth ask Ned for the job?** "Not on the phone." I'd like to see that conversation, and I'm afraid it's set up to happen just before something terrible.
- **Who is the kid?** Nobody knows his name. I want one.
- **Does she ever answer "I'm in love with you"?** I'm curious, but less than I should be. The romance has had its consummation, and the "I'll lose you" question from Ch 7 is the only tension left there.

## Best moment

Tess on the phone:

> "I drove the fire truck up to that north-side house last night, and it was burned to the cellar, and Russ walked the shore till two, and the sea had taken every track, and I sat in the cab and thought I'd be washing you for the box, Calloway."
>
> Her voice went. "Beth."

It's one breathless sentence from someone who isn't a lead, in a voice nobody else in the book has. "Washing you for the box" is specific, regional and awful, and switching from "Calloway" to "Beth" does more emotional work than any of the stated feelings in the chapter. It's also the only moment that made me feel what Beth loses if one of these people is the traitor.

Runner-up: "That ham is load-bearing."
