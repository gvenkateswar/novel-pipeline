"Ned's phone works in one place on this island," Dane said. "Fifty feet round the store. The back step is inside the fifty feet, and Ned has his own key to the door beside it."

The chart table was cold through her sleeves. Out past the town landing the *Margaret Ann* lay to her new mooring with her bow square on the float, and nobody moved on her deck.

"County rang Tess Wednesday," Beth said. "The landing and the hour. She told nobody."

"The county rings its emergency director so that one person on the island knows and keeps quiet about it," he said. "Keeping quiet is the job they gave her."

"Town ring hangs by her safe. Second key to the float box is on it."

"And it hangs where anybody behind that curtain can lift it off the nail, which brings us round to who goes behind the curtain."

"Arsenault stood at her counter and said a store like this lives on the boat. Tess laughed."

"That's a threat."

"To Tess." She looked down at the after deck, open to the harbor a step below the rail, nothing across the stern but the chain. "Somebody tells them where I am tonight."

In the lee of the pier wall one line of his footprints still came down the road to the riprap. He had walked every step of it in the open at four in the morning, with the arm.

"You read water better than anyone I've met," he said. He waited until she looked at him. "Who has a key when she's out?"

"Ned."

He said the next thing quieter. "This isn't water."

Ned's flashlight came aft along the rail below the windows and stopped under them. She could have looked down. She turned her back on that window and pulled the chart across the table to her.

"Not Ned," she said.

"Ned sends Russ along the riprap after dark."

"Don't give Haskell the night," Dane said. "If you want Russ on the riprap, give Russ the order yourself and give Haskell nothing at all."

She had read his book, every night of it down to the two words at the foot, and she wasn't taking his read on anyone yet.

"Ned's the boat," she said.

She took the notebook out of her coat and opened it at a clean page past his last line, and drew the pencil out of the spine. The point was worn down to the wood. She got out the knife she had scored the ham with and shaved the pencil toward her over the chart, and the curls dropped gray onto the soundings. She wrote *Teresa Marr* in the square capitals the island school had taught her, and she pressed so hard on the first R that the lead went thick and shone, and all the way through the second word she was thinking of Tess's left hand blue to the wrist with other people's hours, and of Tess with an arm through hers against the cold, walking her home up the Harbor Road from every dance and every wake on the island since she was fourteen, and she finished the last R and lifted the point off the page.

She turned the book round and pushed it across. "For Pike."

He didn't pick it up. He turned the book back so the name faced her, and held the pencil out to her across it, rubber end first.

"It's pencil," he said.

She put both hands in her coat pockets.

"Wind's backing, and the rail's making ice already," Ned called up the outside ladder. "I'll double that stern line before I go up for supper."
