# Chapter Nine: The North Beach

A lantern came along the shore at half past two, low and swinging, and Dane had his gun on it before he could see whose hand it was in.

Beth was behind him at the far end of the north beach, where he had put her. At one o'clock he had watched the tree line from the walkway around the top of the lighthouse, and from up there he had found the one place on the beach the trees couldn't see, behind the rocks at that end. He had brought her down to it along the shore.

The swell sucked in under the rocks and went quiet, and came again, slow now that the wind was down.

The lantern came up onto the first iced rock: a big stooped man in a green wool jacket, slow on his bad hip, a lantern in one hand and a long pole with a hook in the other.

"It's Ned," Beth said.

Dane kept the gun where it was. "Afternoon, Ned. Lean that pole on the rock and open your jacket for me, slowly, because I can watch your hands or I can watch the trees, and I'd rather be watching the trees."

"Dane. He's sixty-four."

The gun didn't move. Ned leaned the boat hook against the rock and set the lantern down beside it. He opened the old green wool. Under it was a sweater and nothing else. Dane watched his hands before his face. They were big and red and empty, and they shook a little.

"Your marshal's got that thing on me, Bethie."

"He had it on a seagull this morning."

"The seagull knows what it did," Dane said, and lowered the gun.

Ned buttoned up. "Swell's still heaving in under these rocks. Somebody'll need to push that boat off them with a pole, or it'll come in and stay."

Beth stood up straight. Dane saw her get ready, the way she got ready to throw a heavy rope.

"I want your job in June, Ned. I'm asking for it."

Ned looked at the sea. "Sea's going down." Then he looked at her. "It's yours. I've had the letter to the board written since the day you got your license. I was waiting on you to ask."

Beth's face went red under the wind-burn, and for a second she didn't seem to know what to do with her hands. Dane let her hold it. It was the last good thing that afternoon.

"Then you'd better hear the rest," Ned said, "since you're going."

He didn't look at Dane. "Two years ago Cole came to me. He said he had lobster for restaurants that had to cross at night. So after you'd gone home, I took the boat back over alone. He paid cash. He said, don't you open that truck, Ned. And I never did. Two years I ran his trucks across in the dark and asked nothing."

"You knew," Beth said.

"I guessed. My boy in Portland is on that stuff, Bethie. So I told myself it was lobster, because I couldn't stand for it to be the other thing."

"That's why you said I didn't see it."

"I didn't want you to have seen it."

Beth stood very still. Then, quietly: "Two kids, Ned. I taught one of them to row."

She hit him in the chest with both fists, once. He took it, and he didn't step back.

Dane had watched Ned's hands the whole time, and they had stayed open. He thought it through once, fast and plain. A man who had taken Cole's money for two years had known about three o'clock since yesterday afternoon.

He said it to her face, with Ned there to hear it.

"If he sold the hour, they're in those trees now."

* * *

Dane had a hand on her shoulder and was already putting her down behind the biggest rock at the end.

"If I send this boat away," he said, low, to her face, "there won't be another before the next storm blows out."

No helicopter would come up through icing for a woman who wasn't dying. It was this boat or nothing.

"You stay behind this rock until I say go. When I say go, you go straight into the water and out to the boat. You don't set foot on the sand."

"Ned. On the sand, one step ahead of me, on my left."

Ned went where he was put without a word, where Dane could see his hands and the trees in one look.

At the other end of the beach, where the road came down, Tess's red fire truck sat at the top. The sky to the south had gone black.

Beth crouched against the granite, wet-eyed, one hand flat on the rock.

"After Monday nobody needs me dead."

Dane crouched so his face was level with hers. "Two years ago in New Bedford, Cole's people burned down the house of a woman who'd testified. After."

"Then I'll keep a bucket by the stove."

It wasn't an answer, and he knew it.

He caught himself working it out with his eyes on the trees: how long the drive was from the Portland office to the Pruett ferry, and whether a man could ask to stay on in Maine, which was no kind of thing a man worked out about a witness. Somebody had pinned her face to a wall in September, and no boat could take her away from that.

He heard the engine before he saw the boat. Then it came out of the gray off the middle of the beach, lifting and dropping in the swell: the Marine Patrol boat, the state police on the water.

Tess's voice came thin across the water from the speaker on its cabin.

"There's a rock just under the water off the point. Keep it on your left. Come in slow. Slower than that."

Dane stepped out past the edge of the rock and lifted one arm, so the boat saw him and the trees didn't see her.

The boat swung toward the rocks' end.

Now it was pointing straight at her, for anyone in those trees to see. He kept his arm up, because it was this boat or none.

It came in as close as the officer at the wheel dared and held there, rising and falling. The second officer leaned out over the side with one arm reaching.

"Go," Dane said.

Beth went into the sea. Dane waded in behind her to his thighs, between her and the trees, and his eyes went to the tree line.

Snow was coming off the spruce in the middle of the tree line. One bough dropped its load, then the next, then the next, coming along the top of the beach toward the rocks' end, keeping pace with the boat.

The snow came off the next bough over, closer.

* * *

"Beth, get down!"

She dropped to her chin in the water behind the swell, and the first shot from the trees went over her head.

It hit the officer leaning out for her hand. He sat down hard on the deck, holding his shoulder.

"Shit," Dane said.

The cold had his legs to the thigh. The flash had come from the trees at this end, right above the rocks, where the line of falling snow had stopped. Dane set his feet on the stones under the water and brought the gun up. The swell lifted him and set him down. He waited for the bottom of it, when the sea went still for half a breath, and fired once at the place the flash had been.

Up in the trees something lurched. A branch shook. Then the snow up there stopped moving.

The next shot from the trees went through the side of the boat, low, near the waterline. The officer at the wheel shouted, "He's put one through the side! We're taking water!"

Another shot came, wild, and smacked into the sea beside Ned.

Ned was in the sea. He had come off the sand past Dane with nothing in his hands, and waded out until the swell came up to his chest, and now he stood at Beth's shoulder, between her and the trees.

Dane had put him on his left so he could watch his hands for a gun. And Ned had walked into water he couldn't swim in, with his hands empty, to stand in front of her. Dane didn't know what that was. There was no time to find out.

The officer at the wheel was looking at him. The hurt man was bleeding onto the deck, the boat had a hole in it, and the swell was too high to get Beth up that side with a man shooting.

Dane waved them off with his free arm. "Take him in. Go."

He didn't try again.

He got Beth by the arm and Ned by the back of the jacket and dragged them both through the swell toward the rocks, and he talked the whole way, low and steady, his eyes on the trees.

"He was right above us, where the snow quit falling. I think I hit him. Nothing's moving up there, so he's down or he's crawling off. Keep low. Rock's in front of you. Up you go."

He shoved Beth up onto the iced granite and in behind the biggest rock, and hauled Ned up after her. Then he went down over her, his body on hers on the ice, and laid his gun on the trees over her shoulder.

Nothing moved up there. He watched a long time, and the snow stayed on the boughs.

Then he got his arms around her. She was shaking, from the cold and from all of it, so hard he could hear her teeth. He held her and kept his chin over her shoulder, where he could see the trees.

He had been afraid for her for the length of that one minute, and now she was shaking under him, alive, and the relief was so big he shut his eyes against her hair before he opened them again on the trees.

Ned sat against the rock with the sea running out of his jacket, his empty hands on his knees.

Out on the water the boat backed off and swung away into the swell, low at the back, with the hurt officer on the deck and the sea getting in at the side. Tess's voice was still coming out of its speaker.

"Beth? Beth."

Then: "Calloway."

It got thinner as the boat went, and smaller, and then there was only the swell, sucking in under the rocks and going quiet.

* * *

"Go home along the shore, Ned," Dane said, to both of them. "I won't leave her to walk you in, the kid's hit and gone to ground, and I can't hand you to Russ while he's on my list."

Ned picked up his lantern, which had blown out on the rock, and went.

Beth got up without a word and went the other way, up the rocks at the far end and into the edge of the trees, away from the beach road. It was her shore. She knew every rock of it. Dane kept his gun out and kept himself between her and the woods. Her wet clothes were freezing stiff at the edges, and they crackled when she climbed.

Just inside the tree line, right above the rocks' end, the snow was red.

There was blood the size of a hand, then drops, then a long scraped line through the snow where one foot hadn't lifted, going off into the woods toward the east side.

"I hit him," Dane said.

He didn't follow it. Following it meant leaving her.

At a big split rock farther along the shore, where the land turned and the beach was out of sight, he got her down out of the wind and took out his phone. No signal. His boss would hear about the beach from the Marine Patrol before she heard it from him.

"Anybody could guess the north beach, Dane Reyes," Beth said. "It's the only sand on this end of the island."

"He knew three o'clock. The wind was down by one. A boat could have come at one, and he sat in those trees and waited for three."

She didn't answer.

"The Marine Patrol had three o'clock too," Dane said, "and my boss had it from me, and she's the one who gave it to them."

And me, his boss had said, on the tower.

"So it's one of your three, or it's one of mine."

Neither of them said the name. Beth turned her head and looked down the shore toward the village, where the lantern had gone. Then she looked away. Dane saw her do it and said nothing.

There would be no boat now until this storm blew through, and Monday was six days off.

"Which one?"
