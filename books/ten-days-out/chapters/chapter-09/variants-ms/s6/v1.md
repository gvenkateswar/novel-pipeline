The generator roared out back of Marr's. Tess held a dish towel of snow to her mouth and squared the cigarette rack pack by pack with her free hand.

Through the front window Lyle Arsenault sat in Russ's truck under the dome light, his hands behind him.

Ned sat on the stool, the clean collar from supper buttoned to the top. Beth stood across the counter with her skinned palms on the cold wood.

"Lip's nothing," Tess said into the towel. "Rack's a disgrace."

"I heard you on nine," Beth said. "Tell me how."

"Wind's gone round northwest," Ned said. "She'll lie quiet now."

"Ned," Beth said.

Ned looked at the coffee urn.

"The boy on my deck asked where my cop was," Beth said. "On sixty-eight I was aboard by myself. That's all Tess had. You had a marshal aboard and Russ on the pier. Nobody else had both."

"Lyle came aboard that first Thursday," Ned said. "He said if you went to Portland, I went with you. He gave me nine thousand in hundreds."

"Envelope," Beth said.

"I cut the tag off the store bag," Ned said. "I put the money in your envelope, over your slip. Then I sealed it again with a red one out of my drawer."

"Yellow," Tess said. "Mine are yellow. Nineteen years, a dollar ten the hundred. I bagged that envelope myself, Calloway. One paycheck, one slip."

The red the teller wrote down was the store bag's, and Beth had given it to the fare bag herself.

"Ashby," Beth said.

"He said a store like hers lives on the boat," Ned said. "So I gave them Ashby."

Tess's hand stopped on the rack. She didn't look at Ned.

"Tonight," Beth said.

"After supper I gave them the whole boat from the back step," Ned said. "Russ on the pier in the light, your marshal aboard."

"Lyle wanted night fuel at half past ten," Tess said. "Cash, double the pump. I came down and lit the float."

"Land side was watched, so they'd come by water, under the floods," Ned said. "You'd see them first. That was your trap, the one you had me carry."

"You gave them my marshal," Beth said. "Edward Haskell. You told them where he'd be sitting. A man came up my stern and put his boot on him in the dark, on the arm they shot Thursday."

Down at the pier Dane sat at the foot of the brow with the boy in Ned's blankets, his sleeve black through the strips. At eleven he had said Haskell to her in the dark lane, and she had closed his fingers round a mug and gone back up the ladder. Before supper she had pushed Tess's name across the chart table to him, and he had held the pencil out to her, rubber end first.

"Then I heard Lyle out front on his own phone," Ned said. "He said send the boy with the gas. I'd never once thought of gas."

Beth waited.

"So I let myself in the back and keyed nine," Ned said. "Tess came through the curtain, and Lyle put her into the counter."

"Every New Year's she tells me ask me sober," Ned said. "I laid that ham in to ask her at Thanksgiving."

"Haskell," Tess said into the towel.

"I asked her tonight at supper instead," he said. "Sober."

Tess took the towel down off her mouth. Her lip was split and swelling. She looked at Ned for a long time. She didn't look at Beth. Then she went through the curtain into her office, and behind it the safe's dial turned.

The bell over the door rang. Russ came in and took his cap off.

Before Russ could open his mouth, Ned held his wrists out together across the counter where he had bought his coffee since Beth was in diapers.

Russ's hand went to his belt. Behind the curtain the dial went round, stopped, and went round again.
