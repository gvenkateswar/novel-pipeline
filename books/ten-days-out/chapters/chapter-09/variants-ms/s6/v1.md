The generator roared out back of Marr's. Tess had a dish towel of snow pressed to her mouth, and with her free hand she was squaring the cigarette rack, pack by pack, faster than anybody had ever bought from it.

"Whitcomb came through that door at a run," Tess said into the towel. "Two tries to put Lyle on the floor. That coat of Lyle's cost more than my float."

Through the front window Russ's truck sat at the dock head with its dome light on, and Lyle Arsenault sat in the passenger seat with his hands behind him.

Ned was on the stool by the counter, the clean collar from supper buttoned to the top. The store's set sat on the shelf behind him, still on nine. Beth stood across the counter from him with her hands on the cold wood. The buoy line had taken the skin off both palms.

"Lip's nothing," Tess said. "Rack's a disgrace."

"I heard you on nine," Beth said. "Tell me how."

"Wind's gone round northwest," Ned said. "She'll lie quiet now."

"Ned," Beth said.

He looked at the coffee urn.

"The boy on my deck asked where my cop was," Beth said. "On sixty-eight I was aboard by myself. That's all Tess had. You had a marshal aboard and Russ on the pier. Nobody else had both."

Ned put his hands on his knees.

"Lyle came aboard that first Thursday, after you'd gone up the hill," he said. "He said if you went to Portland, I went with you. He gave me nine thousand in hundreds."

"Envelope," Beth said.

"I cut the tag off the store bag," Ned said. "I put the money in your envelope, over your slip. Then I sealed the bag again with a red one out of my drawer."

"Yellow," Tess said. The rack stopped under her hand and started again. "Mine are yellow. Nineteen years, yellow, a dollar ten the hundred. I bagged that envelope myself, Calloway. One paycheck, one slip."

The red the teller wrote down had been the store bag's, and Beth had handed it to the fare bag herself, alone in the crew mess, because the fare bag was the boat's.

"Ashby," Beth said.

"He said a store like hers lives on the boat," Ned said. "So I gave them Ashby."

Tess's hand came off the rack. It stayed in the air over the Marlboros, and she kept her eyes on the cartons.

"And the fish house," Beth said.

"The fish house I knew," Ned said. "I've seen you row shingles out to it every fall. That one I kept."

The fish house had a cot in it, and a red case open on the bench, and the nights the two of them had spent there. Ned had kept that and handed over everything else.

"Tonight," Beth said.

"After supper I went down the back step with my key," Ned said. "I gave them the whole boat. Russ on the pier in the light, your marshal aboard."

"Lyle wanted night fuel at half past ten," Tess said, fast. "Cash, double the pump. I came down from the house and lit the float."

"Land side was watched, so they'd come by water, under the floods," Ned said. "You'd see them first. That was your trap, the one you had me carry."

"You gave them my marshal," Beth said. She said it quietly. "Edward Haskell. You told them where he'd be sitting. A man came up my stern and put his boot on him in the dark, on the arm they shot Thursday."

Down at the pier Dane sat at the foot of the brow with the boy wrapped in Ned's blankets and the strips gone black through his sleeve. At eleven he had said Haskell to her in the dark lane, and she had closed his fingers round a mug and gone back up the ladder. Before supper she had written Tess's name for Pike and pushed the book across the chart table to him, and he had pushed the pencil back.

"Then I heard Lyle out front on his own phone," Ned said. "He said send the boy with the gas. I'd never once thought of gas."

Beth waited.

"So I let myself in the back and keyed nine," Ned said. "Tess came through the curtain, and Lyle put her into the counter."

"That's enough, Haskell," Tess said. "Store's closed."

"Every New Year's she tells me ask me sober," Ned said. "I laid that ham in to ask her at Thanksgiving."

He looked at the back of Tess's head.

"I asked her tonight at supper instead," he said. "Sober."

Tess took the towel down off her mouth. Her lip was split and swelling. She looked at Ned for a long time. She didn't look at Beth. Then she went through the curtain into her office, and behind it Beth heard the safe's dial turn.

The bell over the door rang. Russ came in and took his cap off.

Before Russ could open his mouth, Ned held his wrists out together across the counter where he had bought his coffee since Beth was in diapers.

Russ's hand went to his belt. Beth's skinned hands lay on the wood a foot from Ned's. Behind the curtain the dial went round, and stopped, and went round again.
