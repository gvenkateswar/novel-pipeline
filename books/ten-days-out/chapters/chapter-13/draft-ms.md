# Chapter Thirteen: Her Father's Kitchen

Dane Reyes lay in the snow under Beth's kitchen window at first light and watched the kid wash her dishes.

The kid stood at her sink with his left arm bound to his body in one of her dish towels. He washed a mug one-handed and set it upside down to drain. His pistol lay on the drainboard beside the mugs. He stood on his right leg and let the left one hang.

Dane had his face at the corner of the lowest pane and his gun in his right hand. The cold came up through his coat into his chest and his bad right knee. Orange stove light lay across the glass.

The boy had lit that stove before dark last night, with the smoke going up for the whole village to see. The cold had beaten him, and he was weaker this morning than he'd been all week.

Dane wanted to put him through the glass, and he lay still.

One line of tracks came up through the new snow from the cove, a clean print and a long smear, over and over. None went out. Dane's own board still crossed the back door. Beth had told him which board inside the front door talked, and which window a Calloway went out of when she had to run, and where a man could lie so the stove light kept him off the glass, and he was lying there now on the strength of it, and his knee had already filed a complaint.

A quarter mile down the lane, Beth sat in Russ's truck behind the rise.

"You wait in the truck behind the rise," he'd told her at four, in the town office. "Dirt stops a bullet. And if this goes wrong, I want to know where you are."

"I'll go. Don't flatter yourself." She had her arms folded. "If anybody bleeds, I'm coming up."

"Yes."

"You said yes fast."

"I've lost to you every day since the oar. I'm getting quick at it. Give me till Monday and I'll lose before you open your mouth."

"Except about Portland." She looked at him. "Marshal—" She stopped on the word. Then she took the first-aid box out of his hands without touching his fingers.

Yesterday he had told her what he'd asked for. It still stood.

Behind the glass the kid lifted his head.

Dane went still. The boy's eyes came to the window and rested on the snow outside it. If he came to the glass, there was nowhere to be. A gull went over. The kid went back to the mug.

Then Russ's fist hit the front door, hard, from beside it, behind the stone chimney that ran up the front wall. Dane had told him twice to stand there. Inside, the kid's wet hand closed on the pistol.

* * *

The kid was gone from the sink, and the pistol with him.

Dane came up off the snow with his back to the wall under the sill. His right knee had set in the cold, and getting it under him cost a second he didn't have.

Inside the front door a board creaked. *That one talks when you step on it,* Beth had said. The kid was at the door.

Russ's voice came from beside the door, behind the chimney, where it belonged.

"Hey in there! Russ Whitcomb, the constable. You know me off the harbor. You're hurt, son. Come on out and we'll get you warm."

Then the voice moved. Dane heard it come round to the middle, square in front of the door.

"I've got nothing in my hands. See? Nothing. Come on out."

Russ had worked two summers beside that boy on the back of Cole's boat, and he'd spent twenty years talking drunk men out of houses.

Dane couldn't leave the window. *Anybody in that kitchen who has to run goes out over the sink,* Beth had said. *I did, at sixteen.* His first thought was Beth, in the truck behind the rise, out of any line from this house. He held the window.

The shot cracked through the door.

Russ yelled. Dane heard him go down hard on the step.

"My leg! Damn it, he got my leg!"

A review board would call it Dane's plan and Dane's constable. He held the window.

The sash went up over his head, shoved from inside by a shoulder. The pistol came over the sill in the kid's good hand, and the rest of him behind it.

Dane took the gun wrist in his left hand as it cleared the sill. The kid twisted, and the pistol went off beside Dane's head.

Snow and grit hit his face. A high whine filled his left ear, and for a breath it was the only sound anywhere. He bent the wrist back hard against the window frame.

"Let go of it."

The pistol dropped into the snow. Dane pulled, and the kid came the rest of the way out and down, and Dane put him face down with a knee across his back.

The kid spoke into the snow. "My arm."

"I know which one. I'm the one who shot it."

Dane picked the pistol out of the snow and shoved it into his belt at the back. Only then did he holster his own gun. He had no cuffs, because Russ's pair was on Ned at the town office. He brought the kid's good wrist across to the bound one at his chest and pulled a cable tie tight round both, one of the long black ones off Tess's hardware shelf. He put a second round the first.

A boy with Cole's lawyer's number on his phone could sit in a room in Portland and say who sent him. He could only do it alive.

Dane hauled him up. The kid's left heel dragged a line round the corner of the house. Russ lay on his back on the step with both hands clamped on his thigh, and his blood was on the snow. Her front door had a splintered hole in it at chest height.

Dane took the kid past him and in, laid him on his side on the kitchen floor, and ran a third tie from his wrists to the iron leg of the sink.

From the bottom of the lane came Russ's pickup in low gear, sliding on the turn and coming on fast.

* * *

Beth was out of the cab before it stopped rocking, the ferry's first-aid box under her arm, her gloves already gone. She went down on her knees on the step.

The wound was halfway up the thigh and bleeding hard. Russ's blood lay bright on the snow of the step and steamed in the cold.

"Knife."

Dane put Ned's rope knife in her hand. She slit the trouser leg from the knee up, opened the last dressing in the box and packed the hole with two fingers, deep. Russ made a sound Dane had never heard from him.

"Shit," Beth told the wound. "Hold this."

Dane put his hands where hers had been.

"Press. Harder."

He pressed. She ran the strap from the box round the thigh above the hole and knotted it, then took the ferry's wrench out of her coat pocket. She put the handle through the knot and twisted. One turn. Two. Under Dane's hands the bleeding slowed, then stopped. She tied the wrench off against the leg with the tail of the strap.

Dane read his watch out loud. "Twenty to seven."

"Remember it."

The box lay empty on the step. "Don't move," she told Russ.

"Not dying. You hear that? I'm not dying. Did I stand in the door? I stood in the door. Twenty years, and I stood in the door like a summer kid." Russ found Dane. "You said the side."

"I did."

The fire truck came up the lane, loud in the snow. Tess got down with the stretcher and her handheld radio and went straight to Russ. She took the wrench handle out of Beth's fingers and checked it herself. She didn't look at Beth.

She keyed the radio. "Coast Guard, this is Cutter fire. We've got a man shot in the thigh. Strap's on, bleeding's stopped. He's talking."

"Is the shooter secured?"

"A boy. Cable ties. He's going down to the town office with the marshal."

"Nobody's flying a stable patient in this ceiling. Cape Cod comes if he gets worse. If he's not going into shock, in an hour ease the strap slow. Keep the packing in and a tight bandage on. If it bleeds, tighten it again."

Every boat in the bay with a radio had just heard there was a boy in ties at the town office. Dane would have kept that off the air, and Tess wasn't asking him anything today.

He texted his boss with Russ's blood on his thumb. *Have him. Alive. My constable shot in the leg, bleeding stopped.*

"Haskell's cuffed to the pipe in the back room," Tess told him. "On that hip he's going noplace."

"Tess," Russ said. "I stood in the door."

"I can see that, Whitcomb, you old fool. Lift."

They got Russ onto the stretcher and into the bed of his own pickup. Tess picked his shotgun up off the chimney stone and laid it on the seat. She tossed Dane the fire-truck keys without a word and drove the pickup down the lane at a crawl.

Beth was still on her knees with Russ's blood to her wrists. Dane held a hand down to her.

She went past it to his coat, two fistfuls, and came up off the step on them. Her face was a foot from his.

"I want you, Marshal. I haven't stopped."

Dane put his hand over one of her fists on the wool and kept it there for one breath. The hold still stood, and he gave her nothing back but his face.

She let go and went into the kitchen. The prints of her hands stayed on his coat, red on the wool.

* * *

The stove the kid had lit was still going, and under the woodsmoke her kitchen smelled of somebody else's two nights in it, wet wool and canned stew, and on the boards the kid breathed short and wet against the leg of the sink.

Beth stood at the sink wiping Russ's blood off her hands on a dish towel. The mugs the kid had washed hung on her hooks with their handles out.

"You put the mugs away wrong," Beth told the kid.

She took one down, turned it, and hung it back with the handle in. She stood with her hand on it.

Dane laughed once through his nose and stopped it.

His hand came up toward hers on the mug. He put it in his coat pocket, and turned, and checked the kid's ties at the sink leg. They held.

One of her dish towels was round the kid's arm, and another was stuffed in the broken pane by her back door. She folded the third and hung it on the stove rail.

The pantry door stood open where the kid had eaten off her shelves. On the inside of it, in old soft pencil, someone had drawn the ledges at the harbor mouth as small careful shapes, and one line ran inside them that wasn't on any chart.

"What's the line?"

"My father's way home. Nobody's taken it since him."

The kid laughed once from the floor, and it hurt him.

Dane turned. "Who's coming for her?"

The kid turned his face up off the boards. "You think I was what's coming? They've got a boat."

* * *

Dane saw the orange coat when it was still a mile off, coming round the head of the harbor in the snow.

His eye went back along the road to the point. In the lee, where nothing lay in November, a big fishing boat sat quiet on the gray water, and it hadn't been there at eleven. The icing was up in the cloud. A skiff could get into the lee under it, and a helicopter couldn't get down through it.

Beth sat on the town office step with the bent rod from the ferry's steering across her knees, brushing it clean for the co-op's vise. The steel was cold enough to burn, and the wire brush rasped on it. The bend showed where the pin had sheared and the rudder had taken the pier.

"Inside," Dane said.

"No." She kept brushing.

He stood in the front doorway with the kid's rifle. Behind him Russ slept on the couch, the strap off since noon and a tight bandage on, and Tess had gone to her store for wood. The back-room door stood open. Ned slept sitting up against the pipe, and the kid lay tied on the floor beside him. From the doorway, with that door open, Dane could see the road, the step and both prisoners at once. He thought about shutting it and left it.

The coat came on. It belonged to a big, slow man in his sixties with a gray beard, bare-headed in the snow, and the coat was new. He stopped at the foot of the step.

"Heard on the radio you've got a man shot," he said, pleasant. "I've got gauze aboard, if you're short."

They were short.

"We're fine," Beth said.

Dane came down the step and stood in front of her. Beth got up, stepped out beside him and put her hand on his arm.

"Don't stand in front of me," she said, low, to him only.

Her hand stayed on his arm. Dane kept very still so she'd leave it there.

"Name?"

"Hal Mercer. Out of Gloucester. Laying in out of the weather."

Dane held up his phone. "Smile."

The man smiled, and there was a gold tooth in it. He turned the smile on Beth. "Captain. Come June, anyway."

Nobody had asked him. He looked at the rod in her hands.

"That's no good for a crossing, Captain. Weather's no good either. I'd wait for Tuesday."

Beth looked him up and down. "That coat's never been wet."

Mercer's eyes went past Dane's shoulder, through the front room and the open back-room door, to the floor. He smiled at what he saw there. Dane turned his head. On the boards, the kid looked at Mercer, then turned his face to the wall.

Mercer took a sealed packet of gauze out of his pocket and set it on the bottom step in the snow. Then he went back down the harbor road.

Dane didn't leave the step. He watched the orange coat the whole mile round the head of the harbor, the rifle in his hands, until the snow took it. A man who walked a marshal away from his witness wanted him walked away.

Inside, he crouched by the kid. "Is that him?"

The kid kept his face to the wall and said nothing. Dane waited long enough for it to count, then stood.

The man had come to be looked at. He'd come to see her face, and to let the boy see his, so the boy would stay quiet in Portland, and he'd said June, which only Ned and Cole had known since September, when Ned told Cole who'd have the boat after him, so the man had his June from Cole. And someday a review board would read about an open door with a boy behind it and a name beside it, and the name would be Dane's. He'd been very pleased with that door.

He sent the photo and the name to his boss on one bar. Her answer came inside a minute. *Nothing to hold him on. I'll run the name.* A man laying in out of the weather had walked up a public road and offered gauze for a shot man. Nobody could arrest him for that.

They wouldn't come for the town office. A marshal, a rifle and a shotgun behind a lit window in a village of two hundred and forty, and a shot-up town office looked like murder.

The hold was right. It would be right on Monday at two.

At dusk Tess came back up the road with an armload of wood and the shotgun and went in past them without a word to Beth. In the doorway she stopped long enough to tell Dane, "Wood's on the town, Marshal. The ties aren't."

The gauze still lay on the bottom step, white under new snow. Beth hadn't touched it.

She stood up with the rod and looked at him.

"Cancel it, Dane."

"No. He walked a mile in the snow to look at you."

"He told me to wait for Tuesday. You want me in a room in Portland till spring. Which one of you is going to ask me?"

He didn't answer. She went down the step with the rod toward the co-op and its vise, and he took the rifle and followed, a step wide on the water side.

Off the point, through the snow, a single light came on.
