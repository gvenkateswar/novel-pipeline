## Where I got bored

- **"The fishermen's co-op was where the island's lobstermen sold their catch together, and over it sat the net loft, the long room where the men mended traps and nets in winter."** We're running from a man with a gun, and the book stops to define a co-op for me. Chapter 2 did the same thing ("the float, the floating dock below the stone pier", "the car deck, the open deck where the trucks park"). Every time it happens I fall out of the scene.
- **The chase's middle stretch.** "Still here," Dane said. Then "I'm on your left... Keep going." Then "Still with you," he said. Three check-ins inside a page. By the second one I'd stopped feeling the danger and started waiting for the next one. Running across open snow upright should be the scariest moment in the chase. It reads like the dullest, because the gunman is just "a second shot went off farther back, wild" and nothing comes of it.
- **The final scene, from "What's at the Dyer house?" on.** It's a Q&A: Dane asks, Russ answers, Dane asks, Russ answers, with "Dane said" tagged onto every question. My attention went at "The call came from her own phone?" because I already knew from Chapter 3 that the call came from the Dyer house. The scene is confirming things I'd already been told.

## Where I got confused

- **Where the gunman is.** First he's "up on the road." Then "He's above us. Left." Then his steps are "going east, toward Russ's lit house," so he's moving away. Then Dane says "He's back by the boathouse," and a shot comes from "farther back." So did he turn around? Is he following them or not? I couldn't draw a map of it.
- **Why the gunman doesn't just come down.** He's armed, he's tracking them by sound, and he's twenty feet above two people lying under a boathouse, one of them bleeding. He walks off instead. Last chapter he walked right up to Russ's window and looked in before firing, so this one is suddenly timid. If it's deliberate, the page doesn't tell me.
- **"She cleaned the cut with her bare hands."** Cleaned it with what? No water, no alcohol wipe, nothing from the kit. I pictured her rubbing a wound with dirty fingers she'd just warmed at a kerosene heater.
- **Was there a second person?** If the "nephew" phoned from inside the Dyer house at one in the morning, and the shooting started not long after, was the caller the shooter, or are there two men? The last line seems to want me to have that thought, but I'm not sure. If there are two people on the island working against her, that's a big deal, and it's left lying there.
- **"Late."** I noticed Russ dodging the question and I'm suspicious, but I can't tell if I'm meant to be. Hanging on that one word, I'm half sure it's a clue and half sure it's just Russ being Russ.

## Where I stopped believing

- **Dane's jury argument disappears.** Chapter 3 stopped the kiss on a strong, specific reason: Cole's lawyer tells twelve strangers the marshal slept with the only witness, and Cole goes home. Three hours later, with the gunman still out there and Dane still bleeding, he goes ahead, and the reason never crosses his mind. He doesn't win the argument with himself. He doesn't lose it either. It's just gone. I'd have believed him giving in. I don't believe him forgetting.
- **Beth forgets Russ.** At the end of Chapter 3 the first thing she says is "Russ." He's a man she's known since she was six, and he walked out into what she'd already called a trap. In this chapter she doesn't think of him once between the woodpile and his knock on the door. She warms her hands, she patches Dane up, she has sex, she sleeps. For a heroine whose whole reason for staying is loyalty to the island and to Ned, that's cold, and it doesn't feel like her.
- **A marshal in an active shooting falls asleep with his gun on the bench.** He does reach for it at the end, and I'll give the book that. But "His eyes hadn't gone to the bench in a long time... he had stopped looking at it at all" is the book admitting he's being negligent and hoping I find that romantic. I half did, and half thought: this man is bad at his job.
- **Russ: "Took me all night to think of it."** The island constable, who taught Beth to choke a generator, couldn't think of the place her father mended his traps every winter? It's convenient. It keeps the two of them alone until morning.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Every chapter opens on both full names.** "Beth Calloway woke wanting the marshal..." / "Dane Reyes lost the argument..." / "Beth Calloway ran Dane Reyes along the shore..." By the third time it's a template.
- **The "I'm still here" tic.** Ch 2: "I'll keep talking till you tell me to stop." Ch 3: "Just keep breathing where I can hear it," "I'm still here." Ch 4: "Still here," "Still with you," "I'm on your left." It was a nice trait once. Now it's a habit the writing can't stop doing.
- **Wanting gets stated outright, chapter after chapter.** "She wanted them on her." "He wanted her." "He wanted that again." "He wanted her anyway." Now "She wanted him. She had decided back on the ice." I don't need to be told any more. The chapter already shows it, with her palm left flat on his taped arm.
- **The same consent exchange, word for word.** Ch 3: "Can I?" / "Yes." Ch 4: "Can I?" / "Yes." then "Still yes?" / "Yes." It's in the right place, but it's the identical exchange both times, so it reads like a checklist.
- **The same small gestures over and over.** Beth pulls her gloves off with her teeth (Ch 2 and Ch 4) and tears the tape with her teeth. Russ takes his cap off and puts it back on (Ch 2, Ch 3 and twice here). Dane looks at the gun "the way he looked at every door."
- **Neat summing-up lines.** "Neither of them had won, and she could see he knew it." "A dead machine, starting under her hands. Nothing in the world was better." "The sound he made was low and rough, the only thing he'd said all night without words." Each one closes a beat by explaining it to me.
- **The closing line.** Beth: "Anybody on this island could walk in that door." Russ: "Somebody walked in and used her phone." That's the same thought said twice, and the second time it's flatter. The chapter ends on the point I'd already reached, not on a turn.
- **The "Ms. Calloway" / "Dane Reyes" exchange.** Each of them makes a point and the other answers it point for point. Nobody talks like that while bleeding in a freezing loft.

## What I want to know next

- Who phoned from the Dyer house, and is that the same man who shot through Russ's window? If not, who's the second one?
- Where was Russ, and why "Late"? Is the constable compromised, or just ashamed?
- Who told Cole that Beth had seen? Chapter 2 asked this outright, and two chapters later nobody has tried to find out.
- Ned. Chapter 1 told me Ned Haskell had never been afraid in eleven years, then showed him afraid. Since then he's ropes and a bad hip. What does he know?
- Does Dane tell his boss where they spent the night, and does the loft come back at the grand jury?
- Who's the kid in the hoodie? He has a face, a split lip and a dragging foot, and he's "from here," but nobody has said a name or a family.

The questions are real, but they're the same ones I had at the end of Chapter 2. Nothing in this chapter answered one or opened a new one, apart from a maybe-second-person I'm not sure the book meant. What's pulling me forward now is the romance. The suspense is coasting.

## Prediction

Russ was at the Dyer house or somewhere he shouldn't have been. He isn't the leak, but he's covering for someone, probably family or Ned. The hoodie kid is a local boy in debt to Cole, and Beth will know his mother. If the morning call to the boss happens on schedule, the book will stall again.

## Listener pass (reading it aloud)

- The long single-sentence paragraph ("The rope of the net pressed its squares into her shoulders...") works out loud. It's the one passage where the sentences stop being short and clipped, and it sounds like her losing control.
- The final interrogation is hard on the ear: four questions in a row, each tagged "Dane said."
- "Still here" / "Still with you" lands like a refrain read aloud. It sounds like a tic.

## Best moment

> He reached past her to the bench for the trauma kit and opened it in the heater light like a man hoping for the best. Gauze. Tape. A small pair of scissors.
>
> "It's a very disappointing kit," he said.
>
> She laughed out loud, the real thing, and clapped her own hand over her mouth. He looked at the door. She looked at the door. Nothing on the stair.

This is the book at its best. The joke is funny, and it's in character: dry, deadpan, government-issue. Her laugh is the one she's been holding back since the oar, and it also pays off his wish in Chapter 1 to make a witness laugh. Then the fear comes straight back: they both look at the door. Funny, sexy and dangerous all fit in four lines. (The chapter title gives the joke away before you get there, though.) The bare feet on his shins ("Cruel," he said) is a close second.
