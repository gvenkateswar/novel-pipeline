# Chapter Two: The Weather Hold

The back door shook on its latch, iron on iron, and stopped, and shook again.

Beth set the stove lid back. Somebody in her mudroom was trying to come into her kitchen politely, and then less politely, and the old iron wasn't having either.

She lifted the thumb-piece hard and put her shoulder to the boards, and the door went open on him.

Dane stood in the mudroom with snow in his hair and his right hand on his hip under the jacket, the outer door banging in the wind behind him.

"Lift and shove," she said.

He took his hand off his hip, pulled the door shut between them and tried it: a clank, a rattle, the whole door bucking in its frame. Then he lifted and shoved, and it came, loud. The third time it opened without a sound.

"Nobody who's been in this house makes the noise you made first."

She went by him onto the step.

The path fell away under her to the Harbor Road, white already at its edges, and below the road lay the store and its dock and the fuel float. The float was bare. She went along the moorings with her eyes the way she'd go along a chain with her hand, boat by boat, and found the skiff past the last of them: one man at the oars, his face to her hill, pulling long and even for the outer harbor. He rowed like somebody who had learned before he could walk. The squall came down across the harbor in a gray wall with snow inside it and took the outer moorings, and then the skiff, two lengths short of any boat, the man still pulling. She stood in the cold and looked at the place where he had been while the moorings came back out of the snow one at a time, and he didn't.

Dane said nothing. When she turned, he was looking at the same place.

He made the call there on the step, under open sky, with the harbor at his back. She went in. Through the kitchen window she watched his mouth: short, a long listen, short again. The wind took all of it. She put a stick in the stove it didn't need.

He came in with snow on his shoulders and shut the door without a sound.

"The grand jury sits Monday," he said. "Last sitting before Pelletier walks. They want you in front of it."

She looked once at the subpoena under the barometer and left it there.

"Nothing's crossing in this."

"Then tonight it's somewhere with one way in from the land. A door that locks. Somebody awake." He didn't say where. He waited, the way he had waited on the ramp.

She looked down at the slip, and the boat in it going white.

"Boat."

* * *

The store's generator roared out back, flat and steady, and Beth could feel it come up through the step into her heels.

Tess had the door propped on her hip and her glasses pushed up with the pencil through them. She ran the storm net from behind her counter, the island's roll call on the radio every night of weather, so that nobody's house went dark without somebody knowing why, and she was ten minutes from running it.

"Calloway. I've got Whitcomb aboard his boat, the Pinkhams on candles, and nothing from you. Where you sleeping?"

"On the boat."

Tess pulled a ballpoint with no cap from her vest and wrote it on the back of her left hand, *CALLOWAY — BOAT*, under a column of other names climbing toward her knuckles. She blew on the ink. Beth watched her own name dry on somebody else's skin.

Tess dug in the vest for the cap. A fold of bills came up with it, thick, hundreds on the outside, more than the store took in a week in November. It was back in the red pocket before the cap was on the pen.

"Milk's up a quarter till the boat runs." Tess was brighter all at once, and quicker. "Bread's gone. Eggs I've got. Eggs I've got till Easter. Marshal, hold this." She put the door in his hand. "You go on the net as 'plus one.' Free to say. Two dollars to hear."

Beth looked at the pocket. So did Dane. Neither of them said anything.

Out on the visitors' mooring a dragger's deck lights came on all together, white from stem to stern, though nobody moved on her deck. A Gloucester boat, in for the weather. Dane's eyes went to the dragger and stayed there.

"That one?"

"In for the weather."

Tess took her door back and went in with her inked hand held up in front of her to dry, and the bell swung on its spring behind her.

* * *

Beth went forward down the one lane of steel between the two trucks, the fire company's propane truck on her left and the co-op's bait truck on her right, chained there for the two o'clock that never ran. The tide was coming in on top of the storm. Every sea lifted the whole boat and shoved her bow up under the steel bridge the cars drove across from the shore, and the bridge ground on its pilings over her head, a long iron groan and a knock, and again.

She wanted the bow lines doubled before the top of the tide, and she wanted her own hands on them. Dane had asked her twice to come in out of it. When she didn't, he came out into it, and now he was a stride behind her with the snow driving sideways between them.

She had the spare line off its hook and the eye in her fist when something above them screamed.

It came from the seaward end of the bridge, where it hung from its two towers, a shriek she heard in her back teeth, and the cable that had held that corner up came across the bow at head height, whipping, alive.

He hit her from behind and took her down flat under the bulwark, the waist-high wall of steel along the bow, and on the way down her fist went into his collar and closed.

Then it came to her in order. The steel first, wet and so cold against her cheek it burned. Then him, all of him, across her back and shoulders, his knee by her hip, his arm over her head. Then his voice, right at her ear, low, and it didn't stop.

"That was a cable. It went over us and over the bait truck, and I think it's in the water on the far side. I'm on top of you. That's me, I'm heavy, I'm sorry about it. Your face is on the steel, so keep it there. The bridge is still up. I can see the left tower, and it's leaning, and I don't love the lean. I'm going to stay right here until you tell me to move, and you're not telling me anything, so we're staying."

She told him nothing. Under his voice she heard the loose end of the cable rattle along the bow and go quiet; the bridge groaning on its pilings; a sea booming hollow under the bow; and a hum up in the towers, high and thin, from whatever was still holding.

The cable lay still. A sea came under the boat and lifted the two of them and set them down again, and neither of them moved, and her fist stayed in his collar.

Then the bridge came down.

It came down on the bow with a sound she took through the deck into her ribs, and it twisted as it came, and settled with its far end half in the water: the road off the island, folded across her boat, its shore end still bolted to the land. Beth let out all the air she had. Her shoulders came down from where they had been since the night freight.

Then she was up. The boat had to come off the slip before that wreck ground through her. She threw off the slip lines and ran the bow line out and threw it across the gap to the granite pier beside the slip, and it went over the first time. Dane had the tail and was hauling it toward himself.

"Other way. Unless you want to go with it."

He let it run the right way and said nothing.

Up in the wheelhouse Ned had her backing and walking sideways off the slip, the bridge scraping down her bow as she came free, and he laid her in alongside the pier as if she were coming in on a Tuesday in June.

The power went in the same surge. The lights along the Harbor Road went out together, every porch and pole, and the harbor went black from the slip to the church. Out on the visitors' mooring the dragger didn't so much as flicker.

Ned came down the outside ladder with a flashlight. "Water's coming in up there." He went to the hatch forward and down it, into the space right up in the bow under the car deck. Ned couldn't swim. He never had, and nobody on the island knew it but her. She went down after him.

He was standing in water to his shins with the pumps already going and the flashlight on the plating where the bridge had hit, a long crack with the sea coming through it in a sheet. He looked at it the way he looked at weather.

"Well," Ned said. "That's the bridge."

Dane's head and shoulders were in the hatch above them. "Does it do that a lot?"

"Just the once."

"When does it run again?"

"She's not carrying anybody till somebody from the Coast Guard has his hand on that." Ned put his own hand beside the crack. He'd call it in to the Coast Guard tonight, by the book. "Ten days, if the state gets a barge out."

Dane came down two rungs and laid his hand on the hull by Ned's. "At least it's steel."

"Steel doesn't burn," Ned said. "Everything in it does."

He handed Beth the flashlight and bent to clear the pump intake, his beard nearly in the water. "Hold it there, Bethie. No. There." She held it there until he grunted. Then she gave it back and climbed out.

The car deck was dark under her. The floods were out, and the whole island was black from the shore to the spine of it, and then a generator caught somewhere along the Harbor Road and a kitchen window came up yellow, and another farther on, one window at a time along the shore, like somebody walking it with a lamp. Out on her mooring the dragger lay lit from end to end, as if nothing had happened.

* * *

At the stern the car deck ran out low and open to the black water, with nothing across it but a chain. A sea slopped in over the steel around their feet and drained away.

"Anyone with a skiff," Dane said.

"Nobody's coming over that stern in this."

"I believe you." He said it politely, to the chain. "But the whole island heard on the net that she's aboard this boat tonight, which makes her own dark house the one place nobody's looking."

Ned looked at the stern a long time and didn't answer, which on this boat was a yes.

Dane went up the aluminum gangway from the boat's side to the pier, its wheels grinding on the granite each time she surged, and along the pier to look at the road, until he was a shape at the far end, out of earshot.

Ned pulled his gloves off and held them out to her without a word, as he did every winter. She took them and put them on, and they were still warm from him.

"Where'll you be?"

"Home. Don't tell anybody."

"Nobody but me and God." He tapped the handheld on her coat, which lived on nine and always had. "You need me, nine. Like your dad."

She went up the gangway. At the top she turned. Ned had gone a few steps forward along the dark deck toward his pumps, and he turned once too, and nodded to her, and went on.

They went home by the shore with no lamp. She went first; it was her shore. Rockweed slick under the new snow, sleet coming in off the water into her face, and the knee-high ledge below the church that her feet stepped over without asking her, and that she heard him find behind her with his shin.

The back steps were iced. She thumbed the flashlight on to see them, and they came up out of the dark glazed and gray, the rail white, and she was most of the way up before he spoke.

"Light."

She killed it. In the dark she went up the last of them by feel and in through the outer door, and found the latch under her thumb in Ned's wool, and lifted it and shoved, and the door went open without a sound.

* * *

The mudroom's outer door knocked against its latch in every gust. A hollow wooden knock, then nothing, then the knock.

They sat on the kitchen floor with their backs to the chimney, the one wall in the house a bullet wouldn't go through, and her father's blanket over the two of them. No lamp, no stove. Through the kitchen window the harbor lay black, with a generator window here and there along the shore and, out on the visitors' mooring, the dragger in her deck lights.

A man stood on her afterdeck, facing the hill.

Beth knew the set of him. The man in the skiff had sat that way off the fuel float, square to her house. Standing still, from up here, a man showed you no limp at all.

"Him, maybe," she said.

"Maybe."

The door knocked.

"Who's awake?" Dane said, low. "Give me the island."

So she gave it to him in a whisper, house by house along the shore. The Pinkhams were on candles; Earl wouldn't run the generator after supper because of what diesel cost, and because Doris liked candles, and he liked Doris. The two yellow windows past the co-op were the Grindles, and Grace Grindle would have the pair of them married by Tuesday. Ida Dyer had her scanner on, the way she'd had it on since Carter, and she'd have heard Ned call the Coast Guard. And Tess had done the net by now. Beth could hear her do it, and she did it for him, bright and quick, net control on a bad night:

"Calloway, aboard. Marshal, aboard." A small pause, exactly Tess's. "Allegedly."

Beside her he went entirely still, and then she felt him turn his head in the dark to look at her.

Below them the store went dark. A flashlight came out of it and went east along the Harbor Road to Tess's, and a window came up in Tess's house, and after a while it went out.

"Tess," she whispered. "Bed. Late, for her."

The door knocked.

"You left one out," he said.

"Which."

"The house that's talked about me most today."

She waited.

"You. Since this morning."

She put her face in the blanket and laughed, and none of it got out. It shook her against his shoulder, and his knee was against hers under the blanket, and neither of them moved it.

A little after eleven an engine came up the Harbor Road from the village end. She heard it before anything else: a truck in low gear, tires hissing in the slush, and no light on the road at all. A pickup with no lights. It came round the shoulder of the knoll and slowed under the house on the road side, and idled there. She could feel the engine through the floor.

"Still," Dane said, on a breath, and then nothing.

The engine idled. In the mudroom the door knocked. Then the pickup went on up the road and over the shoulder and was gone. For as long as it had sat there he hadn't said one word, and she had heard every second of that.

The boat was crippled and open at the stern, the storm was on, the truck could be sitting up the road with its engine off, and the brick at her back and the gun on his hip were the best she had.

"Portland has to hear that," he said. "And the phone needs sky."

He got out from under the blanket without a sound and went down the hall and out the front, to the road side. From the brick she could see along the hall into the front room, and through its window she saw almost nothing of him: a faint glow under his chin, his face lit from below, his back to the road the truck had taken. The harbor side of the house was behind him.

The mudroom door knocked. Then the next gust came, she heard it go over the roof, and the door didn't.

The one after came hard off the water and went along the clapboards and shook the kitchen window in its frame, and in the mudroom nothing moved. That door had knocked in every northeast blow since she was born. Her father had meant to fix it every fall and never did, because he said it told him when the wind went round. It went quiet for one thing. Somebody was inside the outer door, holding it, standing in her mudroom in the dark with a hand on the boards, waiting for the house to settle.

She listened. Her father's clock on the shelf over the sink, going on. The wind on the clapboards. Snow ticking at the window. From the mudroom, nothing, and nothing, and nothing.

She left the blanket and went down the hall without a light, along the wall where the boards didn't talk, and opened the front door a crack and got him by the sleeve and pulled him in. At his ear: "Don't talk. Door's gone quiet."

He came back with her to the brick with his hand already on the gun, and then he talked, so low it came to her through his shoulder, and he didn't stop.

"I'm on your right. The brick's at your back, and it's still warm, which is good of it. Your father's clock is going to keep going, and that's fine, let it. The mudroom's on my side of you. Anything that comes through that door comes to me first." His hand found her knee under the blanket, once, and went back to the gun. "He comes to us. Not the other way."

The green hands of her father's clock stood at twenty to twelve.

The latch lifted. It stuck. It lifted again.

Nobody who had been in this house made that sound.

Dane was at the door before the second lift had finished, against the wall on the hinge side, the gun down along his leg. "Evening," he said through the boards, in an ordinary voice, the first ordinary voice in the house all night.

The outer door banged. Feet went down the back steps, fast and uneven, and away down the path toward the harbor, and the wind took the sound.

He waited the length of a long breath. Then he lifted and shoved, and the door opened without a sound, and past his shoulder she saw the outer door swinging and the back steps white under the sky, with dark scuffs going down them in the sleet.

He shut both doors and came back to the brick. He didn't sit.

"Who knew you were here?"

"You."

She saw it again: the small glow under his chin out in the front yard, a few minutes before the door went quiet.
