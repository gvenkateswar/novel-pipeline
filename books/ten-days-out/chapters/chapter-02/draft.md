# Chapter Two: The Weather Hold

The back door shook on its latch, iron on iron, and stopped, and shook again.

Somebody in her mudroom was trying to come into her kitchen politely, and then less politely, and the old iron wasn't having either. Beth lifted the thumb-piece hard and put her shoulder to the boards, and the door went open on him.

Dane stood in the mudroom with snow in his hair and his right hand on his hip under the jacket.

"Lift and shove," she said.

He shut the door between them and tried it: a clank, a rattle, the whole door bucking. Then he lifted and shoved, and it came, loud. The third time it opened without a sound.

"Nobody who's been in this house makes the noise you made first."

She went by him onto the step. Below the road the fuel float was bare. She went along the moorings with her eyes the way she'd go along a chain with her hand, and found the skiff past the last of them: one man at the oars, his face to her hill, pulling long and even for the outer harbor. He rowed like somebody who had learned before he could walk. A squall came down across the water in a gray wall with snow inside it and took the outer moorings, and then the skiff, two lengths short of any boat. She stood in the cold and looked at the place where he had been while the moorings came back out of the snow one at a time, and he didn't.

Dane said nothing. He was looking at the same place.

He made the call there under open sky. Through the kitchen window she watched his mouth and couldn't hear a word of it, and she put a stick in the stove it didn't need.

He came in with snow on his shoulders.

"The grand jury sits Monday. Last sitting before Pelletier walks. They want you in front of it."

She looked once at the subpoena under the barometer.

"Nothing's crossing in this."

"Then tonight it's somewhere with one way in from the land. A door that locks. Somebody awake." He didn't say where. He waited, the way he had waited on the ramp.

She looked down at the boat in the slip, going white.

"Boat."

* * *

The store's generator roared out back, and Beth felt it come up through the step into her heels.

Tess had the door propped on her hip and the pencil through her glasses. She ran the storm net, the island's roll call on the radio every night of weather, so nobody's house went dark without somebody knowing why.

"Calloway. I've got Whitcomb aboard his boat, the Pinkhams on candles, and nothing from you. Where you sleeping?"

"On the boat."

Tess wrote it in ballpoint on the back of her left hand, *CALLOWAY — BOAT*, under a column of other names, and blew on the ink. Beth watched her own name dry on somebody else's skin.

She dug in her vest for the pen cap. A fold of bills came up with it, hundreds on the outside, more than the store took in a week in November. It was back in the red pocket before the cap was on.

"Milk's up a quarter till the boat runs." Tess was brighter all at once. "Bread's gone. Eggs I've got till Easter. Marshal, you go on the net as 'plus one.' Free to say. Two dollars to hear."

Beth looked at the pocket. So did Dane. Neither of them said anything.

Out on the visitors' mooring a dragger's deck lights came on, stem to stern, with nobody on her deck. A Gloucester boat. Dane's eyes went to it and stayed.

"That one?"

"In for the weather."

Tess went in with her inked hand held up to dry, and the bell swung on its spring behind her.

* * *

Beth went forward down the one lane of steel between the two trucks, the fire company's propane truck and the co-op's bait truck, chained there for the two o'clock that never ran. The tide was coming in on top of the storm. Every sea lifted the boat and shoved her bow up under the steel bridge the cars drove across from the shore, and the bridge ground on its pilings over her head, a long iron groan and a knock, and again.

She wanted the bow lines doubled before the top of the tide, by her own hands. Dane had asked her twice to come in. When she didn't, he came out, and now he was a stride behind her in the driving snow.

She had the spare line in her fist when something above them screamed.

It came from the seaward end of the bridge, where it hung from its towers, a shriek she heard in her back teeth, and a cable came across the bow at head height, whipping, alive.

He hit her from behind and took her down flat under the bulwark, the waist-high wall of steel along the bow, and on the way down her fist went into his collar and closed.

Then it came to her in order. The steel, so cold against her cheek it burned. Then him, all of him, across her back, his knee by her hip, his arm over her head. Then his voice at her ear, low, and it didn't stop.

"That was a cable. It went over us and over the bait truck, and I think it's in the water. I'm on top of you. That's me, I'm heavy, I'm sorry about it. Keep your face on the steel. The bridge is still up. The left tower's leaning, and I don't love the lean. I'm staying right here until you tell me to move, and you're not telling me anything, so we're staying."

She told him nothing. Under his voice she heard the cable's end rattle along the bow and go quiet; the bridge groaning; a sea booming hollow under them; a hum up in the towers, high and thin, from whatever was still holding.

The cable lay still. A sea lifted the two of them and set them down again, and neither of them moved, and her fist stayed in his collar.

Then the bridge came down.

It came down on the bow with a sound she took through the deck into her ribs, and twisted, and settled with its far end in the water: the road off the island, folded across her boat. Beth let out all the air she had. Her shoulders came down from where they had been since the night freight.

Then she was up. The boat had to come off before that wreck ground through her. She threw the bow line across the gap to the granite pier beside the slip, and it went over the first time. Dane had the tail and was hauling it toward himself.

"Other way. Unless you want to go with it."

He let it run and said nothing.

Ned backed her off the slip with the bridge scraping down her bow, and laid her in alongside the pier as if it were a Tuesday in June.

The power went in the same surge. The lights along the Harbor Road went out together, and the harbor went black. Out on the visitors' mooring the dragger didn't so much as flicker.

Ned came down the outside ladder with a flashlight. "Water's coming in up there." He went down the hatch forward, into the space right up in the bow under the car deck. Ned couldn't swim, and nobody on the island knew it but her. She went down after him.

He stood in water to his shins with the pumps going and the flashlight on a long crack in the plating, the sea coming through it in a sheet. He looked at it the way he looked at weather.

"Well," Ned said. "That's the bridge."

Dane's head and shoulders were in the hatch above them. "Does it do that a lot?"

"Just the once."

"When does it run again?"

"She's not carrying anybody till somebody from the Coast Guard has his hand on that." Ned put his own hand beside the crack. He'd call it in to the Coast Guard tonight, by the book. "Ten days, if the state gets a barge out."

Dane came down two rungs and laid his hand on the hull. "At least it's steel."

"Steel doesn't burn," Ned said. "Everything in it does."

He handed Beth the flashlight and bent to the pump intake, his beard nearly in the water. "Hold it there, Bethie. No. There." She held it until he grunted. Then she climbed out.

The car deck was dark. The whole island was black from the shore to the spine of it, and then a generator caught somewhere along the Harbor Road and a kitchen window came up yellow, and another farther on, one window at a time, like somebody walking the shore with a lamp. Out on her mooring the dragger lay lit from end to end, as if nothing had happened.

* * *

At the stern the deck ran out low and open to the black water, with nothing across it but a chain. A sea slopped in around their feet and drained away.

"Anyone with a skiff," Dane said.

"Nobody's coming over that stern in this."

"I believe you." He said it politely, to the chain. "But the whole island heard on the net that she's aboard tonight, which makes her own dark house the one place nobody's looking."

Ned looked at the stern a long time and didn't answer, which on this boat was a yes.

Dane went up the aluminum gangway to the pier, its wheels grinding on the granite each time the boat surged, and on to the road end, out of earshot.

Ned pulled off his gloves and held them out to her without a word, as he did every winter. She put them on. They were warm from him.

"Where'll you be?"

"Home. Don't tell anybody."

"Nobody but me and God." He tapped the handheld on her coat, which lived on nine. "You need me, nine. Like your dad."

At the top of the gangway she turned. Ned had gone a few steps forward toward his pumps, and he turned once too, and nodded to her, and went on.

They went home by the shore with no lamp, her first; it was her shore. Sleet off the water in her face, rockweed under the snow, and the knee-high ledge below the church that her feet stepped over without asking her, and that she heard him find behind her with his shin.

The back steps were iced. She thumbed the flashlight on, and they came up out of the dark glazed and gray, and she was most of the way up before he spoke.

"Light."

She killed it. In the dark she went in through the outer door, found the latch under her thumb in Ned's wool, lifted it and shoved, and the door went open without a sound.

* * *

The mudroom's outer door knocked against its latch in every gust. A hollow knock, then nothing, then the knock.

They sat on the kitchen floor with their backs to the chimney, the one wall in the house a bullet wouldn't go through, under her father's blanket. No lamp, no stove. Out on the harbor the dragger lay in her deck lights, and a man stood on her afterdeck, facing the hill.

Beth knew the set of him. The man in the skiff had sat that way, square to her house. Standing still, from up here, a man showed you no limp at all.

"Him, maybe," she said.

"Maybe."

The door knocked.

"Who's awake?" Dane said, low. "Give me the island."

So she gave it to him in a whisper, house by house. The Pinkhams were on candles, because Earl begrudged the diesel and Doris liked candles and he liked Doris. The two yellow windows past the co-op were the Grindles, and Grace Grindle would have the pair of them married by Tuesday. Ida Dyer had her scanner on, the way she'd had it on since Carter, and she'd have heard Ned call the Coast Guard. And Tess had done the net by now, and Beth did it for him, bright and quick, net control on a bad night:

"Calloway, aboard. Marshal, aboard." A small pause, exactly Tess's. "Allegedly."

She felt him turn his head in the dark to look at her.

Below them the store went dark. A flashlight went east along the Harbor Road to Tess's, and a window came up in Tess's house, and after a while went out.

"Tess," she whispered. "Bed. Late, for her."

The door knocked.

"You left one out," he said.

"Which."

"The house that's talked about me most today."

She waited.

"You. Since this morning."

She put her face in the blanket and laughed without a sound. It shook her against his shoulder, and his knee was against hers, and neither of them moved it.

A little after eleven an engine came up the Harbor Road from the village end, a truck in low gear with no light on the road at all. A pickup with no lights. It slowed under the house on the road side and idled there. She could feel it through the floor.

"Still," Dane said, on a breath, and then nothing.

The door knocked. The pickup went on up the road and was gone. For as long as it had sat there he hadn't said one word, and she had heard every second of that.

The boat was crippled and open at the stern, the storm was on, the truck could be waiting up the road, and the brick at her back and the gun on his hip were the best she had.

"Portland has to hear that," he said. "And the phone needs sky."

He went out the front, to the road side. Through the front-room window she could see almost nothing of him: a faint glow under his chin, his back to the road the truck had taken, the harbor side of the house behind him.

The mudroom door knocked. The next gust went over the roof, and the door didn't.

The one after came hard off the water and shook the kitchen window in its frame, and in the mudroom nothing moved. That door had knocked in every northeast blow since she was born. Her father never fixed it, because he said it told him when the wind went round. It went quiet for one thing. Somebody was inside the outer door, holding it, standing in her mudroom in the dark, waiting for the house to settle.

She listened. Her father's clock over the sink, going on. The wind on the clapboards. Snow ticking at the window. From the mudroom, nothing, and nothing, and nothing.

She went down the hall without a light, along the wall where the boards didn't talk, and opened the front door a crack and got him by the sleeve. At his ear: "Don't talk. Door's gone quiet."

Back at the brick his hand was on the gun, and then he talked, so low it came to her through his shoulder, and he didn't stop.

"I'm on your right. The brick's at your back, and it's still warm, which is good of it. Your father's clock is going to keep going, and that's fine, let it. Anything that comes through that door comes to me first." His hand found her knee under the blanket, once, and went back to the gun. "He comes to us. Not the other way."

The green hands of her father's clock stood at twenty to twelve.

The latch lifted. It stuck. It lifted again.

Nobody who had been in this house made that sound.

Dane was at the door before the second lift had finished, against the wall on the hinge side. "Evening," he said through the boards, in an ordinary voice, the first in the house all night.

The outer door banged. Feet went down the back steps, fast and uneven, and away toward the harbor.

He waited the length of a long breath. Then he lifted and shoved, and past his shoulder she saw the outer door swinging and the back steps white under the sky, with dark scuffs going down them in the sleet.

He shut both doors and came back to the brick. He didn't sit.

"Who knew you were here?"

"You."

She saw it again: the small glow under his chin out in the front yard, a few minutes before the door went quiet.
