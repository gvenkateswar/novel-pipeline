# Chapter Seven: Cold Water

Dane Reyes did exactly what she did, right up until the wave took him off the rock.

She dropped flat on a big rounded rock, still holding his hand, jammed her free arm into a crack on the high side, and put her face down on the ice.

"Down. Arm in the crack. Face in."

He shoved the gun into its holster and went flat beside her. He pushed his right arm into a crack up to the elbow. Inside, it was glazed with ice. He put his face on the rock.

Out in the dark the sea pulled back off the shore with a long dragging roar, and then there was no sound at all.

Then it came.

It came over them black and went white on top of them, all of the sea at once. It shoved him flat into the rock. He held. Her hand held his.

Then it went back out, and the going out was worse. It took his legs and his coat and pulled, slow and enormous, the whole bay leaning on him. His arm slid in the iced crack. His elbow came out, then his wrist, and the rock went out from under his chest.

Her hand was still in his. The sea had him now, and it would take whatever he was holding. He opened his hand so it wouldn't take her too.

Then the cold.

For the first second it was only his chest, locking shut. Then he gasped, and the gasp was sea, salt down his throat. His breath went out of him and wouldn't come back. He tried to say her name, and nothing came. He had talked his way through every bad night of nine years, and now he had no voice at all.

His coat filled. His boots filled. A rock he never saw hit his hip under the water, and he lost which way was up.

Then two hands closed on his coat collar.

Beth had come down off her rock onto the next one, into the sea to her thighs, and she had his collar in both fists. The next swell lifted him. She waited for it, let the sea hand him to her, and hauled with her shoulders and her back, the way she hauled a ferry rope. He came up the rock on his stomach, coughing sea.

She dragged him along the waterline into the black under the overhang of the bank, behind a big rock at the edge of the water. He was shaking so hard his teeth knocked, loud as dice in a cup.

She put her hand over his mouth, gently, and held his jaw still.

At the top of the bank the flashlight found the white water where he had gone in. It stopped there.

It stayed.

Dane counted, because he counted everything. He got to forty, and the light was still on the water. The sea came up white through it and went back, and the light waited for something to come up with it. Sixty. The cold was in his hands now, and in his jaw under her palm. Four people had known about that house, and it had burned, and the one place nobody had known about, nobody had come, and he couldn't stop hearing it. Ninety. He lost count somewhere after that.

His hands had stopped closing. He told the right one to make a fist, and it lay on the rock like somebody else's.

The light lifted and swept once along the rocks toward them. It stopped a body-length short and went back to the water.

Then it went away up the bank toward the fire, and it didn't come back.

Beth took her hand off his mouth. Her lips were at his ear, and it was the first thing she had said since the wave.

"He thinks we drowned."

Down here it was dark all the way to the water.

* * *

Beth had his arm over her shoulders and most of his weight, and she was talking.

"Left foot. Flat one. Now up."

He put his left foot where she said. His right knee had gone stiff, and every step on it came late.

"Round the point. Then the lighthouse."

She was the one who went quiet when it was bad. He couldn't talk, so she did, in orders, to feel his weight answer.

He went down on one knee. "Dane." She had him up. "Dane. Feet."

"I tried one of those apps once," she said, flat. "It found me nine men inside fifty miles, and two of them were my cousins."

He laughed with no sound at all. She felt it in his ribs and kept walking.

"Every kid on this island has been in that window."

The keeper's house stood beside the tower, where the lighthouse keeper used to live, a summer rental now, shut for the winter. She lifted the window on its frame, and it came up. She got him over the sill and followed.

Inside it was black, and cold plaster, and mothballs.

"No fire, no light," she said. "Smoke shows white from the whole north end."

Her hand found a switch on the wall and clicked it. Nothing.

She stripped him. His fingers couldn't do buttons. Hers were raw from the ice, and they just could. Coat, sweater, shirt. She dragged his boots off and tipped them, and the sea ran out onto a stranger's floor.

She reached for the gun on his hip. He put his hand over hers and held it there. So she set it on the floor beside the bed and put his hand on it.

The tape on his arm was gone, and the cut under it was closed, scrubbed white by the salt.

"This is medical," she said, flat, and he heard her wet jeans hit the floor, and her shirt after them. She was soaked to the waist, and her teeth were going too.

He had the answer ready. He had no voice to put it in.

Then the blankets. She made trips through the dark house, and they landed on the bed in loads until it was a hill. She got in and pulled him chest to chest.

The first night, in the snow, he had rubbed her arms. She left his alone. She folded them in against his own body and put her whole front against his.

"You warm the middle first," she said.

Then, lower: "Come here. You're shaking. Let me be the warm one for once."

He shook. It came in long rounds from the jaw down, and let go, and came again smaller. Her heart went against his chest, fast and then slower. His hands came back, burning in every finger, which meant they were still his.

He wanted her. Every part of him that could want anything was busy keeping him alive, under a hill of strangers' blankets, too cold to do one thing about it, and it was the most naked he had ever been in front of anyone.

His voice came back rough.

"I fell asleep on watch, and you nearly burned for it."

"You'd been up three nights. I heard you go under, and I let you."

"I felt myself going. I let you let me."

"You let go of my hand."

"It was going to take you with me."

"I was holding the rock." An inch from his face, in the dark. "You don't get to decide what I can hold."

"Fair."

The shaking came once more, small, and went.

He'd had the line ready for twenty minutes.

"Best medical I've ever had."

She laughed out loud, right against his throat.

Two, he thought.

* * *

Dane Reyes woke with dry feet.

He had slept, and she had let him again. She had found wool socks in a summer person's drawer and put them on him in the dark, thick and dry. He moved his toes in them. They were his own feet again.

"I've been shot at, burned out of a house and drowned since lunch," he said, "and somebody's wool socks are the best thing that's happened to me all day, which tells you about my day."

"Nothing out there but wind," she said. "I've been listening."

He believed her, and noticed that he did.

He took the gun apart on the blanket by feel, dried each piece on a pillowcase and put it back together blind, the way the Army taught him. His phone lit up with no signal, two nights now with no word to his boss, and nothing to be done about it from here.

"He watched the water a long time," he said, "and then he went to tell somebody it was done."

"So I'm dead."

"As of tonight. Nobody on this island knows different. So if anybody comes for you now, somebody had to tell them you're alive."

"Then he can take my picture down."

Somebody had pinned her face to that wall in September, two months before she'd seen anything, and he still couldn't make it add up.

"You said off that hill you'd kiss me for an hour."

"I did."

"We've been off it a long time."

She lay down facing him.

"I want you. I'm saying it out loud so you can't pretend I didn't."

The woman who wouldn't let Tess forgive her a penny, who carried her own bag down her steps before he could offer, who said she didn't owe anybody with her hand on his taped arm, had just asked him for something, out loud, and he knew it was the first time she had asked anyone for anything.

His mouth went quiet.

"Beth."

She got up out of the blankets into the cold. He heard her feet on the boards, a zipper on the wet coat over the chair, her feet coming back fast. She slid in against him, cold all down one side, and put something small in his hand. Foil.

"The box is soup," she said. "These are fine."

He laughed into her hair. The sea had taken his tape, his voice and very nearly him, and it had left her these.

She was almost his height, and in the dark her mouth was level with his without either of them looking for it. He couldn't see her at all.

"I can't see you," he said.

"Then I'll tell you."

She kissed him, slow, and he started on the hour he owed her.

"Slower," she said, and he went slower. Her lips were dry with salt. So was her throat when he got there, and her shoulder, faintly bitter, the whole sea still on her skin.

She touched him with the backs of her fingers, along his jaw, down his chest, then the inside of her forearm across his ribs. He knew why. Her palms were skinned raw from the ice, and she was keeping them off him and touching him everywhere she could reach anyway.

Her knuckles stopped on two sore places on his collarbone. "That's me," she said.

He put his hand flat on her back. Under it was the long hard muscle that had hauled him out of the sea, and he felt it move when she breathed.

He wanted her. In nine years there had always been something to watch, a window, a door, a road, and here there was no window and no door he could see, and the only thing he wanted to watch was her, and he couldn't see her. So he listened.

His mouth found her breast, and she pressed into it.

"That," she said. The back of her hand held his head there. "That."

He took her nipple into his mouth, and she made a low sound in her throat.

Out of habit he reached for the twelve people from Maine and how this would sound to them. Nobody was there. Nobody could see this. As of tonight nobody even knew they were alive.

He slid his hand down between her thighs, and she was wet, and she said, "Come here," against his mouth, impatient.

He moved over her, and his right knee cracked, loud in the dark.

"You creak like an old boat," she said.

It was the nicest thing anyone had ever said to him, and he didn't tell her why.

The foil took him two tries. Then his hand waited on her hip.

"Tell me," he said.

"Yes." Her heel hooked the back of his bad knee. "Now, Dane."

He went into her slowly, all the way, and they both stopped breathing and started again together.

Neither of them moved for a while. Her breath shook against his mouth. Hours ago she had been as cold as he was, and now her skin was warm everywhere he touched it. Then she rolled her hips up under him, once, slow, and he forgot every plan he had ever made.

She held his face between her wrists, palms turned out. She told him what she wanted in single words, and he did each one.

"Harder," she said, and later, "Yes." Her thighs tightened on his hips.

Once, he thought of the three people on this island she had said would die for her, and that one of them might be selling her. Then she said his name, and he put it down.

He had stopped counting. He didn't know when. Somewhere in the last few minutes he had stopped, and he only noticed because there was nothing left in his head but her voice.

"Like that," she said. "Dane. Like that."

She came with her mouth open against his jaw and her wrists locked behind his neck, and he felt it all through her, around him, and he held still inside her until she was done.

"Now you."

He came with his face in her salt hair, saying her name and nothing else.

She put her head on his shoulder. The wind went around the tower next door.

Monday was Portland. After that she would go home to her ferry and her father's house, and he would go back to Boston. She would never leave this island. He had known that since the oar.

"I'm going to get you off this island alive," he said, "and then I'm going to lose you, aren't I?"

She didn't answer. Under the blankets she found his hand and held it hard, the way she had held it on the rock before the sea, and she didn't let go.
