# Chapter Seven: His Handwriting

"Same set runs the road and the float," Beth said.

She had her boot on the head of the ramp, and she took it back. The co-op's generator in the shed behind the landing fed the harbor road lights and the float lights off the one panel. The road was lit all the way to the store. Below, the float lay black on the water. Somebody had been in the box.

"Ned set that timer. Ned and the town ring had the only keys to the box."

Dane's head came round to her.

"Turn round."

He turned them. His right hand went flat between her shoulders and brought her about on the planks. He didn't argue. He didn't ask what she'd seen.

Off the landing the *Margaret Ann*'s engine knocked, low and slow, and her exhaust lay flat along the harbor. Beth could taste it over the herring from the shed at their backs. Under the knock, somewhere beneath the float, where nothing ought to lie at this tide, an oar touched aluminum. Once.

She heard it first. His hand on her back hadn't changed.

The flash came from out past the float, small and yellow. The road lights behind them had made the two of them one shape on the ramp head, and the crack came flat across the water after it. Dane's left arm jumped out from his side. His right hand had already shoved her round the corner of the bait shed into the dark of the lane.

"Down," Beth said. "Lane."

Then she had nothing more to say. The lane was frozen ruts, the shed wall, her own boots. She listened past all of it for the oar.

Dane talked. He came round the corner behind her talking, low, close at her back, in no hurry at all.

"I'm behind you. I'm right behind you. You've got the wall on your right, keep it there. That was me, left arm, outside, up high. It's wet. It moves. Fingers move. That's the first time all week I've done what you said the first time you said it, and this is what I get for—"

He didn't finish it. They were past the shed's back door, down the lane and off the end of it onto the shore under the bank.

The ebb had drawn off more. The ledges ran wet and black below the bank. At the last one the water came in over her boot tops and filled them, cold as a slap through the wool. She went through it. He came behind her, still talking, and she could tell from his breath where his feet were.

Under the bank, out of sight of the landing, she stopped and stripped her right glove. She found his arm in the dark by his voice. The jacket was torn high on the outside, and under it the wet was warm, a furrow the length of her finger in the meat of the arm. Shallow. She ran her thumb down to the elbow and found nothing else.

"Squeeze."

His left hand closed on hers. Slow. Not hard. It closed.

The gray was coming up in the east behind the ledges. Out past them a light came in on the swell, high and white, making for the float. The lifeboat. It came on. It slowed. Its light swung, steadied, swung away.

It turned.

Dane got the phone out of his jacket with his right hand. He tried to bring the left up to hold it to the sky, and the arm stopped at his chest. He held the phone out to her.

She held it up at arm's length into the gray. It buzzed in her fist, and she brought it down and read it herself.

*Pike: Lifeboat turned back. Not again today. First light Saturday. LifeFlight for the arm?*

"Saturday," she said.

"Fuck." He said it once, flat, at the screen. He hadn't sworn at the arm. His eyes came off the phone and onto her, and stayed.

"Ballfield," she said.

"Is open ground with that deck looking straight up the harbor at it, and the front's in by mid-morning. A graze doesn't buy that. No." He reached up with his good hand while she held the phone and thumbed one word into it, and she put it back up to the sky to send.

Out past the ledges the lifeboat's light went away south with the inspector aboard, smaller at every sea, while she stood under the bank in the gray with her arm still raised, holding the phone up to the sky for a man who couldn't lift his.

* * *

The shears from his own kit had taken the sleeve off at the shoulder. Dane sat on the bench with the arm across his knee. His jacket hung wet on the nail, and the cold came off it into the room against the stove's heat.

Beth reached the red Pelican case down off the shelf over the bench and thumbed its catches. Her father's boat kit lay inside as he had packed it: gauze in its paper, a brown bottle, white tape, a card of butterfly strips gone yellow at the edges and still sealed. Old, and nothing missing.

She washed the furrow from the kettle. It was open like a split in a glove. She pinched the edges together between her left thumb and finger and laid the first strip across it at the middle. The next went beside it. She worked out toward each end, one strip at a time, across the cut, as you'd close a deckhand's hand on the boat with a load still to go.

To reach the far end she had to lean across him. His breath went over her wrist. His skin was cold where her fingers held it, and under the cold, along the inside of the arm, he was warm. The blood on her fingers smelled of iron in the stove's heat.

"By the time I tell this in Lowell," Dane said, "it's a much bigger hole."

"Tess'll have it through your heart by noon."

He laughed. It came out of him short and real, and she was near enough to it herself. Then the name sat in the room between them. The ring hung on its nail by Tess's safe, and Tess wrote down whatever came over her counter.

Neither of them went on with it.

Then nothing. The stove. The first snow going sideways past the edge of the blanket over the window, onto the cove.

He let her lay the last strip. Then he spoke, slow, and very polite.

"Ms. Calloway. You asked Haskell about the float. Below decks, where I couldn't hear it. Tell me what you said, exactly, the way you'd tell a man who's going to write it down."

She pressed the last strip flat with her thumb.

"I said, 'Is the float workable on the ebb?' He said, 'Which tide?' I said, 'Tomorrow's first.'"

She gave it to him in order with nothing in front of it. Then she went on, and heard herself going quicker.

"Ned sets it to run till light. Always has. Somebody with the other key turned it off. Other key hangs by a safe, behind a curtain that doesn't shut. Anybody at that counter can reach it."

He didn't argue the key. He reached across himself with his right hand to the jacket on the nail, found the notebook in its inside pocket and the pencil in its spine, and held the book flat on his knee under his good hand.

"I'm going to write that down. Then I'm going to sleep for one hour, because I can't hold the gun above my shoulder, and you're going to put the bar across that door."

He wrote. She stood over him and saw her own words go down in pencil. *Which tide.* The curtain. The letter B. The paper took them off her as fast as she had said them.

He shut the book, put it back in the jacket and lay down on the cot on his right side. The gun went on the bench by his right hand.

Beth lifted the oak bar off the wall and dropped it into its irons across the door. Both her hands stayed on the oak till the iron quit ringing.

* * *

The snow came harder across the cove. Beth lifted a corner of the gray blanket off the small window and hooked it over a nail, and the snow-light came in gray-white and lay across the bench.

Dane slept on his right side with his hand on the gun. The strips held. His breath had gone long and stayed there. On the wire along the stovepipe their wool steamed itself dry, and the room smelled of it.

She took the notebook out of his jacket on the nail. She wanted to see what he had made of her an hour back.

The paper was the kind that doesn't mind water. The jacket had been wet through, and the pencil hadn't run a line. It smelled of pencil, of cedar and lead.

Her answer about Ned was the first page she came to. He had it in short lines: the float, the tide, Ned's question back to her, the key by the safe, the curtain, the time in the margin. One page back was her plan from Wednesday night. Every ledge of it. Before six. The lane behind the bait shed. The ramp head. He had got all of it, and got it right.

She turned back further, past the pages she had let him write, to the ones she hadn't known were there.

*every place we've slept, and B's family property*

The fish house was written down. She read it as a list of houses. Then she read it the other way. Both held.

Her own kitchen floor, under the blanket, with her back to the chimney. The Ashby kitchen. This cot, with him asleep on it. A pencil line with a date over it, in a book that went to Portland.

*$9,000 cash in subject's envelope, over her slip, credited and flagged*

Her thumb stopped on the page.

Nine thousand dollars. She had never heard of it. She was hearing of it now, from a sheet of paper, in her own fish house. Her slip went into the envelope at the store, the flap tucked, never sealed. Three people saw that slip. Tess, who bagged it. The teller in Pruett, who counted it. Herself. She saw Tess's hands, blue to the wrist, folding the flap under. She saw the teller's, quick, on the far side of the window. She saw her own hands pass the pouch down to the courier at Pruett on Friday morning. Dane had stood on the ramp in his wrong boots, watching the pouch go and nothing else.

Nine thousand dollars she hadn't earned, credited to her name. She had owed nobody a dollar in eight years. She had seen to it. Somebody had put money in her envelope for a man with a notebook to find. He had found it. He had written it down and carried it into her bed. Her face went hot in the cold room. She kept her thumb where it was.

*D5 0150 report. No indication.*

Tuesday. Ten to two. The top of the tide. She had been down under the window with the skiff's light climbing the wall over her head, and he had been up on the ledge in the dark, telling Portland. The gravel bar had been over a man's head. Walked the bar. The flashlight at the Ashby end had been somebody else's. He had knocked after, soaked at the ankles, and she had lifted the oak bar for him.

Nothing to report on her. That was what he had sent at ten to two.

*D6 am. Q: CU written rpt. Pouch: two bags. Seal noted: red.*

Wednesday morning was the rock and the first sun on her face, and Pike and the weather. Q. Her eyes went over the rest of it and back up the page to the money.

On Saturday a line had lit up on his phone by her drainboard. *Do not disclose to witness.* She had read it standing at the sink as the ordinary things they kept from witnesses: names, plans, where the other man was held.

She shut the book on her thumb.

She sat with it shut and listened to him breathe. Under the snow the cove slapped at the ledge and drew back. Witness, on Pike's line. Subject, in his pencil. The same woman.

She had told him who sees the slip with his hands on the injector pump and the light going. She had given him the plan in the coal light with his shirt on the floor.

She opened it again, at the money.

Then she looked from the page to his arm. Five strips across it, the paper of the middle one gone pink.

She said it to him low, so as not to wake him.

"They missed the one they'd paid."

* * *

The snow had closed the cove. The far shore was gone. The low had drawn the water off the ledges below the bank, and she could see the first of them dry and dark before the white took them.

She put the notebook inside her coat against her ribs and did the coat up over it. She clipped her handheld inside, on nine.

The fish house was on paper now. It was in a book that went to Portland, and from Portland to whoever read what Portland had. She would not sleep another night in a place that was written down.

She took the red Pelican case off the shelf and opened it on the bench beside the gun, where his right hand would find it, the card of strips on top. She left the lid up. She had never once left it open.

She went to the door. The oak bar lay across it in its irons. She got both forearms under it and lifted it out. It was heavy across her arms, and the irons were cold where her wrists touched them coming free.

She stood holding it.

With it out, the door was only a door. Nobody could bar it from the outside. Behind her he slept on his right side, and he couldn't lift his left arm past his chest. She could say his name. He would wake; he woke at a footstep. She held the oak bar and didn't say it.

She shifted it into the crook of one arm, its end on the planks, and went to the cot. His mouth had come a little open. The stubble had gone dark along his jaw, and the gun lay under his right hand with the open case beside it. She held her hand over his chest, an inch off his shirt. The warmth came up off him into her palm. She took the hand back without touching him.

Snow hissed along the outside of the door. She carried the oak bar back across the planks and stood it against the wall beside the door, upright, its foot square on the floor so it wouldn't slide. She opened the door on the snow, and it came in across the sill onto the planks. She stepped out into it and drew the door to behind her, and in the last of the gap she saw the room.

The oak bar leaned beside the door, where it lived.

* * *

She came along under the bank at the low with the snow in her face and nobody on the ledges but her, and went up the bank by the Union Church and in at its side door to wait for dark and the night low.

The church had no heat in it. Her breath showed inside a building. Snow-light came through the clear windows and lay gray along the pews. Up in the tower the bell rope knocked against the boards in the wind, a dull wooden knock. Behind the vestry wall, in the lean-to, the minister's split oak was stacked to the rafters, and she could smell it through the boards, oak and wet bark.

A truck came along the Harbor Road, slow, wipers going. Russ's.

She stood at the window with her hand on the sash. Russ was the *Second Wind* and four hundred traps. He was also a badge. She could step out on the side step and lift a hand, and he would stop. Badges wrote things down. She kept her hand on the sash. The taillights went on toward the store, red in the snow, then gone.

Early afternoon. Out on the road side a handheld squawked.

A man's voice came through wind and static in pieces. "…church…" Then more she couldn't get. She knew every voice on nine by its first word, every man on the island and half the bay. She didn't know this one.

They were working the buildings down near the landing. Nobody had followed her here. Nobody knew to.

On the front steps of the church: a step. A drag. A step.

She knew the walk.

She was out through the vestry door before the front one moved. Ten yards of open snow to the woodshed, the notebook hard against her ribs under the coat. If he took her, he took every place in it: the fish house, and the man asleep in the fish house behind a door with nothing across it.

She went into the lean-to and through it to the open far side. There she turned and hauled the stack down behind her. The oak came off the top in a long rush across the gap, and the noise went off like a door slammed. Now he knew where she had gone. It bought her the seconds.

She went over the bank and down it on her heels and the snow went out from under her and she was on the weed and the ledge she wanted had gone under the flood and the cold came in over her boots and up her legs and she had the notebook up out of the wet in her fist and she was still going.

"Christ," she said, to the cold.

The flood was making. Above half tide the ledges were awash, and she went in to her hips and along under the bank, where nobody with that heel could follow on the weed. She kept moving.

Above her, along the top of the bank, the step and the drag came on, level with her, and stopped.

* * *

Under the face of the stone pier the riprap was black with weed, and over her head the brow's wheels ground on the granite at every surge.

She had come along the riprap below the road at the night low, out of the store's sight, and she had waited here since. The snow was over. The wind was rising ahead of the gale, and it found her even under the pier. Ned came on deck at two to check the lines and the pump. He had done it at two every night of every winter she'd worked for him. She knew his hours better than her own.

At two a flashlight came along the deck, held low, and stopped at the head of the brow.

She came up off the riprap onto the pier and into its light.

"Wind's going round to the nor'west," Ned said. "She'll blow harder by morning."

He held the light down on the brow so she could see her feet, and he let her aboard without a question. Big and stooped in the green jacket, he went ahead of her along the deck slow on the hip and took her into the crew mess.

The heater was going. A bulkhead lamp burned over the table, and somebody's mug had left a ring on it. Under her boots, under the steel, the forepeak pump ran steady, the sound of the boat keeping herself. A chart of the bay curled off the bulkhead at one corner where the tape had let go.

"Coffee," Ned said, and went for it. He hadn't said her name.

She stood at the heater with her back to it. Her wet clothes went stiff as they warmed, the jeans first, then the cuffs of her coat. She unbuttoned the coat and took the notebook out. The edges of it were wet through. The pages were sound.

She turned to the line about the seal and put her thumb under it.

The fare bag was always red. So that was the one the teller had written down: the boat's.

She turned the page back to the money.

Ned stood in the doorway with two mugs.

"Where's your marshal?"
