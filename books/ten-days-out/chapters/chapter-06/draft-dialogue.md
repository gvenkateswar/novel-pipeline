# Chapter Six: Good Till August

He tucked the pack into his jacket and went out under the bell. Through the glass Beth saw him pass Dane on the dock. A nod. Dane stayed on the piling.

At the end of the dock she turned. Through the front window Tess stood at the counter with her pen. She uncapped it and wrote something on the back of her own hand. From here Beth couldn't read it.

The phone went off in his jacket as he topped the rock. He didn't reach for it yet.

"Dollar says she opens with the weather."

"Nobody'd take that bet."

He answered it with his back to the wind. Afternoon, the tide high, the shore under the bank awash all the way to town, so nobody was walking anywhere. Beth climbed up after him and sat one rock below, facing up. The lichen was wet through her jeans.

He listened more than he talked. He got the notebook out one-handed and wrote on his knee, his thumb pinning the pages the wind kept trying to take.

Far down past his shoulder the inner harbor lay small and exact. The dragger sat on her guest mooring. Glass flashed on her wheelhouse, once, then again.

"Down," Beth said.

He went down on one knee, still listening, and finished the call crouched, his head below the line of the rock. The glass didn't flash again.

When it was done he sat looking at what he'd written. His thumb went to the red tag on his wrist and turned it once.

"Pike?"

"Pike. Weather."

Beth looked at the harbor.

He zipped the notebook into his jacket. She was already going down off the rock toward the fish house.

* * *

Wednesday's first sun came over the water flat from the east and lay on the ledge. It lay on both of them. Beth held her face up to it with her eyes shut. It had no warmth in it worth the name, and she stayed in it anyway until he moved.

Pike's answers had come in on the ledge before light. He gave them to her going down off the rock, one at a time.

"Coast Guard's sending a lifeboat at first light tomorrow, to the town float, because it's the only lee in this swell."

The rock was wet. She took it slow, and he came down behind her.

"They're bringing the inspector over for the ferry, which saves the government a boat, and you go back with them."

"No helicopter."

"Pike asked. 'District won't put a helicopter on a non-emergency move, and the one they've got is offshore on a case.'" He stepped down after her. "If it isn't tomorrow, it's Saturday at best, and Monday you're in Portland."

They came down off the granite onto the shore under the bank. The ebb was drawing off the ledges. The weed lay flat on them, shining.

"The landing's cleared with the county," he said. "Coast Guard won't use a town landing without the town knowing."

Beth stopped on the weed. The county would ring the island. The island had one person to ring.

"You told the county?"

"Portland did. Before they told me."

She went on.

Where the shore turned, the low ground opened, and there was the inner harbor. The *Margaret Ann* was off her guest mooring. She lay on a new one, closer in, with the town landing square in front of her bow.

"Moorings swing," Dane said.

"That isn't swinging."

She stood and looked at the dragger until he touched her sleeve.

"Tomorrow we go down to look. Anything wrong at the ramp head, we turn round."

"I'll know before you do."

"You will."

"Russ has a boat."

"Russ has a thermos. If anybody on that deck saw anybody Sunday, they saw Russ standing up on the hill with it. He stays at his own stove. I'd like him boring for a week."

They went on under the bank.

"Here's who knows the hour," he said. "Nobody. Not Tess, not Russ." He walked a few steps. "Nobody. Not Ned."

"Nobody," she said.

The last ledge before the road was all weed. His hand came under her elbow and stayed there till she had rock.

Below the road they went along the riprap, out of the store's sight, to the foot of the stone pier. Ned stood at the head of the brow and let them aboard. Dane went straight up the outside ladder to the wheelhouse to watch the pier.

Beth heard the forepeak before she saw it. The pump had quit. The boat had gone quiet under her boots.

The hatch stood open. Below it the water was up over the floor plates and working at the second rung of the ladder. Ned was down in it to his shins with the old impeller in his hand, its vanes chewed to stubs.

She went down the ladder. The cold came through her boots at the second rung and kept coming.

The forepeak smelled of rust and cold paint. Forward, where the frames came together, the crack ran a thin dark line down the plating. The water came in along it without any hurry.

"Glass is up," Ned said. He looked at the water round his shins. "So's this."

"Pump's not."

"New one's in the mess." He handed her the old one and went up the ladder slow on the bad hip.

At the top he stopped, his head and shoulders in the light of the hatch, and looked back down at her. Then he was gone.

She stood in the water with the dead impeller and listened to the boat. The cold had got into the bones of her fingers by then, an ache down to the wrist.

Ned came down with the box. Marr's price sticker was on the end of it. Her eye went over it and on.

He knelt in the water at the housing. She held the new impeller in place, both hands on the cold steel, while he turned it to seat it.

"That unit was dark Thursday," she said.

"He always runs his unit. Thermo King, he runs it continuous for live product, every Thursday since the Fourth of July two years back, you can hear it from the wheelhouse."

Beth kept her hands where they were.

The water moved against them. He got the first bolt in, then the second.

"Crates come open now and again," Ned said. "You know they do. A year back I started seeing too many pieces to the crate for the weight. Figured it was shorts. Boxes rode heavy, and I never gave that a thought. That contract's the winter boat. Lose it, the district runs her three days a week. Somebody gets laid off, and it wouldn't be me. So I never said."

He set the wrench down on the housing. "I thought it was shorts, Bethie."

She took her hand off the impeller and put it on his arm, on the wet green wool, a second. Then she picked up the wrench and gave it back to him.

He threw the switch. The pump coughed, caught and ran. The water started down the rung. Ned sat back on his heels in it and listened.

She had said Nobody on the shore, not half an hour back, and meant it. Ned was the boat. A question to the boat told nobody. She knew what she'd promised, and she asked him anyway.

"Is the float workable on the ebb?"

Ned wiped his hands down his jacket. "Which tide?"

"Tomorrow's first."

The gray blanket was nailed over the small window so no light showed on the water, and the drum ran low, its heat on one side of the room. Their wool steamed on the wire. His pot sat on the back of the drum, still half full; he had cooked for a crew. The bar was across the door. The gun lay on the bench at the head of the cot, within his reach.

Beth took the box out of her coat and set it on the bench beside the gun.

"Four seventy-nine," she said, in her Tess voice.

Dane looked at the box. "I'd have asked for a receipt. The government reimburses incidentals."

She laughed. He sat on the edge of the cot to work a boot off, and she sat down beside him.

"Why a boat?"

He got the boot off and held it. "Two summers sternmanning out of Rockport, fifteen and sixteen, for my mother's cousin, who had a thirty-two-footer and a bad back."

She took his hand off the boot and turned it over. Her thumb found the ridge of callus across the base of his fingers and went along it.

"That's where," she said. "Why?"

"Because of a girl named Marisol." He let her keep the hand. "In the car, on the way to the safe site, she asked me what I'd do if I weren't this. I said I didn't know. She said everybody who says that wants a boat."

The drum ticked.

"Then she asked for ten minutes at her sister's, and I gave her ten minutes. She's buried in Lawrence."

Beth got up. She took her wallet out of her coat on the nail, found the license and put it in his hand. "Hundred-ton," she said. "Never used." The corners were still sharp. Her father had done everything right on the water and lost anyway. She had never wanted to be the one at the wheel when it went wrong.

He tipped it to the drum's glow and read it. Then he gave it back, and she put it away.

Thursday. They were out of the fish house in the full dark, with the stars gone.

The swell came onto the ledges heavier than the wind could account for, a long slow lift that raised the weed and laid it down again. The wind had gone round in the night. She felt it on the wrong side of her face.

The ledges were dry behind the ebb, black under her boots, all but the last one under the bank.

She went in. The water came up past her knees, then closed round her thighs, and the cold took the air out of her in one pull. She stood in it until she had it back. Thigh-deep.

Behind her Dane was talking, low, a little at a time. She let him.

"Six days on this island," he said, "and I'm a worse wader every one of them. I didn't know you could lose a skill you never had."

"You were fine Monday."

His hand found the back of her coat and held on.

The rock came up under her boots. Then the lane, up behind the bait shed, frozen ruts and nobody in it. The shed's back door was shut. The ramp head. Off the landing the dragger's engine turned over.

Below them, the float.

The float lights were dark.
