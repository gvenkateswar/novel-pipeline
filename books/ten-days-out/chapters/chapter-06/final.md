# Chapter Six: Who Knew

Beth Calloway was frying onions in a stranger's kitchen at dusk, with the marshal asleep in the chair behind her, when she smelled smoke that wasn't hers.

She had found butter in the cold pantry and two onions in the grocery box. She had cut them thick and let them go slow in an iron pan. The smell had filled every shut room of the house before anything else was ready, the window over the sink had gone blue, and her feet were dry for the first time in three days.

The onions went from white to gold and stopped spitting.

Dane had sat up all last night in that chair with his gun on his knee and his face to the door. The condoms she had bought at Tess's counter were still zipped in her coat on the hook. At noon she'd made him take his boots off so his socks would dry.

Twenty minutes ago she'd heard him go under, and she'd let him sleep. The wind had covered every sound on the hillside since Friday.

Tonight she was going to wake him on purpose and take him up to the big bed, where she had slept alone last night with the door open so she could hear his chair. He wasn't getting the chair again.

Then a second smell came in under the onions: wet wood, and burning paint. Her stove smoke went up the chimney, so it took her a minute to know this was something else.

She slid the pan off the heat and stood still.

At the back of the house, where the wall met the woodpile, something was working, low and soft, like a kettle just before it boils.

Fire makes almost no sound until it is too late. She had listened all day and heard it late. Fear came up her arms and into her hands.

She crossed the kitchen and put her palm on his chest.

He was out of the chair with the gun in his hand before his eyes were open.

"I was resting my eyes," Dane said, "which is what my father says every Sunday, and he's lying too."

She didn't laugh. She pointed.

The back hall had gone orange. Smoke came along the ceiling, slow and gray.

Dane went to the edge of the front window. Down by the steps a flashlight shone, low and steady, at the door.

"He lit the back so we'd use the front," Dane said. "Dollar says he's waiting at the bottom of the steps."

Beth could see the light from where she stood. "I don't bet on what I can see. Pantry."

She had tried every window in this house today. The pantry one faced the woods, away from the steps, and it opened if you hit it.

She took her coat off the hook first and pulled it on over her father's sweater, then grabbed both pairs of boots off the mat. Her bag and the groceries stayed. On the way past she shut the little air vent on the stove. It was habit, and it was crazy.

The pantry window stuck. She hit the frame with the heel of her hand, the way you hit a swollen door, and it went up with a bang.

She went out first with both pairs of boots and dropped into the snow in her socks. She turned with her arms up for him. Above her, smoke rolled out over the sill into the last of the light.

* * *

The snow came through her socks at once, wet and heavy and over her ankles.

Dane dropped down beside her. The first night, this man had stood in the snow in his boots and put his coat around her. Now he stood in it in his socks.

They crouched against the side wall, out of sight of the front steps. Around the back corner the fire was loud now, and orange light spilled onto the snow.

Her boots went on in two seconds. His wouldn't. His had sat wet on the mat all day, and the laces had gone stiff as wire.

She pulled her gloves off with her teeth, knelt in the snow and laced them for him, fast, bare fingers, double knots. He kept the gun on the corner of the house.

"My mother used to do that," Dane said, "and she talked the whole time."

"Your mother had more time."

She started the second boot. He spoke low over her head, his eyes still on the corner. "When we're off this hill, I'm going to kiss you for an hour. It goes on the list ahead of sleep. I've thought hard about where on the list."

"Boots first," Beth said. She pulled the last knot tight. "An hour's not long, Marshal." She shoved her gloves in her pocket.

They went up into the spruces, away from the steps. Under the trees the snow came to their knees.

Fifty feet in, under the biggest tree, Beth stopped.

The snow there was packed hard and yellow-gray. A log had been brushed clear to sit on. Boot prints went from the log to the trunk and back, more than anybody could count. One clean print. Then the drag.

From the log she could see straight down between the branches into the kitchen window, to the stove where her pan was.

The man had stood here and watched her cook. He had been here since before first light, maybe all night. He had watched Dane sit up in that chair with his gun on his knee, and he had waited for Dane to fall asleep. All day she had stood at that window and thought nobody on earth could see her.

Dane looked at the log, and the prints, and the window.

The man who talked through everything said nothing. In the firelight his face was gray. He had slept, and the man had waited for it, and she could see Dane telling himself so.

So she had to be the one to talk, and it cost her.

"Dane. Move."

The first shot came from the corner of the house below them and cracked into the spruce over their heads. Snow came down off the branches onto them, all at once.

* * *

Beth ran, and she had his hand. His left hand in her right, below the tape on his arm. His gun was in his other hand.

She took them along the slope and then down through the trees toward the north shore, wide of the house. The snow went from her knees to her shins, and then there was rock under it.

"Light's coming up the side," Dane said behind her. "He's found the pantry window, and he's on our prints, and the foot makes him slow."

She said nothing. She listened.

The flashlight swung through the trunks above them, there and gone.

"He's stopped," Dane said. "He's listening, which means if we move he hears it, so here's what we—"

She pressed his hand once, hard.

He stopped in the middle of the word. Nobody in her whole life had ever stopped for her that fast.

They crouched behind a boulder while the light went over them, slow across the snow. Between gusts the wind dropped, and sleet ticked in the needles. Dane whispered at her ear. "Light's on the birches. Now it's past us. Now he's turning, which I don't love, because it means he heard something, and it was probably me."

"Quieter," Beth said.

"This is my quiet."

The man fired at the sound. The shot cracked close and loud in the trees and hit the boulder, and chips of rock stung her cheek. A second shot went into the dark past them.

They ran.

Behind them the light crossed a clearing, and in it she saw his track: one print, then a long line plowed by the bad foot.

"Nobody could see this house," Dane said, between breaths. "Somebody told him."

"I know."

She did know. Nobody had found the loft but Russ, and Russ had come up the outside stair in the open, with her bag. Four people had known about this house by Saturday afternoon. Now it was burning behind her, and three of the four were hers.

She tried her old habit, one plain sentence for each of them. Tess Marr had walked her home from every dance and every wake on this island. Tess was. The sentence stopped there. Russ Whitcomb had taught her to bait a trap when she was six. Russ was. That one stopped too.

Ned Haskell had hauled her off the float by her collar two mornings ago. Ned was. Nothing came after it. For the first time in her life she couldn't finish one.

"And one of them's mine," Dane said, running.

Three of them would die for me, she had told him yesterday. Now it sounded like what a woman says right before she finds out she's wrong.

Her hand stayed in his. She had known this man three days, and she trusted him more than anyone she had known her whole life. That was the worst news of the day. She held on harder.

They broke out of the trees at the top of the shore. Below them gray granite ran down into black water, white where the sea came up over it. Behind them, up the hill, the summer house was one whole lit window of fire, and they were standing in its light.

* * *

The rocks below were glazed with ice where the spray had frozen. The sea came up white over the low ones and went back. Beth had known this shore since she could walk.

At the top of the bank the flashlight came out of the trees and stopped.

"Down to the water," she said. "He won't come down iced rock on that foot. Not toward a man with a gun."

"And if we stay up here?"

"Up here we're lit. Down there it's dark, all the way north."

Dane looked at the rocks, and the ice, and the black water, and down at his own feet.

"Before we start," he said, "you should know I'm terrible at this, and every rock down there looks the same to me, and there were witnesses."

"I know. I watched you jump."

A shot cracked down from the bank and went wide, off the rock to their left.

Dane raised his gun. Past the glare there was nothing to aim at, and he didn't fire. Beth saw that.

They went down. She went first, on her heels and her hands, and the ice burned her palms, because her gloves were still in her pocket. She put her feet only where she knew the rock: the round backs, the cracks that held. Above her his boots scraped and caught, scraped and caught.

Then she heard it, out in the dark. A long gathering sound, the sea pulling back off the rocks and building. Dane couldn't see it yet. She could hear how big it was.

She had lost a house tonight, and her bag, and her trust in her three people, all in one hour. She was not losing him on her own shore.

She turned back to him on the rock, took his hand, and looked at him in the last of the fire's light. Out in the dark the wave came up white.

"Hold my hand and do exactly what I do."
