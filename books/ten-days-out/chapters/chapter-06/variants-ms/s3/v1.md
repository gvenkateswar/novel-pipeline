Wednesday's first sun came flat off the water and lay on the ledge, on both of them, with no warmth in it worth the name.

The phone went off in his jacket.

"Dollar says Portland opens with the weather."

"Nobody'd take that bet."

He answered it with his back to the wind. The wind came over the granite with nothing in its way and took every word before it reached her. He listened more than he talked, and wrote on his knee.

Far down past his shoulder the dragger sat on her guest mooring. Glass flashed on her wheelhouse, once, then again.

"Down," Beth said.

He went to one knee and finished the call crouched below the line of the rock. The glass didn't flash again.

"Pike?"

"Pike. Weather." He came down off the rock behind her. "Lifeboat at first light tomorrow to the town float, because it's the only lee in this swell, and the inspector rides over on her for the ferry while you ride back. Their helicopter's offshore on a case, and the county's cleared the landing."

The granite was wet, and she took it slowly. At the bottom the ebb was drawing off the ledges under the bank. The weed lay flat on them, shining.

The county would ring the island. The island had one person to ring.

"You told the county?"

"Portland did. Before they told me."

She went on along the weed.

"Going aboard this morning," she said. "Pump's eating impellers. Ned hears from me I'm going, or I don't go."

Dane walked a few steps. "He hears that you're going. He never hears when."

"Fine."

"Here's who knows the hour," he said. "Nobody. Not Tess, not Russ." He looked at her. "Nobody. Not Ned."

"Nobody," she said.

Where the shore turned, the bank fell away, and the low ground opened onto the inner harbor with nothing higher than her knee between them and the water. The *Margaret Ann* was off her guest mooring. She lay on a new one, closer in, with the town landing square in front of her bow. A man came aft along her rail with the left heel dragging. At the corner of her wheelhouse he stopped, facing the shore.

"Moorings swing," Dane said.

"That isn't swinging."

He put two fingers on her sleeve. "Back to the turn. We wait under the bank until he goes inside."

Beth looked at the water. Behind them the shore ran a mile and a quarter back to the cove and its one roof. Turn now, and they drew that glass up the shore to her father's door. And two people squatting in the weed were two people with a reason to squat.

"Turn round now and he walks us home," she said. "Walk."

She put her arm through his. Under the soft-shell his arm went hard, then gave.

"Slower," she said. "Walk like you live here."

"I've never lived anywhere this slowly."

They went out onto the open ground at an islander's pace. She kept her face on the harbor. A woman who had seen that boat every day of her life had no reason to look at it now.

Out on the rail the man turned with them.

"Last time anybody held my arm like this," Dane said, low, "I was six, and it was Merrimack Street."

"Shorter steps."

His elbow stayed tight against her ribs all the way across, his breathing slower than hers.

The last ledge before the riprap was all weed. His free hand came under her elbow and stayed there till she had rock.

On the riprap, in the lee of the pier face, he spoke without turning his head.

"Tomorrow we go down to look. Anything wrong at the ramp head, we turn round."

"I'll know before you do."

"You will."

At the foot of the stone pier she looked back. The man had turned all the way round, and he stood there while they went up the pier and over the brow onto the *North Reach*. She took her arm out of Dane's at the top. He went up the outside ladder to the wheelhouse to watch the pier.

Beth heard the forepeak before she saw it. The pump had quit. The boat had gone quiet under her boots.

The hatch stood open. Below it the water was up over the floor plates and working at the second rung of the ladder. Ned was down in it to his shins with the old impeller in his hand, its vanes chewed to stubs.

She went down the ladder. The cold came through her boots at the second rung and kept coming. The forepeak smelled of rust and cold paint.

"Glass is up," Ned said. He looked at the water round his shins. "So's this."

"Pump's not."

"New one's in the mess." He handed her the old one and went up the ladder a rung at a time, both hands on the rails.

At the top he stopped, his head and shoulders in the light of the hatch, and looked back down at her. Then he was gone.

She stood in the water with the dead impeller and listened to the boat. The cold had got into the bones of her fingers, an ache down to the wrist.

Ned came down with the new impeller in a box with Marr's price sticker on the end.

He knelt in the water at the housing. She held the impeller in place, both hands on the cold steel, while he turned it to seat it.

"Third since the bridge," Ned said. "She eats them when the water's dirty. Always has."

The vanes went in. She kept the pressure on.

"That unit was dark Thursday," she said.

"He always runs his unit. Thermo King, he runs it continuous for live product, every Thursday since the Fourth of July two years back, you can hear it from the wheelhouse."

Beth kept her hands where they were.

The water moved against them. He got the first bolt in, then the second.

"Crates come open now and again," Ned said. "You know they do. A year back I started seeing too many pieces to the crate for the weight. Figured it was shorts. Boxes rode heavy, and I never gave that a thought. That contract's the winter boat. Lose it, the district runs her three days a week. Somebody gets laid off, and it wouldn't be me. So I never said."

He set the wrench down on the housing. "I thought it was shorts, Bethie."

She took her hand off the impeller and put it on his arm, on the wet green wool, a second. Then she picked up the wrench and gave it back to him.

He threw the switch. The pump coughed, caught and ran. The water started down the rung. Ned sat back on his heels in it and listened.

Up in the wheelhouse Dane was watching the pier for her. She had said Nobody to his face on the shore, with her arm through his. Ned's the boat. You asked the boat about the water.

"Is the float workable on the ebb?" she said.

Ned looked up at her. "Which tide?"

"Tomorrow's first."

He wiped his hands down his jacket and reached past her for the tide table tacked to the bulkhead.

"Wind goes round tonight. Morning's quiet." He ran a black fingernail down the card. "High's half past three. First light she's a third gone, plenty under the float, best lee in the harbor. Shore under the bank's dry by then, all but the last ledge, and that one's still deep in the dark. Float's inshore end sits down near the low, so you want to be off her before nine. Hand me that rag."
