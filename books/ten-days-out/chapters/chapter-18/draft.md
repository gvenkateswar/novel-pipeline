# Chapter Eighteen: The Right Boots

Beth Calloway bailed out Dane Reyes's brand-new old boat on Thanksgiving morning, laughing too hard to do it well.

He sat on the engine box with his coffee mug. He dipped it into the water around his boots, lifted it over the side, and poured it out, one cup at a time. The doctor had said he could lift a mug. He was holding the doctor to it.

"I can't lift anything," he said.

"I know. I'm bailing."

She scraped the bucket along the bottom planks and threw the water over the rail. Cold water came over her ankles again before the bucket was back. The harbor lay so still she could hear each throw land.

It was a wooden lobster boat, older than both of them, its cedar planks gone gray. He had bought it by phone on Tuesday without seeing it. It was the boat in Pruett he'd been driving up to see on the Saturday he got the call instead. It had come across on her car deck Wednesday, and now it sat on Russ's empty mooring, low in the water and leaking from every seam.

"You bought a boat you never saw."

"I'd seen eleven. I knew what I'd get."

"Leaks."

"I knew it would leak."

She ran her thumb along a seam. Her palms were healing pink under the light wrap, and the cold made them ache.

"It'll take up in a week. Till then you bail."

The planks had dried out all fall on land, and in the water they'd swell until the seams closed.

She looked at his feet. Brown leather, high laced, oiled, with good soles. Water ran over the toes and stayed out.

"Those are the right boots."

"Tess charged me full price."

"She charges everybody full price."

"She called it the marshal price."

Beth laughed again and lost half a bucket into her lap.

Across the harbor the ferry lay at the stone pier, white in the sun. The board had her running it until June, when they decided. From its cabin she would see this boat every time she came in.

Dane watched the seams with her. "Dollar says we're still bailing at noon."

She looked down at the planks. She couldn't see into them. Nobody could. Wood took up when it took up.

"Done."

He stopped with the mug in the air. He stared at her, and he kept staring, and he didn't say one word.

"Bail," she said.

He bailed. He told her about the engine while he did it, and how he meant to strip it down in the spring on his own days off. She wanted him, in those boots, on his leaking boat. She said it to herself in exactly those words and kept bailing.

The church bell rang noon across the water.

The water was back over her ankles.

She dug in her coat pocket and came up with a wet dollar and put it in his hand. His hand was warm. She owed nobody a dollar, and she wasn't going to start with him.

He folded it once and put it in his shirt pocket, over the stitches.

"I'm keeping this one."

"Bail, Marshal."

* * *

Beth stood in the kitchen doorway and watched Dane at the oven.

He had his sleeves pushed up past the elbow, and the steam off the pan rose around his forearms when he bent to spoon the fat over. The woodstove was going. The smell had filled the house before anything else was close to ready, and she could have stood there and watched him do that all afternoon.

"It's Thanksgiving," she said. "That's a ham."

"I've ruined every turkey I ever met. A ham forgives you."

The letter had come Wednesday in the mail bag she'd carried across herself. She sat down with it at her father's table and opened it.

Pencil. Ned's hand, big and slanted, pressed hard enough to dent the paper.

*Beth,*

It began with her name. There was nothing in front of it.

*Cold here. You can't see the water from anywhere in this place.*

*My boy's at the clinic in Portland. Second week. He says it's the worst place he's ever been. I told him that's how you know.*

*I wrote the board Monday and took my name off your letter. A letter from me would cost you that job. Get somebody from the island to speak for you.*

*Ned.*

That was all. No sorry. Nothing asked, and nothing about the beach or the road.

She read it once and turned it face down on the table. Ned Haskell had hauled her out from between the ferry and the pier on the first morning and sold her by the end of the week, and a page in pencil didn't change what that added up to.

Nobody from Cutter had spoken for her to the board now. In June they'd decide.

She got up. The drawer under the counter had stuck for years. She worked it out with both wrapped hands, found the candle stub on the windowsill, and rubbed it along the wooden runners until they shone. She slid the drawer back in. It ran the whole way, and by the time it did, she knew.

"He took his name off my letter to the board," she said.

Dane set the spoon down. "Who'll you ask?"

"Tess."

"She isn't speaking to you."

"Then she can write."

She put Ned's letter in the drawer and shut it. It ran smooth.

* * *

Beth heard the cane on the front step before she heard the knock.

Russ stood on the step in his brown jacket and his Red Sox cap, leaning on the cane with both hands. They'd flown him out Tuesday, and he'd come home on her ferry Wednesday night against the doctor's advice, and she'd made him sit in the cabin with his leg up the whole way across.

He ran his thumb over the pale new plug of wood in her front door, at hip height, where the bullet had come through.

"Did you fix that? You did."

"Tuesday," Dane said behind her.

Tess came up the step after Russ with a pie in both hands, apple, the crust crimped all the way round. She had her red work vest on under her coat and her fingerless gloves on. She held the pie out to Beth.

"Calloway."

That was all. She didn't hug her. She walked past into the kitchen and sat down in the chair by the stove, the one she had always taken at this table, and kept her gloves on.

Beth set the pie on the counter and stood a moment with her hands on either side of it.

Dane carved. The ham was far too big for four people, and he had known it would be when he bought it, and he carved it thick anyway while the light went yellow in the windows. Russ watched the platter come to the table.

"Is it the same ham? It isn't."

They ate. Russ had seconds and then thirds, with his bad leg out straight under the table. Tess talked to Russ and to Dane about the price of heating oil and the Patriots, and passed Beth the potatoes without looking at her. Nobody went hungry. There would be enough left for the week.

When the plates were pushed back, Dane put his forearms on the table and didn't reach for his coffee.

"I'm on leave till January, and then I start at the Maine office," he said. "I'd like to stay on this island and live in this house with Beth, and she's said yes, and she told me to ask the two of you, because it's your island, and I'd like to hear it from you."

Under the table Beth's knee was against his. She watched his mouth while he said it, and she didn't care who saw.

Russ took his cap off and put it back on. "Do we mind? We don't."

Tess looked at Dane's hands first, as if she were pricing them. Then she looked at his face, for a long time.

"Coffee's a dollar, Marshal. Same as everybody."

* * *

Beth dried while Tess washed, and Tess ran the water too hot.

Steam came up off the sink into the cold kitchen and fogged the bottom of the window. Tess pulled her fingerless gloves off to wash and set them on the windowsill, one on top of the other. Beth did that. Neither of them said so.

Through the doorway Russ had his leg up on a chair, and Dane was telling him about the engine.

"You'll open those hands up again," Tess said to the dishwater.

"Then I'll tape them."

Tess handed her a plate. The towel rasped on Beth's healing palms through the wrap. She set the plate on the stack, and then she said it, all of it, before she could find something else to fix.

"Ned took his name off my letter to the board. I need somebody from here to speak for me in June. Will you?"

Tess rinsed a glass and held it up to the window. "I wrote them Tuesday."

Beth stopped drying.

Tuesday. The day after Portland. Russ flown off the island that morning, and Tess not saying one word to her, and Tess had sat down that same day and written to the board.

"You're not speaking to me."

"I'm not. I'm writing to the board."

Beth put the towel over her shoulder. The water ran.

"I put you on the list," she said. "I'd put you on it again."

"I know you would." Tess scrubbed at a pan that was already clean. "Somebody you loved was selling you, and I lied to you all week about Russ. I'd have put me on it too." She set the pan in the rack hard. "You never once asked me to my face. You've known me your whole life."

"Would you have told me about Russ?"

"No."

Neither of them moved. Then Tess said the true part, low, with her hands still in the water.

"He got shot on your step."

"He did. I can't pay that back."

"No. You can't."

Beth owed nobody a dollar. She had made sure of it for thirty-three years. She would owe Tess this for the rest of her life, and neither of them tried to make it smaller.

Through the doorway Dane laughed at something Russ said and pressed a hand to his ribs where the stitches were, and went on laughing. Beth wanted to go in there and put her own hand over his.

Tess turned off the water.

"Beth."

Beth looked at her. Tess met her eyes.

"You're the best hand on that water, and I won't have a stranger from the mainland at our wheel. That's what the letter says. The rest isn't in it."

She handed Beth the next plate.

"Dry, Calloway."

* * *

Beth found Russ at the front window with one arm in his coat, leaning on his cane and looking out at the dark.

The lamp was on behind them. When she stood beside him, the cold came off the glass onto her face. The harbor was black. Far out past the harbor mouth there was one light. It sat low on the water and didn't move, too far off to be anything she could name.

Tess came to Russ's other shoulder. She looked out, and then she looked at Russ, and he turned his head and met her eyes.

Beth wanted them gone. She wanted the lamp out and the dishes stacked and Dane to herself. She stood at the glass and waited.

"In New Bedford they burned that woman's house after she testified," Russ said. "Didn't they? They did." He worked his other arm into the coat. "Does a cell stop people like that?"

Dane stood by the front door with Tess's pie plate in his hand. He didn't make a joke of it.

"Not always," he said. "So I watch the door."

He had no gun. The deputies had kept it at Pruett, and it was still in Portland. He'd watch the door anyway.

"There's one more thing," Dane said. "The kid swore he never put that phone behind the bait truck's wheel."

Russ leaned his weight onto the cane.

"Then somebody on this island did. Who? I don't know." He was quiet a moment. "I will."

Tess buttoned her coat to the chin. "We will."

Dane gave Tess her pie plate and gave Russ a foil package of leftovers the size of a toolbox. Russ held it against his chest with his free arm and said nothing at all.

They went out onto the step, the cane first. Tess stopped in the doorway with the cold coming in around her.

"Next year's at mine, Calloway."

Then she went down the path after Russ. Beth listened to the cane on the frozen road until she couldn't hear it.

Dane shut the door. He slid the new bolt he'd put on Tuesday and tried it, and then he stood with his hand on it longer than any bolt needed. Beth wanted that hand off the bolt and on her, and she crossed the room to him.

"Your ten days are up, Marshal."

"I know."

She put her wrapped hand over his on the bolt. "So. Are you any good at your job?"

He turned his hand over under hers and held on.

"Ask me every night."
