## Where I got bored

- **The sleeping-arrangement argument in the store.** "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will." / "She's sleeping at mine." / "No. She isn't." Tess has already made the offer and Beth has already said no ("Can't. Not tonight, Tess."). This is the same exchange again, with Russ added as a third voice.
- **"Going back in through the office, Beth picked the two keys for Tess's Ford on their MARR'S tag off the charging shelf and hung them back on the hook, her knuckles in the cords, Ned's phone in its rubber band among the others."** My attention dropped here. It is an inventory sentence. I could tell it was planting things (the Ford keys, Ned's phone), and the sentence was so busy doing that job that I stopped seeing the room.
- **"Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color."** It reads as a rule the book wants me to remember for later, not as something Tess would say in a fury. I filed it away as a clue and lost the scene while I did.
- **Ned at the side wall.** The Ashby key exchange is short, but it repeats "Me and God" from Ch 02 almost word for word. The first time it was charming. This time I could see it becoming a catchphrase.

## Where I got confused

- **The Ch 02 cliffhanger is dropped.** Ch 02 ends on "Who knew you were here?" / "You." and her picturing his phone glowing on the front step. Ch 03 opens with "nobody came back," and that conversation never happens. They sat all night behind the chimney and apparently said nothing about it. I wanted that exchange. Its weight gets moved onto the phone-reading the next morning, which works, but I felt the gap.
- **"Twenty-two forty."** Tess holds up her knuckles with a time on them and I had no idea what it meant. I worked out from the next line ("Your back steps had you home") that she saw Beth's flashlight on the back steps at 10:40, but only after a second read. On first pass it read as a number for its own sake.
- **"Then you know who else has a window."** Who? Whose window? I think this means someone else watches Beth's steps, but I don't know who, and I'm not sure if I'm meant to know yet.
- **Russ.** In Ch 01 he was a name on a list ("Russ was the *Second Wind*"). In Ch 03 he arrives with Dane as if they've been working together all morning. When did they meet? Why is Russ helping?
- **Arsenault.** "Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, the dragger that had stayed lit through the blackout." In Ch 02 it was only "the Gloucester boat," and the man had no name. Now he has a surname, a boat name, a first name (Lyle) and an account at Tess's, all in two lines. I got it, but it felt sudden.
- **"Saturday noon."** The message said "Firm at thirty-one. Saturday noon," and Dane says the thirty-one is his. I took that to mean the boat he was going to look at. Then the store clock hits noon and Dane looks at the clock, then at Arsenault. Why Arsenault? Is the boat seller Arsenault? Was the meeting something else? I can't tell whether the book is telling me to suspect Dane, Arsenault, or nobody. Being unsure would be fine if I could tell it was on purpose. I couldn't.
- **"She hadn't given him Ned last night."** What didn't she give him about Ned? The only thing I can think of is the bags at the foot of the ladder in Ch 01, or Ned saying "Nobody but me and God." I don't know what she was protecting him from.
- **"Then don't sit with your back to me."** I read the chair business twice. I think she takes the chair facing the door so he has to sit with his back to it, as a test of whether he'll trust her. I got there, but slowly.
- **Who says "Sea coming. Stand still."?** I think Beth shouts it from the back step, but there's no tag, and Tess has just spoken. Same with "What am I looking at?" (Dane, I think, about the log) and "Nobody but—" (Beth, I think, cutting in on Ned).

## Where I stopped believing

- **The phone face up on the table, unlocked, with the message open.** In Ch 01 the book made a point of him turning the screen away "as if he did it in his sleep." Hours after a night when his phone glowing on the step may have got someone into her mudroom, he puts the phone face up in front of the witness with "Do not disclose to witness" on the screen. Unless he wanted her to see it, and the page gives no sign of that, this is the plot needing her to read it. It's the biggest convenience in the book so far.
- **A marshal leaves his only witness to chase a man into the fish houses.** He drops her in a store with Tess's hand on her sleeve and runs off after a hood. It gives us the great moment where he stops dead because she told him to, but after two chapters of "stay behind me" it seemed out of character, and the book doesn't comment on it.
- **Calling in from the porch again.** After last night, he phones Portland with her standing next to him and tells them where she is. "North end. Not the house" softens it a little. I believed Beth letting it happen, because that's the point and it hurts. I'm less sure a careful man would do it at all without saying something about last night's leak first.
- **The consent exchange.** "Do you want—" / "Yes." / "Do you?" / "Yes." I believed the kiss and the hands. This exchange felt like the book making sure, more than two people out of breath in a shed. "Under. Not over." was much better, because it sounds like her.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **The "longer than X needed" construction.** It's now a tic across all three chapters. In Ch 03: "stayed that way longer than the pouring needed," "a beat longer than snow needed," "a carton of D batteries that didn't need moving." In earlier chapters: "longer than the chain needed," "a stick in the stove it didn't need," "made the line do a thing it didn't need to." I started looking for it, and once a reader is looking for a pattern, the pattern is all they hear.
- **Acting before deciding.** "before she'd decided anything," "she hadn't decided to, and her mouth was asking for things before she'd thought them," and in Ch 01 "before she'd thought about it." Beth's body keeps acting ahead of her, and the book keeps telling me so in the same words.
- **Callbacks stacked up.** "Lift and shove," "Me and God," the milk ("You're out of milk" / "Hand me the milk"), the hand going to the hip. Each one is fine. Three or four in one chapter makes the book feel pleased with its own echoes.
- **Dane's quips all have the same shape.** "I owe you a floor, Ms. Marr. Put it on the government's account." / "I went in like a man looking for the restroom." He's funny, but every line is self-deprecating with a formal turn at the end. By the third chapter I can predict where his sentences will go.
- **Tess and Russ talk alike.** Clipped, wry, island-flinty: "They're all hard." / "Didn't, did you." / "Restroom's for customers." If I covered the tags I'm not sure I could tell them apart.
- To be fair: the scene endings in this chapter are mostly not neat button lines. The Tess beat (blankets back under the counter, "She didn't look up") and the porch ending both land on an image, not a quip. That helped.

## What I want to know next

Strong pull. These are the questions:

- **What is Q's request, and what is Dane logging about her?** "Do not disclose to witness" is the hook of the chapter, and he has refused her directly. I want to know if he's using her as bait.
- **Is Tess dirty?** The fold of hundreds in Ch 02, Arsenault's cash stacked on the counter and Tess hurrying to put it on his account, "Don't you look at my book like the bank does," and Beth covering the log with her palm. I think Tess is taking money from the dragger, maybe for fuel at night off Marr's float (the float lit at an odd hour in Ch 01). I want to know how deep she's in, and whether she loves Beth anyway. I think she does, which makes it good.
- **Is Ned in it?** The bags, the colors, "I won't have my money in Ned's color," his phone at the store. I'm afraid of where that's going, and I don't want it to be Ned.
- **Where is the *Margaret Ann* going with her lights off?** Toward the north end, I assume, and Dane has just told Portland "north end."
- **Who leaked last night?** Portland, Q, Dane, or someone with a window. The book hasn't answered and the porch call makes it urgent.
- **What did she not give him about Ned?**

## Predictions

- The Ashby generator tank "rang near empty." It will run dry at the worst possible moment, probably tonight, and the house will go dark.
- The *Margaret Ann* going dark means she's moving. Someone comes to Ashby by water, from the side the house is open to on three sides.
- The leak is upstream of Dane: Portland or Q. Dane is honest but is being used, or is under orders that use Beth. The log and "Q's request" are about treating her as bait, not about betraying her personally.
- Tess is fuelling or banking for Arsenault's people and breaks for Beth before the end.
- Ned carries money on the ferry, maybe without knowing what it is. This will be the book's real heartbreak.
- The "thirty-one" is the Webbers Cove boat. He'll buy it and stay on the island in the last chapter.
- The heel-dragger is local ("quick and sure of every plank," "like he'd built them," rows "like somebody who had learned before he could walk"). Someone Beth grew up with.

## Listener pass (read as audio)

- **The store scene is the hardest to follow by ear.** Four speakers, several untagged lines ("Sea coming. Stand still." / "What am I looking at?" / "Nobody but—"), and a jump from inside the store to Ned "at the side wall" with no line telling me we went outside. Heard aloud, I'd lose track of who is talking and where.
- **"Twenty-two forty"** spoken aloud sounds like a figure or a price, not a time. Without the page in front of me I wouldn't connect it to the back steps.
- **The keys sentence** ("picked the two keys for Tess's Ford on their MARR'S tag off the charging shelf and hung them back on the hook, her knuckles in the cords, Ned's phone in its rubber band among the others") is a tongue-twister and a stack of nouns. A narrator would stumble, and I'd stop listening.
- **The italic phone messages** ("*Pike: Q's request approved. Log daily. Do not disclose to witness.*") will work aloud only if the narrator signals that it's text on a screen. "Pike" and "Q" arriving as bare letters by ear will sound like typos.
- **The kitchen and the shed play very well aloud.** The rhythm in the shed ("It coughed and coughed and ran, ragged and then even, and the shed filled with the hammer of it") and the long final sentence on the porch carry. The kiss paragraph with its run of "and"s works better heard than read.
- The repeated "longer than X needed" shape will be even more noticeable in audio, where the ear catches repetition faster than the eye.

## Best moment

The last paragraph:

> He turned his head once in the middle of it and found her at his shoulder. His mouth stopped. Then he stepped round her on the step, so that he stood between her and the water, and went on talking, and she moved up the last few inches until her arm was against his, and stayed there in the snow while he told Portland, in words she couldn't hear, where she was.

He shields her with his body while his phone gives her away, and she chooses to stand against him anyway. All of the book's tension is in one sentence and none of it is explained. Runner-up: "That lasted until he turned his jaw to the light and ran his thumb along it to check, and she wanted it to be her thumb."
