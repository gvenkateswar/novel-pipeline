# Chapter Three: The Generator Shed

The print was at the foot of the back steps, pressed into last night's slush and frozen hard. Beth set her boot down beside it. In daylight it was plain: a clean right foot, and a left one with the heel dragged out behind it in a groove. The man from the reefer had stood on her steps.

Inside, the stove was lit at last, and the kettle had the window over the sink running with steam.

Dane stood at the counter drawing a thumb along his jaw. His bag in the front room was the size a man packs for one night.

She opened the cabinet over the sink and took down her father's razor, the heavy safety razor with the knurled handle, and set it on the drainboard. She took down his shaving mirror too and propped it in the window frame where he used to prop it. She didn't say anything.

He poured kettle water into the basin and bent to the mirror. "Your father didn't want to see much of himself."

"Enough to shave."

He shaved slowly, the way people handled a dead man's things in front of his daughter. He tipped his head back for the throat, and she watched the long line of it go taut under the lather, and the razor come up it in short strokes, and the cords in his forearm move when he rinsed the blade and tapped it twice on the edge of the basin, which was what her father had done, and which nobody had shown him. His sleeves were shoved up past the elbow. There was a man in her father's kitchen, at her father's sink, being careful, and she was standing by the stove with a mug going cold in her hands and looking at his mouth when he drew his upper lip down for the razor. She told herself she was watching so he wouldn't nick himself on a blade she'd have to clean. That lasted until he turned his jaw to the light and ran his thumb along it to check, and she wanted it to be her thumb.

"He tapped it twice too," she said. "On the basin."

The razor stopped. "I can quit doing that."

"Don't."

He wiped the razor dry on the dish towel, set it back on the drainboard, and went out to the yard with the phone. She didn't go to the window.

He came back in, set the phone face up on the table, and went to the stove for the pot. The phone was still lit.

She picked it up. Last night the same light had been under his chin in the front yard, while somebody stood in her mudroom. One message was open.

*Pike: Q's request approved. Log daily. Do not disclose to witness.*

She pressed the arrow once.

*Firm at thirty-one. Saturday noon.*

He turned with the pot and saw it in her hand, and he shut his eyes for a second, a man who hadn't slept.

"Who's paying you thirty-one, and what don't I get told?"

"Nobody pays me but the government. The thirty-one's mine." He gave her the rest without hurrying. "The other one I can't tell you about. And I won't lie to you about whether there's something."

A log, then. Where they kept her and when, and the things about the case they never told a witness. She set the phone on the drainboard beside her father's razor and left them there.

He turned to the stove to pour, his back to her, and stayed that way longer than the pouring needed.

"Then don't sit with your back to me."

She took the chair facing the door herself, the one he'd have taken. The other faced hers across the table and put the door behind him. He stood with a mug in each hand and looked at it, and she watched him decide to sit in it.

* * *

Marr's was full, because in a storm everybody on Cutter came to the store. The urn coffee was free to the storm. Out back the generator roared flat and steady, and by the counter the floor gave underfoot where the old sills were going soft.

Dane had his coffee and his back to the hardware shelf, and he asked the room the way you'd ask about a lost dog. "Anybody know a man who drags his left heel? Leaves a groove in the slush."

Russ Whitcomb was leaning on the counter. He took his cap off, looked into it, and put it back on. "That narrows it to about forty of us."

"That's thirty-nine more than I was hoping for. What about the bridge cable? I've been thinking about it since it went over my head."

"Somebody cut that cable?" Russ asked the urn. "Somebody'd have to beat the rust to it. That tower's been on the district's list since Labor Day." He pushed off the counter. "Come look at the road end."

The two of them went out, and while the bell was still swinging behind Dane, Tess was snapping a yellow tag through the zipper of the deposit bag, for whenever the boat ran, and talking the whole time, to Beth, to the room, to the bag. "Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color. Propane's up a dollar a tank."

"Not to me."

"To you especially. Calloway, you look like a wet cat."

Then she leaned both hands on the counter.

"Net had you aboard." She said it low, which Tess never did. "Your back steps had you home. Twenty-two forty." She held up the back of her left hand. There in ballpoint among the gallons, *22:40*, and there again in the storm log open on the counter between them, in her square capitals. "Didn't put you on the air, Beth."

Then, fast and bright, before Beth could get a word in: "You'll sleep at mine. I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one. The cot's made up, the stew's on, and the marshal can eat his on the porch. Not wherever the two of you are hiding."

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

"Then tomorrow."

Beth didn't answer that. She looked at the log a second too long, down the night's column of entries, and Tess's voice went sharp as a gaff. "Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

The bell went, and Dane came in stamping snow off his boots, and Beth's palm was flat on the page, over 22:40, before she'd decided anything. The spiral wire bit into her wrist. Under her hand Tess's capitals ran down the night, and she kept her hand there while he crossed the floor, and kept it there when he stopped at her elbow.

"What am I looking at?"

"Nothing in it. Soup for Ida and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk."

He handed her the milk.

Behind the counter the curtain to Tess's office hung half open, and Beth knew what was in there the way she knew boats: the hook with the two keys for Tess's Ford on their MARR'S tag, and under it a shelf of phones charging, the plumber's with the Red Sox sticker, Russ's cracked case, Ned's with the rubber band round it. Dane was looking too. Tess twitched the curtain shut. "Only place on the island it's a phone."

Tess's own phone went off in her vest. She took it on her shoulder and went on ringing up the plumber's propane while she talked. "Ida Dyer. Eighty-eight, with that heart." She listened. "Pilot's call. Icing and ceiling. I know it is. EMT stays with her, then." She put it away. "LifeFlight won't lift her. If they won't fly for Ida, nobody's flying off this rock."

Dane looked at her over his cup. "So far today you're the post office, the hardware store and air traffic control. What is your job, exactly?"

"Emergency management director. Since town meeting. I was slow putting my hand down."

"She never put it down," Beth said.

The bell again. A tall man came in out of the snow in a good Musto jacket, clean-shaven, the only clean-shaven man on a dragger in the bay. He was the *Margaret Ann*, Arsenault out of Gloucester, the boat that had stayed lit through the blackout. "Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having." He counted exact change onto the counter before Tess said the price.

"The gentleman's having the free coffee," Dane said. "But I'll take the thought."

"Put that away, Lyle. It's on your account." Tess pushed the coins back across the counter.

"Tess, you'd give the store away."

She laughed too loud, the laugh you could hear from the fuel float.

He turned to Beth, kind as a deacon. "I bought fuel off your father when you were about this high. Good man. Bad luck with banks."

"Banks weren't luck."

Over the door the clock's hands came together at noon. Dane looked at it, then at Arsenault, and stayed where he was. Beth had read the message that morning, and she watched him let it go.

Beth found Ned round the side wall of the store with a cup from the urn, out of earshot.

"Ashby," she said.

Her father's fish house on Calloway Cove had the tide over its sill and would till Monday. Ashby had water on three sides and one road in, and Ned started its generator every storm, so a generator running out there would only look like Ned.

He worked a key off his ring and put it in her glove. "Nobody but—"

"Me and God. I know."

Russ came round the corner and leaned on the shingles beside them, cap off, cap on, with Dane a step behind him. "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

* * *

The north road went up over the spine of the island in the dark, and the snow squeaked under their boots. Along the shore below, the houses were black except where a generator kept a kitchen window yellow. The Ashby key was cold through her glove. She walked fast. She had been looking at the harbor all day, and he had watched her do it.

"Any boat down there could be theirs," he said to the road ahead of them. "I can't tell good water from bad in this, which you've noticed. And whoever runs you across leaves a wake the whole bay can follow." The snow squeaked a while. "I've been carrying that around since noon."

She didn't answer. Ashby came up at the end of the road, a big shingled shape with water on three sides, and below the house the generator shed sat on its pad. She dragged the shed door open on its frozen track and took hold of the pull cord.

* * *

She primed it by feel and knocked on the tank, which rang near empty, and put the cord in his hand. "Long and even. Don't yank it."

He pulled. The engine coughed once and quit. He pulled again and got nothing but the rasp of the cord going home into the housing, and their two breaths hanging in the beam of the flashlight he'd stood on the fuel can.

"Third one," she said.

"Is that a rule?"

"It is for this one."

On the third pull it caught. It coughed and coughed and ran, ragged and then even, and the shed filled with the hammer of it, and she was watching his face when it happened. He grinned like a boy with a fish on. He stood with the cord handle still in his fist and his other hand pressed to his chest, as if he could feel the engine in there, and maybe he could. Through the open door, up the slope, the big house's windows came up yellow behind the sheets.

"Is that what you look like when something works?"

He looked at her. He had talked all day, to Russ and Tess and the urn and the snow, and now he didn't say a word.

She put her back to the engine housing. It was warm already through her coat and getting warmer, and it shook against her with every stroke. She took his jacket in her fist and brought him down to her.

His mouth was cold for a second and then it wasn't. He went still, the whole length of him, and then he kissed her back slowly, as if he had thought about this and meant to take his time over it. He tasted of the store's terrible coffee. She heard herself make a sound she never made.

"Beth." Against her mouth. Nothing else.

"Your hands," she said.

He brought them up and stopped an inch off her zipper, and they waited there.

"Where?"

"Under. Not over."

She opened her own coat. His hands went in under the sweater and the thermal and onto the bare small of her back, and they were so cold she swore into his mouth. He laughed. She felt it against her teeth, and he kept his hands where they were, which was what she wanted.

Then they warmed, and moved, up her spine and round over her ribs under the wool, slow, his thumbs learning them, and the engine beat at her back and the cold came in at the door and the house up the hill stood lit for them, and she had both hands in his belt, pulling him in, and she noticed she was pulling him and that she hadn't decided to, and that her mouth was asking for things before she'd thought them, and she let it.

"Do you want—"

"Yes." He said it into her hair. Then, quieter: "Do you?"

"Yes."

He leaned in, and his jacket swung against her hip, and something in the inside pocket pressed into her through everything, square and hard. The phone. She knew what was on it as well as if she still held it: *Log daily*, and the other thing, the one he couldn't tell her.

She took her hands back. He stopped at once. His hands came out from under the wool, and the cold went in after them.

"That's the phone," she said.

"Yes."

"I'd rather be kissed by a man I can check." Her voice had gone quiet and exact, the voice she used on deck when a line was about to part. "I read it this morning, Dane Reyes. I put it down by my father's razor, and I didn't ask you twice. I'm asking now. What you write every day. What's in it that I don't get told?"

He stepped back a full pace, so the engine's noise stood between them. When he answered, it was polite and slow. "Ms. Calloway. I'll say this once, because I'd like you to have it right. I won't lie to you about whether there's something. That's all I have that I can give you, and I know what it's worth."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No."

"Don't write me down."

It came up out of her from a long way down, older than him, and she heard it go and couldn't call it back.

He took it without arguing. He looked past her at the door, at the yellow windows up the slope. "I have to call it in. The move, and the house." He picked the flashlight up off the fuel can. "I'll do it from the porch. You can watch me do it."

She shut the shed door on the engine and went up the slope ahead of him with Ned's key in her glove, and let them into a house that smelled of mothballs and cold wallpaper. The furniture stood about under sheets. Somebody from Connecticut had tacked a sheet over the front window too, against the summer sun.

He went out onto the porch, to the top step under the open sky, and she took the edge of the sheet and held it back from the glass.

Snow came down between them through the porch light. He had the phone to his ear and his face turned up to the sky, and his mouth was moving, and she couldn't hear one word of it over the generator down the slope. She watched his breath go up and come apart in the snow.

The move. The house. The north point, water on three sides and one road in. She could say that much of it for him. The rest she couldn't: whether the shed went in, and whether she did, and who read it after Portland.

He turned once in the middle of it and looked straight at the window, at her, and kept talking.

He was still looking at her, still talking. She let go. The sheet dropped back over the glass between them, out of her opening hand.
