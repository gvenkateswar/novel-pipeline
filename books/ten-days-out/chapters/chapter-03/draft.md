# Chapter Three: The Constable's Kitchen

Dane Reyes lost the argument about going back into Beth's house in under a minute, and won the one about Russ Whitcomb's.

He lost the first at the foot of her steps, gun in hand, with snow blowing flat across the yard.

"Wait in Russ's truck," he said. "I'll clear the house and bring out what you need."

She was already on the steps.

"If you walk in first and he's in there, how's that going to sound in a courtroom?"

"It's going to sound like I did it." She opened her own front door.

He got in ahead of her by one step, which was the most he was going to get.

"Kitchen's clear," he said, low. "Hall. Back room. Clear." He kept talking, because she'd stopped. She stood in her kitchen without a word, and after one night he already knew that quiet was how she got scared.

Snow had drifted across the floor under the window over the sink. The house was empty. He'd been sure it wouldn't be.

She packed fast: clothes into a canvas bag, the phone in its zip bag, her father's old sweater pulled on over her own.

He wanted her. He'd wanted her since she swung an oar at his head, and it was the least useful thing he could think in a house a man had broken into. He looked out the window instead.

The truck was clean. Everything Cole Varney's lawyer needed to make go away was standing in this kitchen, putting socks in a bag. Her eyes were the whole case.

And he'd been wrong at the shack. Nobody had given the man her name. The man had known the house and the hill and the window, like everybody on this island, and that was why she couldn't sleep here.

Below, the ferry's ramp hung out over the water, and the lobster boats lay on the rocks. No way off.

At the door she shouldered the bag. "I'll sleep on the ferry. My old bunk in the crew room."

"A boat tied to a pier has one way off it. He came by water last night. And the ferry's the first place anybody here would look for you. Russ has a woodstove, a shotgun and the only working generator on the harbor road, and every house on that road can see his porch. Let them look. Let them see Russ on it."

"Fine."

She carried the bag down the steps herself before he could offer. He followed with nothing in his hands, and he knew it.

* * *

The lights went out at a quarter past four.

Dane was at the kitchen window when the store went dark. Then the church. Then the harbor road, house after house, coming toward them, until the kitchen was black.

In the shed off the kitchen Russ hauled on the generator cord, three times, four.

"Choke, Russ," Beth said from the doorway. She didn't move.

"Is the choke off? It is." Another pull. "Who taught you that? Your father. Who taught your father? Mine, and he never got it right either."

The generator caught with a flat roar, and the bulbs came on.

Russ's was the only lit house on the harbor road now, and every dark window on it could see it. That was what Dane had asked for. He didn't like it.

Russ put his cap back on and took the shotgun out to the porch. "Who's coming up these steps past me? Not him." He sat with the gun across his knees where the whole road could see him.

Dane called his boss on speaker, with Beth right there.

"Dane."

"Russ Whitcomb's house, on the harbor road. He's on the porch with a shotgun. Generator, woodstove, one door at the back."

"Call me at six. The grand jury has to hear her by Monday morning, or the prosecutor says Cole walks."

"She's heard," Beth said.

She poured a mug of Russ's coffee and held it under her chin in both hands, steam on her face, eyes shut. It was the first hot thing she'd had since Thursday.

He couldn't stop watching her. He could still feel her arms around him on the pier this morning, and how he hadn't let go when she told him to. He wanted that again, and more, and he was supposed to be watching the road.

Out there Russ sat in the light, and every dark house on the road faced him. Dane stood at the window and saw what the road saw.

* * *

By ten Dane had turned the kitchen light off so nobody on the road could see in. The only light was the stove's, orange through the crack in its door, and Russ's coffee sat on the back of it turning to tar. The generator roared through the wall, loud enough that anything said in this kitchen would stay in it.

He stood at the edge of the window and watched the road.

Beth came and stood beside him, close. Her eyes were level with his. He didn't have to look down to fight with her, and he liked that more than he could afford to.

"That coffee," she said, "has been on the stove since the last election."

"I drank two cups."

"I saw. I thought about stopping you."

She was looking at his mouth. She didn't pretend otherwise. He wanted her so badly he'd stopped watching the window, and the window was his whole job.

"I'm going to kiss you," she said. "If you've got a reason why not, you've got about three seconds, and it had better be better than a jury."

He counted. He counted everything. He got to two.

She took a fistful of his shirt and kissed him.

He kissed her back hard. His hand went to her jaw, and her back hit Russ's counter, and she didn't care, and neither did he. She tasted of Russ's terrible coffee. Her braid was cold against his wrist and her mouth was hot, and she opened it under his and made a low sound in her throat that he felt down his spine. He slid his other hand under the hem of her father's sweater to her waist. Her skin was warm. She pressed into him, hips and stomach and all of her, and pulled him in by the shirt like a rope she was hauling. For once his head was quiet. Her fingers went into his hair. His thumb found the corner of her mouth. She bit his lower lip, and she wasn't gentle about it, and he heard himself make a sound he had never once made in front of a witness.

That was what stopped him.

He didn't go far. His forehead stayed on hers and his hand stayed on her face, and they breathed.

"The truck was clean," he said, "so you're the whole case, and if Cole's lawyer can stand up and tell twelve strangers that the marshal was sleeping with the only witness, those twelve people start wondering what else the marshal told her to say. And then Cole goes home."

He could already hear the man saying it, slowly, in a good coat, to twelve people from Maine.

"It's a jury," Beth said.

"It's a jury."

"Then don't lie about it." She still had his shirt in her fist. "If they ask, I say yes, and then I tell them what I saw. You think twelve people from Maine never heard of two grown adults in a snowstorm?"

"He'll still say it."

"Let him. I'll still say yes." Her thumb moved along his collarbone. "A jury doesn't get a vote on who kisses me. I do. I voted."

"I don't have an answer to that."

She was right about most of it, and the part she wasn't right about would come due in a courtroom some day, and he wanted her more than he had wanted anything in his life. That was going to be a problem.

"How many witnesses have kissed you?" she asked, as if she wanted the time.

"As of tonight? One. I'm keeping count."

She nearly laughed. He felt it under his hand.

"Tonight's a bad night," he said. "I'm on watch, there's a man out there somewhere, and Russ is ten feet away through that wall with a shotgun."

"Then tomorrow."

"Probably sooner."

* * *

At one in the morning the radio on Russ's porch rail crackled, loud.

Dane sat at the table with his back to the wall. Beth was asleep on the couch by the stove.

"Russ! The Dyer house just phoned the store. Says he's the Dyers' nephew, out in his own boat yesterday to check the house before the storm. Tree's through the roof. He's hurt and can't get his leg out."

"This is Russ Whitcomb," Russ said, as formal as church. "On my way."

Russ leaned in the door. "That's Tess. Who's Tess? The store. And in a storm she's the emergency director, which means she drives the one fire truck and runs this radio."

Beth sat up. "The Dyers have a nephew in Augusta."

A man might be under a tree, and Russ was the only one who could reach him, and Dane wasn't leaving Beth. A tree through a roof in this wind was the likeliest thing in the world. He hated it anyway.

Russ took the shotgun. "Twenty minutes." He went down the steps into the snow, and the porch was empty.

Dane talked, low and steady, about his father's bus route and Russ's coffee, because the house was too quiet with the porch empty. Beth didn't answer. She was listening.

And the generator, the only one on the harbor road, was roaring through the wall and covering every other sound on the island.

"Stop," Beth said.

He stopped. She was listening for something under the roar that he couldn't hear.

The window blew in.

A shot, and glass sprayed across the table. Dane was across the kitchen and had her off the couch and onto the floor before the second shot, his body between her and the window.

"Here's how this goes," he said into her hair. "You stay on the floor, I stay between you and the window, and you keep breathing where I can hear it. You don't have to talk. I'll talk. I'm very good at it, ask anybody in Massachusetts."

Beth said nothing. Her cheek was on the glass, eyes open.

More shots. Glass had opened his left forearm. He noticed it late.

He had his gun out and didn't fire. He couldn't see the man, and behind him was a road of houses full of sleeping people.

He'd do anything to keep this woman breathing where he could hear it, and none of that was the job.

The shots stopped. He counted the gap.

"Back door," he said. "When I say."

* * *

He said it, and they went.

The back door banged behind them, and the cold hit like another door. A shot came at the sound and split wood off the woodpile over their heads. They went down behind it in the snow, the split ends hard against his back.

Down the side of the house, in the spill of the porch light, a line dragged through the new snow under the kitchen window: one boot print and a drag. The man had walked right up and looked in before he fired.

The whole harbor road could see where she was, and Dane had made sure of it. And the call had come from the Dyer house, and Russ had gone.

Blood had come through his left sleeve, above the bruise she'd given him. Beth saw it.

"That's the arm I hit."

"It's my bad arm now. You've ruined it for everybody."

He started to tell her how this was going to go. She put her hand flat on his chest, and he stopped. His heart was going hard under her palm. She had kissed him three hours ago, and he'd follow her anywhere on this island, and that was a terrible thing for a marshal to know about himself.

"I know a place," she said.
