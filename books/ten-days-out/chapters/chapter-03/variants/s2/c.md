Ned was out by the side wall of the store with an urn cup, up from the boat. Dane had gone in ahead of her with Russ, and the bell over the door was still swinging.

"Bethie."

The cove was in the fish house over the sill, and it would be till Monday. She held out her hand. "I need the Ashby key, Ned."

He set the cup on the oil tank and worked a key off his ring with his thumbnail. "I run her every storm anyhow. Nobody'll look twice at a light." He laid it in her palm. "Nobody but—"

"Me and God. I know."

She went in with the key in her pocket.

Inside, the store generator roared out back, and then it didn't. The lights went, and the cooler's hum with them, and the whole store stopped talking at once.

"Mind the counter, Calloway." Tess was through the curtain before Beth had said she would, and the back door banged open on the wind behind her.

Beth went round behind the counter. The storm log lay open on it in Tess's hand, and the morning's store bag sat beside it waiting on its tag. Somebody had left a carton of milk by the till, and she picked it up to put away.

At the front window Dane stood with Russ and a cup of the urn coffee.

"Who on this island drags a left heel?"

Russ leaned on the window frame. "That narrows it to about forty of us. Where you sleeping tonight, Calloway? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

Nobody answered him. Tess wasn't there to.

The bell. A tall man stamped the snow off on the mat, clean-shaven, in a good jacket. Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, the dragger that had stayed lit end to end through the whole blackout.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having." He tipped his head at Dane's cup.

She found the Golds on the rack behind her. She didn't know what they cost, and when she turned round his money was already on the counter, the bills squared and the coins stacked on them.

From behind the curtain, over the clank of the panel: "Put that away, Lyle. It's on your account."

He left it where it was. "She's got you running the place." He looked round the store, friendly. "Long winter for a store, no boat. Hard one, this looks like."

Out back the generator coughed and caught. The lights came up and the cooler hummed.

The clock over the door stood at noon. Dane looked at it, then at Arsenault in front of her, and stayed where he was. *Saturday noon.* The call would have meant the yard, and he wasn't leaving her at a counter with the dragger's skipper.

Arsenault took the Golds, nodded to her, and went out on the bell. The money stayed.

She heard it first. Behind her, through the curtain, a boot on the office boards, and then the other foot after it, dragged, a long wet whisper of rubber on wood. The generator's roar came in louder with it. Tess had left the back door open. The step came again, and the drag, and then nothing but breath, slow, through a nose, close.

She turned her head.

He stood in the gap of the curtain with his hood up, an arm's length from her and from the open book. Snow had caught along the hood's edge and was going to water in the warm. She could smell him, bait and diesel and wet cotton, and under the hood she could see nothing but the line of a jaw, and his breath moving the dark there. His eyes were on the counter. On the book, or her hand by it, or on her. His own hands were in the front pocket of the sweatshirt, and the pocket held its shape.

She put the milk down hard on the counter.

Across the store Dane turned at the sound.

"Hey." Low, and already coming. "Stay right there, friend."

The hood didn't move. Her own hand lay on the counter a foot from Tess's writing, and she couldn't feel it.

"Talk to me." Dane was between the shelves, his voice running ahead of him, even. "Hands out where I can see them."

The man turned. He went back through the office without hurrying, and the keys for Tess's Ford swung on their hook as his shoulder went by. On the back step he went straight through Tess, coming in from the panel, and she went down on the ice by the propane cage.

Dane was past Beth, past Tess, and down the shore. Beth got to the back step with a hand under Tess's arm, and from there she could see the lane between two fish houses, and Dane in it, and the sea coming up it green, lifting the weed off the planks.

"Sea coming. Stand still."

He stood. He took the corner post of the near fish house in both arms, and the sea went through the lane to his knees and sucked back out past him. When it had gone, the man was up on a sill beyond, a step he knew, and over it, and gone.

Dane stayed in the lane where she'd put him, wet to the knee, his hair blown flat to his head.

She got Tess up and in. Going through the office she hung the keys straight on the hook, her knuckles in the charging cords, Ned's phone in its rubber band among the others. At the counter she held Tess's left hand up under the light. The heel of it was raw, with grit in it. Across the knuckles, smeared and plain, the time in ballpoint.

Tess took the hand back.

"Twenty-two forty." She was bright with it, shaking and furious, talking. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home. I can see your steps from right here, Beth. Didn't put you on the air." She jerked her chin at the back door. "So if I saw your light, who else did? I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one. You'll sleep at mine. Cot's made. Marshal can eat on the porch."

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

Tess took the store bag in the scraped hand and yanked the yellow tag through the zipper so hard it sang. "Your pay rides to Pruett in my bag every other week. Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color, and I won't have you in a house I can't see." She dropped the bag under the counter.

Beth's eyes had gone down the night's column.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

The back door banged. Dane came through the office with his jeans black to the knee, and he looked at Tess.

"He went between them like he'd built them. I went in like a man looking for the restroom."

Tess laughed.

He came across to the counter. Beth's palm went flat on the log, over the night's column, before she had decided anything. Her other hand went up and knocked the snow off his shoulder, and it stayed at his wet collar a beat longer than the snow needed. She hadn't given him Ned last night, and she wasn't giving him Tess now.

Tess, at the till, had both of Beth's hands in view.

"What am I looking at?"

"Nothing in it. Soup for the shut-ins and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk."

He picked the carton up from where she'd set it down and held it out. She reached for it with the hand from his collar, and his fingers were still coming off the cardboard when hers closed on it, and her palm stayed where it was on Tess's writing.
