Ned was by the store's side wall with an urn cup. Dane had gone in ahead with Russ, and the bell was still swinging.

"Bethie."

She held out her hand. "I need the Ashby key, Ned."

He worked a key off his ring with his thumbnail and put it in her hand. "I run her every storm anyhow. Nobody'll look twice at a light. Nobody but—"

"Me and God. I know."

She went in with the key in her pocket.

Inside, the generator out back quit, and the lights went with it.

"Mind the counter, Calloway." Tess was through the curtain, and the back door banged open on the wind behind her.

Beth went round behind the counter. The storm log lay open there in Tess's hand. She picked up a carton of milk somebody had left by the till.

Dane was at the front window with Russ and an urn coffee.

"Who on this island drags a left heel?"

Russ leaned on the window frame. "That narrows it to about forty of us. Where you sleeping tonight, Calloway? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

Nobody answered him. Tess wasn't there to.

The bell. A tall man in a good jacket, clean-shaven. Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, lit end to end all through the blackout.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having." He tipped his head at Dane's cup.

She found the Golds behind her. When she turned, his money was on the counter, counted, and she still didn't know the price.

From behind the curtain, over the clank of the panel: "Put that away, Lyle. It's on your account."

"She's got you running the place. Long winter for a store, no boat. Hard one, this looks like."

Out back the generator caught, and the lights came up.

The clock over the door stood at noon. Dane looked at it, then at Arsenault in front of her, and stayed where he was. *Saturday noon.* The call would have meant the yard, and he wasn't leaving her at a counter with the dragger's skipper.

Arsenault took the Golds, nodded to her, and went out on the bell. The money stayed.

She heard it first. Behind her, through the curtain, a boot on the office boards, and then the other foot after it, dragged, a long wet whisper of rubber on wood. The step came again, and the drag, and then nothing but breath, slow, through a nose, close.

She turned her head.

He stood in the gap of the curtain with his hood up, an arm's length from her and from the open book. She could smell him, bait and diesel and wet cotton, and under the hood she could see nothing but the line of a jaw, and his breath moving the dark there. He was looking at the counter, at the book or her hand by it, or at her. His hands were in his sweatshirt pocket, and the pocket held a shape.

She put the milk down hard on the counter.

Dane turned at the sound.

"Hey." Low, and already coming. "Stay right there, friend."

The hood didn't move. Her own hand lay on the counter a foot from Tess's writing, and she couldn't feel it.

"Talk to me." His voice came ahead of him between the shelves. "Hands out where I can see them."

The man turned. He went back through the office without hurrying, and the keys for Tess's Ford swung on their hook as he passed. On the back step he went through Tess, coming in from the panel, and she went down on the ice by the propane cage.

Dane went after him. From the back step, a hand under Tess's arm, Beth could see the lane between two fish houses, and Dane in it, and the sea coming up it green.

"Sea coming. Stand still."

He stood. He got both arms round the corner post of the near fish house, and the sea went through to his knees and sucked back out. When it had gone, the man was up on a sill he knew, and over it, and gone.

She got Tess in, hanging the keys straight on the hook as they passed, her knuckles in the charging cords among the phones, Ned's in its rubber band. At the counter she held Tess's scraped left hand under the light. Across the knuckles, smeared, the time in ballpoint.

Tess took the hand back.

"Twenty-two forty." Shaking, bright, furious. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home. I can see your steps from right here, Beth. Didn't put you on the air. So if I saw your light, who else did? I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one. You'll sleep at mine."

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

Tess took the store bag in the scraped hand and yanked the yellow tag through the zipper so hard it sang. "Your pay rides to Pruett in my bag. Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color, and I won't have you in a house I can't see."

Beth's eyes had gone down the night's column.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

The back door banged, and Dane came through the office black to the knee and looked at Tess.

"He went between them like he'd built them. I went in like a man looking for the restroom."

Tess laughed.

As he crossed to the counter, Beth's palm went flat on the log, over the night's column, before she had decided anything. Her other hand knocked the snow off his shoulder, and it stayed at his wet collar a beat longer than the snow needed. She hadn't given him Ned last night, and she wasn't giving him Tess now.

Tess, at the till, had both of Beth's hands in view.

"What am I looking at?"

"Nothing in it. Soup for the shut-ins and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk."

He picked the carton up and held it out. She took it with the hand from his collar, his fingers still coming off the cardboard, and her palm stayed where it was on Tess's writing.
