
Two cot blankets sat folded by the till, and Beth knew they'd been there since before she came in.

Dane zipped his jacket at the door. "I'll be on the dock with Russ," he said. "I can see this counter from there."

Russ leaned on the open door and let the snow in. "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

The bell rang the two of them out, and Tess waited for it to stop.

Then she held her left hand up across the counter, the time in ballpoint across the knuckles under a column of other people's gallons. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home. Somebody's light going on them last night, low, on and off, like it didn't want to be seen. Twenty-two forty." She turned the hand so Beth could read it. "I can see your steps from right here, Beth. I didn't put you on the air."

"I know you didn't."

"Then know the rest. If I can see them from my counter, so can anybody on that dock. So can anybody on a boat in this harbor with a pair of glasses." Tess came round the end of the counter and squared the blankets that were already square. "I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one. Cot's in my front room. Marshal can eat on the porch. You'll sleep at mine."

Past the frost on the front window, Dane stood at the dock rail with the harbor at his back, facing in.

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

Tess's mouth was open on the next thing when the bell went.

Arsenault came in stamping snow off good boots, and Dane came in behind him and took the end of the counter. Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, the dragger that had stayed lit through the blackout. He was shaved close, and the wind had reddened him where the razor had been.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having," Arsenault said.

"Coffee," Dane said.

Arsenault paid for both, and Tess's face came up bright as she rang it.

The clock over the door came round to noon. *Saturday noon*, the screen had said, and that was the Webbers Cove going to somebody else. Dane looked up at the clock, then at Arsenault beside her. The phone only worked outdoors, and outdoors meant leaving her at this counter with the dragger's skipper. Dane stayed where he was and drank the coffee Arsenault had bought him.

Arsenault took his cigarettes, nodded to her, said "Beth," and went out. Dane set the cup down and went after him to see which way he walked.

Tess had the morning's store bag on the counter before the door was shut. She yanked the yellow tag through the zipper so hard the bag jumped.

"Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color, and I won't have you in a house I can't see."

"I'm not your money, Tess."

"No. My money I can count." She held the sealed bag up between them. "I put you to bed the winter your mother got on the Bangor bus."

"Then you know I don't go where I'm put."

Beth's eyes went down the night's column in the open log by the till and stayed there.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

Tess took the bag through the curtain to the safe, and the curtain caught on its rod and hung open a hand's width behind her.

Through the gap Beth could see the office, Tess on one knee at the safe with her back to the window, and past her, through the back window, the propane cage on the step. A man in a hood stood beside the cage, facing in.

He turned away down the slope, and his left foot came after him through the snow a beat late.

Beth went to the front door and opened it on the wind. "Him," she said.

Dane came off the rail running, and Russ came behind him. "Russ, stay on her," Dane said going by, and he was through the curtain and out the back door.

Beth went through after him with Russ at her shoulder and stopped on the back step. Below it the shore fell away to the fish houses on their tarred posts, with the top of the tide in among them. The hood went down the lane between two of them, quick and sure of every plank. Dane went in after him running, and out past the end of the lane the sea heaped up green on the ledge.

"Sea coming," Beth called down. "Stand still."

He stopped. He got an arm round a corner post, and the sea came up the lane white and went through to his knees and sucked back out at him, all of it pulling for the harbor. The man stepped up onto a sill he knew and was gone through a door.

Dane stood in the lane where she'd put him, his jeans black to the knee and both arms round the post, his shoulders going up and down under the wet jacket.

Russ leaned on the office doorframe in the generator's roar, where he could see the lane and the counter both. "Think he'll come back up this way? I don't," he said.

Tess was out at the counter again when the back door banged. Dane came through the curtain with snow on his shoulders and the sea running out of his boots, breathing hard.

Dane looked down at the water spreading from his boots across her boards. "I owe you a floor, Ms. Marr. Put it on the government's account."

"Get a look at him? Didn't, did you," Russ said.

"He went in between them like he'd built them," Dane said. "I went in like a man looking for the restroom."

Tess laughed. "Restroom's for customers."

Beth put her palm flat on the open log, over the night's column. With the other hand she knocked the snow off his shoulder, and then she turned his wet collar down and smoothed it against his neck. She hadn't given him Ned last night, and she wasn't giving him Tess now.

"What am I looking at?" Dane said, his eyes on her hand.

"Nothing in it. Soup for the shut-ins and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk," Beth said.

He handed her the milk.

Beth and Dane went out the front together with the bell behind them, round to the side wall out of the wind. Russ stayed in by the stove. Ned was at the wall with a paper cup from the urn steaming in his hand, and Dane kept the corner, where he could see her and the moorings.

"I need the Ashby key, Ned," Beth said.

"Bethie." Ned set the cup on the sill and worked a key off his ring with his thumb. "I run her generator every storm anyhow. Nobody'll look twice at a light." He put the key in her glove.

"Nobody but—" Ned said.

"Me and God. I know," Beth said.

She came round the front of the store with the key in her glove. Through the window Tess took the two folded cot blankets off the counter and put them back under it, one and then the other. She didn't look up.
