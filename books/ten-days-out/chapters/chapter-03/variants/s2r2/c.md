
Two cot blankets sat folded by the till, and Beth knew they'd been there since before she came in.

Dane stopped at the door with Russ. "I'll be on the dock," he said. "I can see this counter from there."

Russ leaned on the door and let the snow in. "Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

The bell rang them out, and Tess waited for it to stop.

Then she held her left hand up across the counter, the time in ballpoint on the knuckles. "Net had you aboard. Your back steps had you home, and a light going on them. Twenty-two forty. I can see your steps from right here, Beth. I didn't put you on the air."

"I know you didn't."

"Then know this. If I can see them from my counter, so can anybody on that dock, or on any boat in this harbor." She squared the blankets, which were square. "I walked you home from every dance on this island, and I'll walk you home from this one. Cot's in my front room. You'll sleep at mine."

Out on the dock, Dane stood at the rail facing in.

"Can't. Not tonight, Tess."

Tess's mouth was open on the next thing when the bell went.

Arsenault came in stamping snow off good boots, Dane behind him to the end of the counter. Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*, the dragger that had stayed lit all through the blackout.

"Pack of Golds, and whatever the gentleman's having," Arsenault said.

"Coffee," Dane said.

Arsenault paid for both, and Tess's face came up bright as she rang it.

The clock over the door came round to noon, and *Saturday noon* had been the Webbers Cove. Dane looked at the clock, then at Arsenault. The phone worked only outdoors, and outdoors meant leaving her at this counter with the dragger's skipper. He stayed, and drank the coffee Arsenault had bought him.

Arsenault took his cigarettes, nodded to her, said "Beth," and went out. Dane went after him to see which way he walked.

Tess had the store bag on the counter before the door shut. She yanked the yellow tag through the zipper so hard the bag jumped.

"Yellow's mine. Red's the boat's. I won't have my money in Ned's color, and I won't have you in a house I can't see."

"I'm not your money, Tess."

"No. My money I can count." She held the sealed bag up between them. "I put you to bed the winter your mother got on the Bangor bus."

"Then you know I don't go where I'm put."

Beth's eyes went down the night's column in the log and stayed there.

"Don't you look at my book like the bank does."

Tess took the bag through the curtain to the safe, and the curtain caught on its rod and hung open behind her.

Through the gap, past Tess on one knee at the safe, Beth could see the office's back window and the propane cage on the step outside it. A man in a hood stood beside the cage, facing in.

He turned away down the slope, and his left foot came after him through the snow a beat late.

Beth went to the front door and opened it. "Him," she said.

Dane came off the rail running, Russ behind him. "Russ, stay on her," Dane said going by, and he was through the curtain and out the back.

Beth went as far as the back step, Russ at her shoulder. Below it the fish houses stood on their tarred posts with the top of the tide in among them. The hood went down the lane between two of them, sure of every plank. Dane went in after him, and past the end of the lane the sea heaped up green on the ledge.

"Sea coming," Beth called down. "Stand still."

He stopped. He got an arm round a corner post, and the sea came up the lane white and went through to his knees and sucked back out at him, pulling for the harbor. The man stepped up onto a sill he knew and was gone through a door.

Dane stood in the lane where she'd put him, jeans black to the knee, both arms round the post.

Russ took the office doorway in the generator's roar, where he could see the lane and the counter both.

Tess was back at the counter when the back door banged and Dane came through the curtain, breathing hard, the sea running out of his boots.

Dane looked down at the water spreading across her boards. "I owe you a floor, Ms. Marr. Put it on the government's account."

"Get a look at him? Didn't, did you," Russ said.

"He went in between them like he'd built them," Dane said. "I went in like a man looking for the restroom."

Tess laughed. "Restroom's for customers."

Beth put her palm flat on the open log, over the night's column. With the other hand she knocked the snow off his shoulder and turned his wet collar down against his neck. She hadn't given him Ned last night, and she wasn't giving him Tess now.

"What am I looking at?" Dane said, his eyes on her hand.

"Nothing in it. Soup for the shut-ins and who's short on propane. Hand me the milk," Beth said.

He handed her the milk.

Beth and Dane went out the front with the bell behind them and round to the side wall, out of the wind. Russ stayed in by the stove. Ned was at the wall with a cup from the urn, and Dane kept the corner, where he had her and the moorings both.

"I need the Ashby key, Ned," Beth said.

"Bethie." Ned worked a key off his ring with his thumb. "I run her generator every storm anyhow. Nobody'll look twice at a light." He put the key in her glove.

"Nobody but—" Ned said.

"Me and God. I know," Beth said.

She came round the front of the store with the key in her glove. Through the window Tess took the two folded cot blankets off the counter and put them back under it, one and then the other. She didn't look up.
