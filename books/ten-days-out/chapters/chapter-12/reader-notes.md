## Where I got bored

- **"He had waited for light because the dark was the kid's, and taken the road because Ned couldn't do the shore rocks tied. Ned's lantern had come down this road last night for anybody in these fields to see."** This is the book defending its logistics to me. Nobody asked why they took the road. It sits between Ned's first real answer and Beth's next question and lets the air out.
- **Russ's confession:** "I told her the north-side house. I went to her after the Dyer house. The phone she hung up on Saturday, that was me. Tuesday I kept the harbor road like I said, because every house on it stood empty, and was I leaving hers? I was not." I had to read it twice to sort out what he was confessing to and when. I got the gist (he's with Tess, so that's where he was "late," and that's who he told), but this is a checklist of old clues being ticked off one by one.
- **The afternoon in the town office.** "All afternoon he stayed on the far side of the room…" The summary itself is fine. But the scene before it was a rerun of an argument I've already heard, so I drifted until Beth went out on the steps.

## Where I got confused

- **"I stood in the cabin with the door shut so you wouldn't hear."** In Chapter 1, Beth climbed straight to the cabin after the crate split, told Ned, and then "stayed up there beside him for the rest of the crossing… Neither of them said a word." So when did Ned make a call behind a shut door that she couldn't hear? Also, Cole Varney was driving the truck, sitting twenty feet below the cabin with the dome light on. Why phone Cole when Cole was on the boat and had seen it all? This is the key reveal of the chapter, it doesn't fit what I remember of the first scene of the book, and it bothered me.
- **The knife.** Chapter 11 ended with Ned's rope knife coming "out over Beth" while he thought she was the hurt boy. Chapter 12 never says what the knife was for. Was he cutting a bandage, or was he there to "finish it," as the lawyer's text puts it? The text shows up here ("go finish it") and makes the knife look worse, yet nobody, not Beth, not Dane, asks him. If the book means to leave that open, it didn't feel deliberate. It felt forgotten.
- **"every house on it stood empty."** Why was the whole harbor road empty on Tuesday? Had everyone gone to the beach, or to the fire? I don't know.
- **"the kid isnt answering ned. your son is. go finish it."** On the page, "your son is" reads first as a fragment ("your son is…"). It took a beat to parse it as "your son is answering." Dane's gloss helps, but Beth's reply "He means me" also took me a moment. I think "finish it" means kill Beth, which makes the unasked knife question worse.
- **The phone pockets.** "Her eyes went to the left pocket of Dane's coat. That phone…" This depends on me remembering Chapter 11's inventory (knife right, Ned's phone left, kid's phone over his heart). I did, barely. Then the knife comes "from his right coat pocket," and I could follow because of that inventory, but it felt like bookkeeping.
- **Cole's lawyer.** "That's him. Cole's lawyer." Until now the lawyer existed only in Dane's head, a man "in a good coat" talking to a jury. Now he's a real person who threatens sons and can find a street address in a morning. It's a good turn, but it went by fast and I'm not sure whether he's a new villain or just an errand boy.

## Where I stopped believing

- **The son rescued in eleven minutes.** One call to a Portland desk, and a car pulls a threatened man off a street, alive, inside the time it takes to boil water. The book built Ned's whole betrayal on this threat, then removed it with a phone call. Too easy, and it left me nothing to feel for Ned's boy.
- **"She'll go."** Dane has known since Chapter 7 that "she would never leave this island. He had known that since the oar." In Chapter 3 he told her, "You're right about the vote. It's yours." In Chapter 9 he was secretly working out whether he could transfer to Maine. Then he tells his boss she'll go, with her standing three feet away. He owns it ("I said it because I mean to make you go"), so the book knows. But it's the one move that most cleanly produces a fight, and it's the opposite of what this man has done for eleven chapters. It felt like the breakup came first and the motive was built after it.
- **The breakup itself, "Then they stop. And so do we."** Neither of them brings up the obvious thing the book already planted: he was thinking about staying in Maine. One sentence of honest talk ("I asked about Portland") would change the fight. Two people this blunt with each other wouldn't let it end on one exchange.
- **Questions during the ambush.** Beth asks "Why was my picture on that wall in September?" from the ditch, seconds after a shot. Ned answers, then Dane interrogates him about the supply chain ("Where does Cole get it?"). The kid had run off, so it's possible, but it reads as the book clearing questions while it has everyone in one place.
- **"You've owned the one lie that put you on my list."** Nobody talks like this, least of all Dane, who until now has been the funny one.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Longing beats on a timer.** "Dane was making a desk in Portland do what he said, for the man who had sold her, and Beth wanted to cross the room and put her mouth on his while he was still talking." Then a few pages later: "she wanted both fists in his coat and the desk gone from between them." Each scene gets its quota of wanting, in the same "she wanted [body part] [on/in] [him]" shape the book has used since Chapter 2 ("She wanted them on her").
- **The narrator explaining the plot to me.** "So the police had only her word, and that was why a boy with a gun had hunted her for six days." Also "So he'd know who to buy next." These are the summary sentences an outline would hold.
- **Recycled phrasing that has become a tic:**
  - "politely and slowly" / "I'll say why once, slowly": Dane's tic from Chapters 4 and 5, used twice here.
  - "Russ took his cap off and put it back on": the fourth or fifth time in the book.
  - "hand flat" on something: "Dane's hand came flat on her back," "hand flat on the daybed," and so on. It's everywhere across the book.
  - "eleven minutes": the boss took eleven minutes in Chapter 11, the son takes eleven minutes here, and Beth has had eleven years on the deck.
  - "Two years ago in New Bedford, Cole's people burned down the house of a woman who'd testified": nearly word for word from Chapter 9.
  - Beth's argument, "then shooting at me works, and every one of them learns it," is Chapter 2's "Cole's people learn that kicking in a witness's door works" with the verb changed.
- **"I'd lose you to keep you alive."** It's the kind of line on the movie poster, said aloud.
- **Neat closing image:** "the two of them looked at it together, a step apart." The smoke is earned and chilling. The "a step apart" tag that sums up the relationship is the book tying a bow.
- **The boss** still has no name after twelve chapters and speaks only in clipped, functional lines. She sounds like a plot device on speakerphone.

## What I want to know next

- **Who is in my house?** Smoke from Beth's own chimney, with Russ's truck at the foot of the lane all day. Did the kid get past Russ, or was he in there before Russ arrived? Is Russ all right down there? This is the hook, and it's strong.
- **What was the knife for?** Did Ned walk an hour in a storm to save the boy or to kill him? Since the "go finish it" text, I need this answered.
- **Will Beth take WITSEC?** I'm sure she won't. So the real question is what the book offers instead, and whether Dane gives up his job, his transfer, or the boat.
- **The love declaration.** "Ask me in five days" in Chapter 8, "Don't say it yet" in Chapter 10. The clock is running, and now they've broken up without either of them saying it. I want that paid off.
- **The ferry.** "It's my ferry, once I fix the steering." That's the only way off the island, and she has the captain's license she's never used. I fully expect her to drive herself to Portland.
- **The offshore boat** and Cole's lawyer: these look like the series hook more than the climax of this book.

### Prediction

1. The kid is holed up in her father's house, wounded. Beth goes up the hill herself, probably against Dane's wishes, and the scene turns on "nobody on this island lets a boy bleed to death in the snow." The kid is taken alive, possibly because Beth talks him down or treats his wound. He finally gets a name.
2. Beth repairs the ferry's steering and takes the wheel herself to get to Portland for the Monday grand jury. Ned's job is effectively hers already.
3. She testifies, Cole is indicted, and she refuses WITSEC. Dane resolves it by staying in Maine (the Portland-to-Pruett drive he was working out), maybe with the wooden boat that "needs everything." "Ask me in five days" gets its answer at or after the grand jury.
4. The lawyer is arrested, or at least exposed through Ned's phone. The offshore supplier stays open for Book 2.

How confident am I? High on 2 and 3, which is a problem: the romance's ending is visible from here. The suspense now rides on the kid and the house, and on whether the knife question has a darker answer.

## Listener pass (as heard through text-to-speech)

- **The opening sentence works aloud.** "Beth had held the end of the rope for a mile of the east road before Ned answered her" is clear and holds the ear.
- **"He had waited for light…"** follows Ned's dialogue directly, and the "he" means Dane. By ear I assumed Ned for half a sentence.
- **The texts read aloud are rough.** TTS reads "the kid isnt answering ned. your son is. go finish it." flat, and "your son is" lands as an unfinished sentence. "isnt" may be read oddly. A listener will need Dane's explanation, and gets it.
- **"Late." / "Later than that."** works only if the listener remembers Russ's one-word answer from Chapter 4. In audio, with hours between chapters, I think most will miss it and just hear two odd words.
- **The boss's two paragraphs** ("After she testifies…" and "If she says no, our part ends after Monday. Will she take it?") have no tags between them. By ear, the second sounds like it could be Dane.
- **Russ's confession paragraph** is the hardest stretch to follow by ear: four confessions in a row, with day names, and no breath between them.
- **The pocket bookkeeping** (left pocket, right pocket) is nearly impossible to track in audio.
- **The final paragraph** reads beautifully aloud: "The smoke went up pale into the blue, the truck sat small and dark beneath it…" It's the moment a listener sits up.
- On the page only: "his face grey from no sleep." The rest of the book spells it "gray."

## Best moment

> Dane put Ned's knife from his right coat pocket in her hand. She cut her own knot, and Russ's cuffs closed on the wrists where the rope had been.
>
> In the town office doorway Ned turned once and looked back at Beth. His hands were cuffed, so he nodded. Then he went in.

Nobody explains it, and nobody says a feeling. She cuts the knot he taught her with his own knife, and "His hands were cuffed, so he nodded" does more for Ned than his whole confession. The runner-up is the last image, her own chimney smoking with Russ's truck small and dark below it, which turns the entire chapter into a setup for the next one in one quiet sentence.
