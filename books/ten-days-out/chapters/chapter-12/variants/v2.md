# Chapter Twelve: The Hold

Beth Calloway held the end of the rope for a mile of the east road before Ned answered her.

"Snow's set in for the day," he said.

Nobody had plowed. At the mouth of the dentist's lane the new snow came to the knee, and it squeaked under three sets of boots. On the uphill side an old stone wall ran across the top of a white field. On the downhill side a shallow ditch lay half full. Dane walked a step wide on the uphill side with the kid's rifle on his shoulder.

"Two years ago Cole came down the pier on a still night," Ned said. "He said, after your deckhand's gone home, you take it back across alone. So I did. Two years I ran his trucks over in the dark. He gave me a phone for it. Only number he ever called me on."

The rope was stiff with cold in her bare hand. Beth stopped on it.

"On the beach you told me you'd never opened a truck."

"On the beach I lied to you." He looked at the snow in front of his boots. "Fuel runs, I told the co-op. Over for fuel and back, twice a month, after dark when the pumps are quiet. Nobody asks the captain why he moves his own boat."

She walked again.

"A man rang me on that phone the morning after," Ned said. "He told me the street my boy sleeps on."

The rope went slack in her hand. Then she walked on, and it jerked at every step his bad hip took.

"Why was the truck clean?"

"I called Cole when we tied up at Pruett, while you were on the ramp taking your picture of his plate. I told him you'd call. I know you."

"Why my Thursday?"

"The blow was coming. No night run till it passed, and Cole wouldn't wait on it." His breath went out white. "He said you never open a truck."

Eleven years she had never opened a truck. Eleven years of minding her own deck, and that was what got her chosen.

"Did you know he'd send somebody to my door?"

"Cole said nobody'd be touched. I didn't ask him twice."

Beth stopped walking.

Dane had stopped already. He stood with his head up toward the wall, and she looked at him before she looked at it.

She listened. Wind. Snow ticking on her hat. In a storm the island always made some sound. Up by the wall it made none.

The crack came off the wall. Snow jumped off the road ahead of Ned.

Dane had her by the coat and down in the ditch before the sound finished. Ned came in after her, hard on the bad hip with his hands tied, and rolled until his whole back lay between her and the field.

"Get off me." She shoved him with both hands.

"Ned." Dane was up on one knee at the lip of the ditch. "He's aiming at her. You're in the way of me."

He fired once at the wall.

A shape broke off the far end, bent double, and ran west along the stones toward the village. The bad foot plowed a line behind it. Dane's finger came off the trigger and rested along the guard. The first houses stood past the wall, and he didn't shoot toward houses. He didn't go after the boy either. He had a witness and a tied man in a ditch.

Snow had gone down Beth's collar. She didn't dig it out.

"Why was my picture on that wall in September?"

Ned spoke into the snow. "In September Cole asked me who'd have the boat after me. I told him you. I was proud of it."

"Where does Cole get it?" Dane said, without turning his head.

Ned answered Beth. "It comes in from offshore. A boat. I never saw it." He got his good knee under him and his tied hands on the lip of the ditch. "So I came to stand where he'd have to shoot me first."

He had done it again a minute ago, and she had shoved him off for it.

They climbed out. At the top of the rise Dane's phone found one bar, and he rang Russ. Put it on the island radio. Every door locked. A boy with a pistol coming in from the east, and nobody goes out to help a hurt boy. He said nothing about her.

His hand came onto her back, once, between her shoulders, and went.

Below them on the harbor road a door shut. Then another. Then a bolt went home, loud in the cold.

* * *

Tess's door opened as they came along the harbor road, under a porch light still burning from the night. Every other door stayed shut.

Russ came out in his shirt sleeves in the snow, with his shotgun and his radio. Tess stood behind him in the doorway in his brown work jacket, the cuffs pulled down over her fingers.

Everybody stopped.

"Late," Beth said.

"Later than that," Russ said.

"Not one word, Calloway."

Russ turned to Dane, and his ears went red. "You asked me who I told you were sleeping at my place. I told her. Friday night, from my porch, before the shooting. Saturday at the store, when her phone buzzed and she hung up on it, that was me telling her I'd found you alive. Tuesday I kept to the harbor road because her store's on it. Did I lie to you to keep her name out of it? I did."

Tess stood in his coat in her open door, where every curtain on the road could see her. Beth looked at the sleeves, then at the snow. Her own sleeve was against Dane's. She didn't move it.

"You've owned the one lie that put you on my list," Dane said. "Take him."

Behind them Tess's door shut.

At the town office step Russ took his cuffs off his belt. Beth held out the end of the rope, and Russ put his hand on it. She held on a breath longer. Then she let go of her end.

Dane took Ned's knife out of his coat pocket, put it in her hand and closed her fingers on it with his own, a second longer than it took. She cut her own knot. Russ's cuffs closed where the rope had been.

"Take the truck and the shotgun to the foot of the Calloway lane," Dane told Russ. "A hurt boy goes to ground he knows."

In the town office doorway Ned turned once and looked back at Beth. His hands were cuffed, so he nodded. Then he went in.

* * *

Dane turned the clerk's chair to the stove. "Sit."

"That's mine," Beth said. "I say that."

"I'm borrowing it, because I spent a long time on that knee last night, and you've stood on it all morning like it owes you money."

She sat. "Once."

"Once is how everything starts with you."

The stove ticked. Through the open back-room door Ned sat against the wall in Russ's cuffs, the rope burn red above them.

Ned's cheap phone buzzed on the desk and rattled against the kid's phone. Dane picked it up and turned the screen to her.

*the kid isnt answering ned. your son is. go finish it.*

Ned came up off the back-room floor faster than his hip should have let him.

"Let me answer him. Let me tell him it's done. He'll leave my boy be."

Beth got to the doorway first, on the bad knee. She put both hands on his chest and walked him back down the wall. The green wool was still wet from the road. It was the first time she had touched him since the beach.

"Sit down, Ned."

He sat.

Dane already had his own phone at his ear. On her way back she passed behind him at the window, and he moved his elbow in to let her by. There was room without it.

While it rang he held the kid's phone beside Ned's. The number that had called the kid four times had rung Ned once, the morning after.

"That's him," Ned said from the floor. "Cole's lawyer."

Dane gave Portland police his name and his badge and asked for a car to an address, now.

"Ned. The address."

"Ninety-six Grant Street. Second floor, the door round the back."

Dane was making a desk in Portland do what he said, for the man who had sold her, and Beth wanted to cross the room and put her mouth on his while he was still talking. She stayed by the stove.

Ned's thumbs worked at the rope burn, one wrist and then the other. He never looked at the phone. He looked at Beth.

The stove ticked.

Dane's phone rang. He listened a long time.

"They found him," he said. "He'd used. He's breathing. They've got him in an ambulance."

Ned bent over his cuffed hands until his forehead was on his knees, and stayed there. Beth pulled the back-room door half shut and left her hand on it.

* * *

Dane Reyes gave his boss the arrest, the number and the son with Beth three feet away, and the phone on the window sill went quiet so long he checked the screen.

"A lawyer who can find a man's son in a morning can find a woman on an island," the boss said.

He had known since the ambulance that she would say it, and it still went in under his ribs. Her voice came out small and even and filled a front room built for one clerk.

"After she testifies there's witness protection. A new name in a new state. She can't come back here, and she can't tell anyone from here where she is. Not her friends, not this island, and not you. Reyes, ask her."

He opened his mouth to do it.

"She's here," Beth said. "She's been here the whole call, and she can hear you fine. No."

"Then our part ends after Monday. Call me back, Reyes." The line went dead.

Dane took the phone off the sill and stayed at the window with his back to her. He could ask her again, and he knew how that went, because he had asked her on the tower and she had made the calls, and he had asked her on the cove rocks to stay behind him and she had gone off the rock with a pistol pointed at her, and last night he had asked her into the back bedroom and she had lain down under a quilt for whoever came through the door. She had been right every time. She had paid every time, with her own life, three times in four days, and it had come back to her by about a yard. For four years his whole method had been to ask and then live with the answer. He could already hear how this would sound to whoever judged it, and for once the judge was standing behind him at the stove, and she would be right about this too.

His thumb moved on the screen. Whole sentences, with periods.

*Ask the prosecutor to ask the judge Monday to hold her in Portland under guard after she testifies, until the trial or until she signs. My word: if she goes home, they kill her.*

He sent it. Then he crossed the room, close enough to smell stove smoke in her braid, and held the screen out to her. Nine years of the job had taught him to tilt it away.

"After you testify Monday," he said, "the prosecutor asks the judge to keep you in Portland under guard until the trial, or until you sign."

"Cancel it."

"No. You said no to the one thing that keeps you breathing. This one doesn't need your yes. You'd have heard it from a judge on Monday. You hear it from me."

She didn't step back from him. He watched that make her angrier than the text had.

The phone rang in his hand. He set it on the sill on speaker.

"The prosecutor's agreed to ask. Two o'clock Monday."

"Can you stop it?" Beth said to the phone.

"It rests on my deputy's word that you'll be killed if you go home. Only his word takes it back. I don't overrule the man on the ground."

Beth looked at Dane. "I could tell her I won't go in that room Monday till it's gone."

He waited. It was the one move that beat him, and she had found it in about a second, and he would have made it himself in her place and called it fair.

"I won't. I'm going in that room. You don't get to make me trade it."

She took her coat off the hook.

"Where?"

"My boat's broken."

He couldn't leave a prisoner, and he couldn't leave her. He went into the back room, hauled Ned up off the floor by the arm and got him into his coat one-handed, the rifle sliding on his shoulder.

"Walk where you like," he said to her back. "I'm paid to."

She already had the door open. Snow blew in across the boards, and she was gone into it. Dane went after her with Ned on his arm.

"Reyes?" the phone said to the room.

His thumb ended the call as he passed the sill.

* * *

Dane Reyes moved one of Russ's cuffs off Ned's wrist and closed it round the car-deck rail, where he could see Ned from the steering hatch.

Ned looked at the sky first. "Wind's going round west by dark."

Snow lay on the steel. The lumber truck and the bait truck sat chained where they had stuck, and the ferry knocked against the bare stone where the float used to be. Across the harbor the east rise stood white over the roofs, her house at the top of it.

Beth threw the hatch open at the back of the deck and went down the ladder on the bad knee with the ferry's big wrench.

"I'll hold the light," Dane said.

"Don't hold the light."

He stood at the hatch mouth with the rifle, his back to the trucks, and watched the pier and the road. Down in the cold a rod ran from the wheel back to the rudder. The first storm had bent it and pulled it off the rudder, she said, and the pin at the top was frozen. She hit the pin with the wrench, and the sound rang through the whole hull. She hit it again. Then a third time.

"Is she always like this down a hatch?" Dane asked the rail.

"Snow's letting up," Ned said. Then: "Since she was nine."

Her gloves were off down there, of course, and her hands were red to the wrist on the iron, and they knew where to hit and how hard, and he had been watching them for some time when she looked up and caught him. He looked back at the pier. Too late, by her face. He could have stood at this hatch the rest of his life watching her hands, and he had just made sure he wouldn't.

"Ms. Calloway." He said it down into the hatch, slow and polite. "In New Bedford they burned the house of a woman who had already testified. Testifying ends nothing. On Tuesday a pistol came up at you on the cove rocks. This morning a boy shot at you off a wall."

The wrench stopped.

"The man who loved you most on this island sold you for his son, and the next one will sell you for less."

At the rail Ned looked at the sea and said nothing.

"I wanted to tell your people five," Dane said. "My boss wanted her test, and you wanted them told the truth by you, and a man got shot in the shoulder at three. Every time I've asked you, it was your life you spent."

Beth came up the ladder to her waist and set the wrench on the pin from there.

"That's Cole winning, Dane Reyes. Slower." She leaned on the wrench. "If I leave my island because a boy shot at me, then shooting at me works, and every one of them learns it."

"I know."

"You're right about New Bedford. And the wall, and Ned. And the beach. I wanted them told, and a man paid for it." The wrench slipped. She set it again. "It's still my life to spend."

The pin let go with a crack. She worked the rod free and held it up into the light, bent along its length.

"Co-op vise in the morning," she said, the way she'd give the tide.

"I'd lose you to keep you alive." He put no joke after it.

"You will."

"I know. It's the one thing my job can still do for you, and I've done it."

She reached up. The bad knee wouldn't take the last rung with the rod in her other hand. He gave her his hand, and she came up out of the hatch on it. At the top she kept it. Her palm was cold and steady, the old rope scar under his thumb.

"Then they stop," she said. "And so do we."

She held on a breath past it. Then she let go.

He took it standing. "I'm still your guard until Monday."

"Then stand there."

She sat on the hatch edge with the bent rod across her knees and put the wrench to the next bolt. He stood there. The wrench rang under him every few minutes, and he watched the pier and the road and the rise, and the chain on Ned's wrist moved on the rail.

The light went blue. Ned was facing the east rise, and Dane saw his head come up before anything else moved.

"Wind's backed west," Ned said. Then: "Somebody's lit your stove."

Up on the rise, over the roofs, smoke was rising from her chimney into the last of the light. At the foot of her lane Russ's truck sat where it had sat all day. Her house had stood empty and cold for five days.

Dane's rifle came off his shoulder. Beth was on her feet with the bent rod still in her hand, and the three of them looked up at the smoke. Ned's cuff chain ticked against the rail in the wind.
