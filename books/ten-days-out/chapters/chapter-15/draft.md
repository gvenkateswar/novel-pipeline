# Chapter Fifteen: Eleven Pounds

Dane Reyes was trying to fit an eleven-pound ham into the town-office oven when Beth gave him her condition.

"You ask me. Every time. Even when you're sure. And you never tell anybody I said yes when I didn't."

He stood there with the ham in both hands. It was cold and slick under the glaze, and the kitchen smelled of cloves and brown sugar, and the rack was in the way. He understood her all at once, and it ran through him: it was Wednesday she meant, his own voice saying "She'll go" on the speaker with her three feet off, and it was the terms for something, and she hadn't said what. He was holding eleven pounds of pig and he couldn't put it down.

She had come straight in from the phone on the front-room desk. The ferry board had given her the boat for tonight. She'd asked them for it herself. It was the first thing he had ever seen her ask for out loud and get.

"Give it here," she said. "I'll show you."

She reached for it and couldn't hold it. Her palms were taped in white, and she held them a little away from her. She had spent all of Saturday at the co-op's vise with the steering rod, straightening it again with those hands while he held the light. Today her hands were paying for it.

He kept the ham. He took her terms one at a time, as seriously as terms from a judge.

"Even when I'm sure?"

"Mostly then."

"Yes," he said. "To all of it."

Then she said the part he hadn't seen coming.

"On Thursday I told you I want you. I meant it, and I'm still not going. On Wednesday I said so do we. I decided that for both of us, in that room. Same thing I hated you for."

He didn't let her carry all of it. "I was right about what's out there."

His eyes went to the window over the sink. Down the harbor, past the white water, lay the point. He only looked.

"You were," Beth said. "It's still mine to say." She drew a breath. "So that part's off too."

He still thought the program would keep her breathing. She still wasn't going into it. He had promised her the rest on the other side. They were still on this one.

He looked at her mouth. He thought about kissing her here, against the stove, in a kitchen that smelled of cloves. He didn't ask. Under her rule an ask had to mean it, and Russ was snoring on the couch ten feet off through the open door.

He tried the ham again. It hit the rack and stopped.

"That is not going in that oven," he said.

"A dollar says it does."

He stared at her. She had never once taken his bet. Now she was making her own, with his words.

Beth worked a dollar out of her coat pocket with two taped fingers and set it on the counter beside the oven.

* * *

Tess came in the front door with a roasting pan under one arm and the shotgun in her other hand. Beth held out her wrists for the pan. Tess walked past them and gave it to Dane.

She sat at the front-room desk with the shotgun across her knees.

Russ was awake now, his leg up on two cushions, his cap on.

"Take the rack out," Beth said.

Dane took the rack out. The oven breathed heat into his face.

"Bone end first."

He turned it bone end first.

"Lower. Turn it. There."

He did exactly that and nothing else.

"Is it going in?" Russ said. "It's not going in."

The ham slid in with a soft scrape.

"It's going in," Russ said.

Dane shut the door on it, with a quarter inch to spare. He put a dollar of his own with hers and put both in her taped hand. She folded them into her coat pocket and buttoned it over them.

"The ham is eleven pounds," Dane said, "and there are three of us."

"Four," said Russ.

"Tess doesn't count," Beth said. "She's not talking."

Tess didn't look up.

Dane cracked the window over the sink against the oven heat. The daylight outside was going blue. Down the harbor, past the white water at the mouth, the big boat lay off the point out of the wind, showing its one light, where it had lain since dawn on Saturday. He shut the window and said nothing about it in this room.

Beth stood in the doorway between the kitchen and the front room, where Russ and Tess could both see her.

"Russ. Tess. He's going to ask me something, and I'm going to say yes. I'm telling you myself, so nobody ever says it for me."

Dane stood there with the oven mitt still on one hand. He had planned nothing for this afternoon beyond the ham. She had just done in a doorway what he'd done on a speaker on Wednesday, the right way round.

"You just told them the answer," Dane said.

"Ask anyway." She turned to him. "Even when you're sure."

He took the mitt off. His voice came out lower than he meant.

"Can I kiss you? I'm asking."

"Yes. Come here."

He went. She couldn't put her palms on him, so she hooked her wrists over his shoulders and held her taped hands up behind his neck. He kept his own hands at her waist, careful of hers. The oven was hot at his back, and Russ's shirt rode up his arms when she drew him in. She kissed him slowly, after four days of not, in front of a man she had known since she was six, and she didn't hurry any of it. Her mouth was warm and tasted of coffee. He stopped thinking in sentences. He thought her name, and her mouth, and nothing else.

Tess stood, put the shotgun under her arm, and walked out the front door onto the steps without a word. The door shut behind her.

Beth watched it shut over his shoulder. She didn't let go of him.

From the couch Russ said, "Well."

* * *

Dane Reyes lay on his back on the car deck, the open deck where the trucks parked, with his flashlight on the underside of the bait truck and the cold smell of old herring on his face. The engine was already running. It came up through the steel into his shoulders.

Nothing. He slid out and did the lumber truck, cab and load, then the crew room's two bunks and the engine room. This boat had sat at an open pier last night with the island asleep, and something had watched it from off the point since dawn yesterday. He found nothing. That was the job done right.

At the far end of the passenger cabin, Dane cut the kid's cable tie with Ned's knife, buckled a life jacket on him, and tied his wrists in front again with a fresh tie. The kid said nothing.

Ned sat in Russ's cuffs. Dane opened one cuff, worked Ned's arm through a life jacket, closed the cuff and buckled the jacket across his chest. Ned couldn't swim and had never worn one in thirty years on this boat. He looked down at it on himself and didn't seem to know whose it was.

Dane clipped Russ's handheld radio to his coat and keyed it.

"Here," Tess said from the couch end.

The Coast Guard would fly for Russ when the icing cleared, and it wouldn't clear before Tuesday. The same icing kept anything from flying Beth to Portland. So it was this boat, tonight. Russ waited on the couch, his bleeding stopped, with Tess and the shotgun. The prisoners crossed. Tess couldn't guard two men and nurse Russ, and a boy who could name that lawyer, left ashore, would bring that boat's men to Russ.

Beth came in, and Dane said it to her in front of the kid.

"A text from a phone in nobody's name and Ned's word won't arrest that lawyer. The kid's would. And the kid isn't talking."

The kid turned his face to the black window. The boy had just heard that he was the only one aboard who could put that man in a cell.

Beth went out to the hatch, the square lid in the deck over the steering, and knelt. She levered it up with her wrists and reached down and put her taped hand on the rod. She had spent Saturday at the co-op's vise on it. It was as straight as anyone on this island could make it.

"If it binds," she said, "I'll feel it first."

Dane watched her kneel there in the deck lights, her braid down her back, her hands taped white, checking the thing that had skinned them. He'd have knelt beside her and put his mouth to the back of her neck.

Beth stood and shut the hatch with her boot.

"The certificate says two," she said. "You're the other one."

"Two summers and one night."

"The night counts."

He picked up the kid's rifle. The kid's pistol was in his coat pocket. Beth went up the steel stair to the captain's cabin, and Dane followed her up.

* * *

Beth asked Dane for Ned from the wheel, a little before ten.

"If anything happens at the ledges, I want Ned up here at my shoulder. He's the only other one aboard who knows them."

"I don't like it," Dane said, to her face. "He's the man who sold you to them. Up here he's an arm's length from your wheel."

"He can't swim. He's on this boat with me and the boy he sent after me. He won't put it on the rocks."

"It's your call. I'll bring him."

He kept his view, and she kept the decision.

Dane brought Ned up the steel stair. The rail was the steel bar along the back wall of the cabin. He opened one of Russ's cuffs and closed it on the rail behind Beth, the steel cold under his hand. Ned stood where he had stood for thirty years, with somebody else at the wheel.

Dane put the cuff key in Beth's coat pocket, where she could see him do it.

"In what's left of the sea it can only crawl," Beth said. "Three hours at best, the short way."

At the cabin door, on his way down to the ropes, he stopped.

"There may be a boat out there. Do we go?"

"Go."

It was tonight, or Cole went free at two tomorrow.

From the car deck he looked up at her window: her taped hands on the wheel, her face lit from below. He wanted her. He couldn't do one thing about it until Portland, and he was glad of the rule that said so.

Through the lit cabin window he saw the boy on the far bench, the one who could put that lawyer in a cell. Up the dark road, Russ lay on the town office couch waiting for Tuesday's helicopter, with Tess and the shotgun.

At her call he lifted each rope off its post, threw it clear, and climbed back up.

Nobody was coming out with them. The Coast Guard would come by sea only if that boat came at them, and then from an hour off. The Marine Patrol boat on this stretch had a hole in it, and the state wouldn't send another into a shooting.

She took it off the pier and across the harbor, slowly. At the mouth she slowed again for the ledges, the rocks just under the water on both sides, and white water showed in the deck lights. Dane stood at her shoulder on the side away from Ned, with the rifle.

Past the mouth he turned to the window at the back of the cabin.

Off the point, the one light was moving. It swung in behind them, low on the black water, and under their own engine he heard a bigger one, keeping pace.
