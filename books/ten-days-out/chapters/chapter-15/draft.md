# Chapter Sixteen: Inside the Ledges

The boat showing one light came up on them fast, and Beth Calloway knew in the first minute that she couldn't outrun it.

The light swung out from behind them and came up on her left, close, and the sound of its engine came with it, bigger than her own. It was a fishing boat twice as fast as the ferry. Men stood on its deck in its own light.

The side window beside her broke.

Glass came in over her shoulder and down her collar, and the cold poured in after it. Two more shots hit the cabin wall. She had never been shot at on her own boat. Her hands wanted to let go of the wheel and cover her head. She kept them on the wheel.

Dane was already at the cabin door with the rifle.

"I'm going up top. Say go."

Another shot hit the cabin while he waited for her.

"Go."

He went.

The upper deck was the open roof over the passenger cabin, behind her and above her, up a ladder, with a rail round it. From up there he could see all round. From the wheel she could only see ahead.

The ferry's radio speaker crackled over the engine, and his voice came out of it, thin and close.

"I'm up. I'm at the back rail, behind you. It's on your left, about as far off as the length of the pier. Coming up."

She found the handset with one hand. "Keep talking."

"I'm still talking, so I'm still fine. Keep it straight."

She kept it straight. Between his words she listened for his breath on the radio, and it was there.

"I'm going to shoot at it. Keep it straight."

The rifle cracked over her head, three times. The one light swung away and dropped back.

"It's dropping back. It's a long way off now." Then: "It's coming again. Your left. Faster."

If the ferry went on the rocks tonight, in this sea, everyone ashore would say the storm had done it. She couldn't turn back. The big boat was between her and the harbor mouth.

She kept one hand on the wheel and keyed the ferry's handset with the other.

"Coast Guard. This is the Cutter Island ferry, out from the island toward Pruett. A fishing boat is shooting at us. Four aboard."

A woman's voice came back, far off and plain. They were coming. About an hour.

Then a man's voice came on the same air, calm and close.

"They're an hour off, ferry. Stop your engine and give us the girl and the boy, and the rest of you go home."

Every boat at sea could hear the ferry's radio. That was what it was for. Now they had the hour too.

"Beth, don't answer him," Dane said, as if nobody else were listening. "I'll do the talking."

"You talk a lot, Marshal."

"Everybody says so."

Nobody on that boat had spoken before. Now they knew what he was.

The light came up on her left again, between her and Pruett. Every time she held her course, it closed. Every time she bent right to keep it off, it followed and closed again. It was pushing her toward the island's point, the long arm of land on the far side of the harbor mouth. It wanted her on the rocks.

She turned the wheel toward the point. The dark shape of the land came up on her right, with white water breaking between her and it.

* * *

"It's behind you on your left. Close. It's keeping you on the rocks."

Beth held the ferry along the outside of the white water. The sea boomed on the ledges through the broken window, and every boom came up through the wheel into her hands. Ned had his head turned toward the sound.

The ledges ran on out along the point for a mile. Close in to the shore, inside them, there was one narrow way, and it was the pencil line on her pantry door. A boat that didn't know it would be on the rocks in a boat's length.

The big boat couldn't follow her in there. If it saw her go in on purpose, it would wait at the far end and finish her there. She needed it close behind her, and not looking, when she turned. She couldn't say any of that on the radio. They'd hear.

She keyed the handset.

"Dane. You still owe me an answer."

"On the other side. Get us there."

Anyone listening had heard it. She didn't care. Let them hear it.

"Beth. Can I tell them about the steering?"

The steering was fine. She could feel every spoke of it turning true under her skinned palms. He knew that. He had never once lied to her. Now he was asking her for a lie, to tell them.

"Tell them."

"Her steering's gone."

That was all of it. The men behind her had watched the ferry sit at the pier all Saturday with nobody aboard. They would believe it. A boat that couldn't steer couldn't run for the rocks on purpose.

She let the wheel go slack. The ferry wandered off its line, and its nose drifted right, toward the white water. She let it drift. Behind her the one light swung in, fast, to be aboard before the ferry hit.

"It's coming in behind you," Dane said. "Close. Closer."

The man's voice came over the speaker. "Shut your engine off, ferry. We'll take you off before you hit."

At the rail by her left hand, Ned said quietly, "Kind of them."

The white water was right off her right hand now. She had to turn in at the one gap, and soon. She didn't turn her head.

"Ned. Where do I go in?"

"Alton's way. You've seen it on the door."

"You've taken it."

"Never. He never took anybody."

* * *

She put the wheel over toward the shore.

The ferry came round slowly into the gap, and the white water closed in on both sides of its nose. It was a narrow way between the rocks, and you had to know it to the yard.

She steered from the drawing.

In past the first rock, and close along the shore. She brought the ferry in until the black of the point filled the right-hand windows, and she held it there.

"Breaking on your left," Ned said.

She heard it. The sea broke white on the ledges to her left and on the shore rocks to her right, each wave by itself, and she steered between the two sounds.

Out again at the second, where the pencil bent away from the land. She eased the wheel left, and the shore went away from her right hand.

"Both sides now," Ned said. "Hold it."

She held it. He had spent thirty years outside these rocks, listening to where they broke. He had never once been inside them.

Dane's voice came out of the speaker. "It's turning in after you. It's right behind you." A pause. "It's very big."

Then came shots, a long burst of them, at the upper deck over her head. Dane's voice said, "It's—" and stopped in the middle of the word.

The radio hissed and said nothing.

The third turn was coming, the tightest one, where the pencil line nearly touched the shore. If she let go now, everyone aboard went on the rocks, and he went with them. She made the turn.

She brought the wheel back in hard, and the ferry's side slid past the shore rocks so close she could hear the sea sucking at them. Then the narrow way opened ahead.

Behind her, steel hit rock.

She felt it through the deck before she heard it: a long grinding crash, and the big engine screaming. The one light swung up and over and away. The big boat had come in close behind her, blind, and found the second rock.

The engine screamed again, and the light backed off the ledge. It turned, slowly, and went limping off into the dark, back toward the gap. It stayed up. Its light moved away from her across the water and got smaller.

Ned had his head turned to it, listening. "Nobody's in," he said. "Nobody in the water."

The white water fell behind on both sides. The ferry came out past the end of the point into open water.

Beth keyed the handset.

"Dane."

The speaker hissed. Nothing.

* * *

Beth took the cuff key out of her coat pocket.

They were clear of the rocks. Ned was the only other person aboard who could hold the boat. He couldn't swim, and everyone he had sold was aboard with him: her, and the boy his texts had sent after her.

She unlocked the cuff from the rail and left it hanging open from his wrist. She left him free at her wheel.

"I've got it, Bethie. Go."

He took the wheel where he had stood for thirty years. She went.

The ladder was iced, and the wind on the upper deck hit her full in the face. Empty brass rolled by the rail. Dane lay on his back beside it with the rifle at his side and Russ's radio still in his hand. A bullet had opened a line along his ribs, and Russ's flannel shirt was soaked dark down one side. His eyes were open.

"You left the wheel," he said.

"You stopped talking."

"I got shot. I'll start again in a minute."

"Who's got the boat?"

"Ned."

He looked at her face and didn't argue.

The first-aid box was empty. She pulled off her black knit hat, folded it and pressed it into his side with both taped palms. Her palms hurt. She pressed harder, and held it there.

She wanted him, here, bleeding on the upper deck of her boat in a borrowed shirt, and she had never wanted anything so much in her life.

"Is this the other side?" he said.

"Pruett's the other side. Stay alive till then."

Blue and white lights came fast across the water from the mainland side, about an hour after her call. A Coast Guard boat swung wide of them and went on after the one light, which was a long way off now, and fading.

The radio in Dane's hand spoke. "Ferry, this is the Coast Guard. We're going after the fishing boat. We'll come back and see you in."

She didn't think it would find anything.

Dane got an elbow under himself. "Can I lean on you?"

"Yes."

"I want everybody aboard in one room where I can see them."

She went down the ladder first, backward, with his weight on her shoulder and his hand in her coat collar, toward the cabin where Ned had her boat.

* * *

Beth took the wheel back from Ned. It was warm from his hands.

"Your marshal?" Ned said.

"Talking."

It was true. At the cabin door behind her, Dane was already talking down the steel stair to the kid, telling him to come up slowly.

Ned stepped back from the wheel to the rail. He took the open cuff hanging from his wrist and closed it round the rail. Nobody had asked him. The click was the only sound he made.

The kid came up the stair with his wrists tied in front of him over his life jacket. There was glass in his hair. His own people had shot out the windows where he sat. Dane, holding his side, sat him on the floor against the back wall, a yard from Ned's boots.

The kid talked to Dane, fast, one fact after another.

"It was Cole. Through Cole's lawyer. The lawyer paid me."

"That boat's theirs. It brings the stuff in from offshore."

"Her picture. Cole had me take it off Tess's board in September, so I'd know her. The girl on the boat. The one who'd have the ferry in June."

"I want that written down."

Dane typed it into his phone one-handed, in full sentences, with periods.

Cole had known she'd have this boat before she did, and he had wanted her face for it. Beth kept her hands on the wheel.

At the rail, Ned heard the lawyer named. He shut his eyes.

A little before four the lights of Pruett came up out of the dark. She brought the ferry in against the pier slowly, on the first try. She had never landed it before. Her hands knew how.

Deputies came aboard out of the police lights, in jackets that said what they were. The first thing a deputy took was Dane's gun. Then the kid's rifle. Dane handed them over without a word, holding his side.

A deputy took the key from Beth, freed Ned from the rail and led him off in Russ's cuffs. At the top of the ramp he looked back once and lifted his cuffed hands. Beth didn't lift hers.

The kid went separately, between two deputies, still talking.

Dane went on a stretcher. Snow lay on the planks. The ambulance for Maine Medical Center stood at the head of the pier with its back doors open and light coming out of them. Beth kept her hand on the edge of the stretcher all the way up.

At the doors he turned his head to her.

"Ten o'clock. Will you go in without me?"

If she said yes, she walked into that room alone, with Cole's people knowing her face and him in a hospital bed.

The ambulance man closed the doors.

The ambulance pulled away up the pier road. A deputy held a car door open for her. She got in. Six hours.
