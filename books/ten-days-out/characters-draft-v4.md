## CHARACTERS

### Beth Calloway

- **Name:** Elizabeth Ann Calloway on her license, Beth to everyone. A plain name for a Maine girl born in 1993. Calloway is an old island family. Ned is the only person alive who calls her "Bethie." Tess calls her "Calloway." Dane calls her "Ms. Calloway" on the first night and when he's angry, and "Beth" after that.
- **Age, body, clothes:** 33. Five foot ten, almost Dane's height, so they meet eye to eye (the test chapter: "tall, almost his height"). Strong through the shoulders and arms from eleven years of chains and ropes. Long dark-blond braid, gray eyes, a red wind-burned face from October to April. A white scar across her left palm from a rope at 23. For work: an orange work coat, rubber boots, a black knit hat. At home: her father's old wool sweater, elbows darned in the wrong color. The prose never makes her size or shape a topic.
- **Physical habit others notice:** **she takes her gloves off to do anything that matters**: a chain, a knot, a cut on a man's arm, a dead engine. In sleet, bare-handed, and the gloves go back on after. Dane notices in Ch 04 when she tapes his arm. Tess scolds her for it. It costs her from Ch 11 on, when her hands are burned and she does it anyway in bandages.
- **Age-true behavior (33, in 2026):**
  1. **Her phone lives in a zip bag in her chest pocket.** She has a muted group text with two friends from high school, one in Portland and one in Bangor, both with small kids, and she reads it all at once on Sunday nights and hearts the baby pictures without writing anything.
  2. **She tried a dating app once.** From Pruett, at a fifty-mile radius, it found her nine men, and two were cousins. She deleted it in the ferry office parking lot. She tells Dane this in Ch 04 or Ch 08 as a joke against herself.
  3. **Her body is 33 and works for a living.** Her lower back is stiff after a double run and she stretches it against the kitchen counter without thinking. She can still go out a window at a run, but she feels it the next morning, and she says so.
  - *Also:* she has a saved search for cheap flights to somewhere warm (Florida, once Puerto Rico) that she has never booked. She has never been farther south than Boston. Her music comes from her phone through one earbud on the night runs, and it's mostly what she listened to at 19.
- **The age gap:** five years. Beth: "He was thirty-eight, which on Cutter counts as young." Dane: he notices she's younger than him for about a second on the first night and then forgets it for the rest of the book. It's never mentioned as a problem.
- **Background (single lines):**
  - Born and raised on Cutter. The island school through eighth grade, then high school on the mainland, boarding in the week.
  - Her mother left for Bangor when Beth was nine. They talk at Christmas.
  - Her father, Alton Calloway, was a lobsterman. The bank took his boat eight years ago and sold it to a summer man who made it a picnic boat.
  - Alton died of a heart attack six years ago, at 61. She lives alone in his house and keeps his fish shack on the rocks below it, with his oars still in the corner.
  - One year at the maritime college in Castine. She came home when her father got sick the first time.
  - Deckhand on the island ferry for eleven years, under Ned.
  - She got her captain's license at 30. The island newsletter ran her picture. She has never once taken the wheel on her own.
  - She owes nobody a dollar, on purpose.
- **What she wants that has nothing to do with Dane:** **The captain's job.** Ned retires in June. If no islander asks for it, the ferry board will hire a stranger from the mainland. Beth has the license and the eleven years. She hasn't asked Ned to put her name forward, because asking means saying out loud that he's old, and that she wants something. *What she's doing about it before the book starts:* she renewed the license this spring. She has driven to the ferry office in Pruett twice and sat in the parking lot without going in. She watches every move Ned makes at the wheel and does it in her head after him. (Pays: Ch 08 she tells Dane, and he dares her to ask; Ch 09 she asks Ned on the beach and he says yes; Ch 13 her first time at the wheel; Ch 16 she lands the boat; Ch 18 the job is hers.)
  - *Small want:* to see a warm ocean once. Never booked. It sits behind "Those boots are for a different ocean" and can pay in one line in Ch 18.
- **Wound, and what it makes her do:** Her father did everything right and lost his boat on paper anyway, and she carried the bank man's papers out to his truck because her father asked her to. *Behavior:* she would rather do a job herself than ask for help with it. She pays back every favor at once. She never asks for anything she wants, so she can never be told no. And she trusts her own people completely, because they're all she has.
- **Need:** To ask out loud for what she wants (the job, the man) and let someone give it to her.
- **What she would never do:**
  1. Run. Not from her house, not from her island, not from a man with a gun. Running would tell Cole's people that shooting at a witness works.
  2. Leave a job half done because someone told her to stop.
  3. Lie about something she did. If they ask whether she slept with the marshal, she'll say yes, then tell them what she saw. (Ch 03, Ch 17.)
- **The choice a reasonable reader would condemn:**
  - **Primary (Ch 08 s2, the tower).** With Dane begging her not to, she phones Ned and Russ and asks them both to come down to the beach at three and see her off. Tess has to know, because she clears the landing. The other two don't. *Why she does it:* they're the two men she loves, it may be years before she sees them again, and she won't leave her island treating her own people like suspects. If she does that, Cole has already taken them from her. Cost: the ambush, and a Marine Patrol officer shot. The page gives her the reason in her own words, out loud, to Dane's face.
  - **Secondary (Ch 10 s3, the test).** She lies to all three people she loves, one call at a time, and gives Tess the church. *Why:* she would rather know than go on loving all three of them and wondering about each one. Cost: her best friend runs into a burning building for her (Ch 11). Beth knows the test could hurt whoever turns out to be innocent, and she does it anyway.
- **How she is funny, and at whom:**
  - **The flat verdict.** One plain sentence, conclusion first, no smile to signal the joke. "Those boots are for a different ocean." "That ham is load-bearing." Her target is Dane's mainland softness and anything a man has bought to look prepared.
  - **She says the embarrassing thing out loud on purpose.** Everyone else talks around it, and Beth walks up to the counter and buys the condoms in front of Tess, two lobstermen and God. She asks Dane, "How many witnesses have kissed you?" as if it's an ordinary question. The joke is that she won't be embarrassed. Her targets are Dane, Tess and the island's gossip.
  - **She won't bet.** "I don't bet on what I can see." The relationship's joke (below).
  - *Sample:* Dane: "I'd like to point out that I jumped." / Beth: "You fell toward the boat. It's different."
  - *Who she's funny with:* Ned on the boat, where his slow understatement sets up her verdict. Tess at the counter, where Tess announces and Beth corrects her in four words.
- **What she finds attractive in Dane, plainly:**
  - *First thing she notices:* the boots, and then that he's warm. She leans into him in the snow before she's decided to.
  - His mouth. She thinks about it the first night and every night after, and says so in her head in those words.
  - That he talks to her in the dark, low and steady, when everyone else she knows goes quiet.
  - That he jumps when he plainly jumps badly. He doesn't wait to be good at a thing before he does it.
  - His forearms with his sleeves pushed up, cooking too much food.
  - That he loses an argument to her and says so, out loud, without sulking.
  - That he says what he wants. She has never been with a man who does.
- **Syntax profile:**
  - *Sentence length and shape.* Dialogue: short to medium, and always whole sentences now (no clipped deckhand fragments). Conclusion first; the reason only if pushed. Imperatives with no "please": "Come here." "Give me your arm." "Move." She asks short questions she already knows the answer to, one at a time, and waits. No hedges, no "I think," no "maybe." Interior: plain and physical, a little longer than her speech, and it says what she wants in plain words ("She wanted him"). It lands on a short sentence.
  - *Under pressure.* **Fear:** she goes quiet and listens, because she knows the island by sound. Two or three words at most ("Stop. Listen."). This is the core contrast with Dane, who talks more. **Anger:** she gets plainer and bossier and uses his full name, then does something with her hands (throws the mug, laces her boots). **Lying:** a bad liar out loud. The lie is too short and she follows it with a task or hangs up ("I'm fine. Hold this."). **Wanting:** she says it. "I want you." "Come here." "Don't stop." In the heat scenes her interior runs long and she notices she isn't steering it. This is her high-temperature register, and it has to be on the page.
  - *What she notices first.* Hands, what people are doing with them and whether they know how. Then sounds: a door, a step, an engine that's wrong.
  - *One habit of thought hers alone.* **She sums people up in one plain sentence.** "Russ Whitcomb was a good man who was bad with money." "Cole Varney tipped twenty dollars at Christmas and never once looked her in the eye." She has a verdict on everyone, and the book breaks the one she has on Ned. *Second habit:* when she can't decide, she fixes the nearest broken thing, and by the time it works she knows what she's going to do (the generator in Ch 08, the steering in Ch 13).
  - *Her words, never Dane's:* "Marshal" (as a tease, when she's flirting; "Dane" when it's serious), "Fine." (her pivot), "Quit it.", "Come here.", "Go home.", "That's a no.", "ours", "from away" (once or twice, for mainland people), "the boat" (always the ferry), "wicked" (once in the book at most). She never says "okay" as a pivot and never uses a police or legal word without making fun of it.
- **Ten lines of dialogue:**
  - *Flirting*
    1. "You keep looking at my mouth, Marshal. Say something or do something. I'm cold either way."
    2. "I'm going to kiss you. If you've got a reason why not, you've got about three seconds, and it had better be better than a jury."
  - *Angry*
    3. "You told your boss I'd go. With me standing right here. You decided I'd rather be alive in Ohio than be me, and you didn't even ask."
    4. "Move, Dane, or I go out the window. I've done it before. In socks."
  - *Lying*
    5. "Tess. It's me. We're in the church tonight. Don't tell anyone. Not Russ, not Ned. I have to go." (Ch 10 s3)
    6. "I'm fine. Hold this. No, the other end."
  - *Afraid*
    7. "Stop. Listen. He's on the steps. That's his bad foot."
    8. "Hold my hand and do exactly what I do. Don't think about it. Just do it."
  - *Tender*
    9. "Come here. You're shaking. Let me be the warm one for once."
    10. "Will you stay? I'm asking. I don't, mostly."
- **Five lines of interior narration:**
  1. She wanted him. She'd wanted him since the oar, and she was done calling it the cold.
  2. Russ Whitcomb was a good man who was bad with money, and she had known both of those things since the fourth grade.
  3. She didn't know what to do, so she fixed the generator, and when it caught under her hands she knew she was going to make the calls.
  4. In eleven years Ned had never been afraid, and now he was afraid every time she looked at him, so she stopped looking.
  5. He tasted like bad coffee. Fine. Let the jury hear about it.
- **Three ordinary pleasures:**
  1. Onions frying in butter in a cold kitchen at dusk, and the smell filling the house before anything else is ready. (Ch 06 opens on it.)
  2. A dead machine starting under her bare hands: the generator, the steering, the heater in the truck.
  3. The first hot mug of the morning, held under her chin with both hands until it's cool enough to drink.

### Dane Reyes

- **Name:** Daniel Efraín Reyes on paper. His mother's family called him Dane from the crib to tell him apart from her father, Daniel, and it stuck. Efraín was his father's name. A Lowell, Massachusetts name from a Puerto Rican father and an Irish-American mother, which is common in that city. Beth can learn the full name in one flirting line, optional, no earlier than Ch 07.
- **Age, body, clothes:** 38. Six foot one, lean, a runner. Dark hair cut short, going gray at the temples. Brown eyes. Clean-shaven on the first night and stubbled by Day 3, and it suits him. Old scar across the right knuckles. A dark gray jacket, jeans, and brand-new waterproof boots from a store on Route 1 that also sells fudge, which are wrong for snow, ice and everything else on Cutter. A gun on his right hip and a small first-aid kit he calls the trauma kit. No gun model on the page.
- **Physical habit others notice:** **he sits with his back to the wall and watches people's hands before their faces.** Tess: "Your marshal looks at my hands like he's pricing them." Beth takes the other chair without being asked by Ch 05. Second habit: **he talks.** In a crisis, low and steady, all the way through. Everyone on the island notices that the marshal never shuts up, and Beth notices when he finally does.
- **Age-true behavior (38, in 2026):**
  1. **He texts in full sentences with periods**, which the younger deputies find threatening, and he tilts his phone screen away from whoever is next to him out of job habit.
  2. **His body is starting to send notes.** His right knee stiffens in the cold (the Army). He stretches his back in the morning, and he can't sleep on a bad mattress the way he could at 28. He runs four miles most days "to stay ahead of forty."
  3. **His references are his.** The 2004 Red Sox, which he watched at 16 on his father's couch. A Celtics game his father still argues about. He knows every song on the radio in his mother's car in 1998.
  - *Also:* he is divorced at 34, and he and his ex-wife still text on each other's birthdays. He has started noticing his friends' kids and wondering, and he doesn't say so.
- **The age gap:** see Beth. Neither of them treats it as anything.
- **Background (single lines):**
  - Grew up in Lowell, Massachusetts. His father drove the same city bus route for thirty years. His mother was a police dispatcher. Both are alive, retired, and still arguing about the thermostat.
  - His Spanish is good with his father and bad on the phone.
  - At 15 and 16 he spent two summers working on his mother's cousin's lobster boat out of Rockport, Massachusetts. That's the calluses, and that's where the want comes from.
  - Four years in the Army as a military policeman, three with the Lowell police, then the U.S. Marshals at 29.
  - A deputy marshal out of Boston, on loan to the Portland office for three weeks.
  - Divorced at 34, quietly. He takes the blame, in one line at most.
  - Four years ago in Boston, a witness named Marisol, 24, asked him for ten minutes at her sister's on the way to a safe house. He gave her the ten minutes, and she was shot in the parking lot. He was cleared. He hasn't bent a plan for anybody since.
  - He has protected forty witnesses. Beth is forty-one.
- **What he wants that has nothing to do with Beth:** **A boat nobody can call him off.** An old wooden lobster boat, the kind he worked on at 16, that he can fix himself and take out on his own days off. He wants one thing in his life whose schedule nobody else sets. He's slightly embarrassed by it, because it sounds like a midlife crisis, so he makes jokes about it before anyone else can. *What he's doing about it before the book starts:* he has saved for six years. He has looked at eleven boats in two years and been called away from three of the viewings. He had one booked for Saturday in Pruett. (Pays: Ch 08, "everybody who doesn't know what they'd do instead wants a boat"; Ch 17 "a worse boat"; Ch 18 he bought it the week after Portland, and it leaks.)
  - *Small want:* to make Beth laugh out loud. He keeps count.
- **Wound, and what it makes him do:** He bent a plan once, for a person, and she died in the ten minutes. *Behavior:* he decides for the people he protects and calls it his job. He thinks about everything in terms of how it will look later to whoever is judging it (a review board, a lawyer, a jury). He talks so the witness keeps talking, because if he can hear her, he knows where she is. And he never says "trust me," because people say that just before it goes wrong.
- **His reason for holding back (Ch 03, the lawyer and the jury):** The truck was clean, so Beth's eyes are the whole case. If Cole's lawyer can stand up and tell twelve strangers that the marshal was sleeping with the only witness, those twelve people start wondering what else the marshal told her to say, and Cole walks. Dane believes this and says it to her face, in those words. He also wants her more than he's wanted anything, and he says that too, in his head. Beth answers him (they won't lie about it, and a jury of grown-ups from Maine has heard of two adults in a snowstorm), and she wins most of the argument. He tells her she's right about most of it. His reason comes due on the page in Ch 13 and Ch 17, so it was never a fake obstacle.
- **Need:** To ask her before he decides, and to stand on his own call about a person, out loud, in front of her.
- **What he would never do:**
  1. Leave her alone on the island with an armed man (Ch 02; it's why he stays).
  2. Lie to her. He'll lie to the kid, to the lawyer, even to his own boss in a test, and always in front of her, but never to her.
  3. Say "trust me" or "you're safe." He'll say "I've got the window" or "I'm right here," which are things he can actually check.
- **The choice a reasonable reader would condemn:**
  - **Primary (Ch 13 s1).** On speaker in the town office, with Beth three feet away, he tells his boss she'll go into witness protection after she testifies: a new name, a new state, no contact with anyone from her old life, including him. *Why:* two years ago in New Bedford, Cole's people burned down the house of a woman who'd testified. He watched Beth run into a burning church two nights ago (Ch 11). He would rather lose her than bury her. He decided her life for her because he was scared, and he called it his job.
  - **Secondary (Ch 09 s1).** After Ned confesses on the beach that he took Cole's money, Dane goes on with the rescue instead of calling it off. *Why:* there's no signal on the beach, a turned-away boat means six more days on the island, and he thinks he can cover her. He moves her to the rocks' end and keeps Ned in front of him. A Marine Patrol officer is shot. A reader can fairly say he bet with her life and somebody else paid.
- **How he is funny, and at whom:**
  - **Specific jokes against himself.** The boots. The jump. The trauma kit with no condoms in it: "It's a very disappointing kit." He's always the first to point out how bad he looks. His target is himself, and nobody else gets there first.
  - **"The government" as a person.** "The government is going to hear about it." "The government would like its boots back." His target is his employer and the whole idea of being sent somewhere by someone in an office.
  - **He keeps count, out loud.** "Forty-one, as of this morning." "As of tonight? One. I'm keeping count." His target is Beth, by telling her she's a number that's gone wrong.
  - **He bets on everything.** "Dollar says…" Beth never takes it.
  - *Sample:* Beth: "How many witnesses have kissed you?" / Dane: "As of tonight? One. I'm keeping count."
  - *Who he's funny with:* Beth first. Russ, who answers his own questions and gives Dane a straight man to work against. Tess at the counter (Ch 05).
- **What he finds attractive in Beth, plainly:**
  - *First thing he notices:* an oar coming at his head. Then that she's freezing in her socks and still making fun of his boots. He's in trouble by the end of the first page they share.
  - Her mouth, and wanting to find out what it does when she actually smiles.
  - That she's rude to him and right.
  - Her eyes level with his. He doesn't have to look down to fight with her.
  - Her bare hands in the cold, doing something he couldn't.
  - That she says no without apologizing, and yes the same way.
  - Her laugh when it finally comes, which he has to work for, which is why he counts it.
- **Syntax profile:**
  - *Sentence length and shape.* Dialogue: longer and looser than hers. Whole sentences with "because" and "which," built toward a dry turn at the end. He asks a lot of questions and makes them easy to answer ("Talk to me." "What am I looking at?" "Who's that?"). "Here's how this goes" starts his plans (it's in the approved Ch 01). Interior: long sentences about people, what they want, and how a thing will sound later, often running on "and," ending on a short clause that undercuts him.
  - *Under pressure.* **Fear:** he talks more, low and steady, in the present tense, so she knows where he is and he knows where she is. If both leads go quiet in a danger scene, the scene has lost a character. **Anger:** very polite and slow. "Ms. Calloway" comes back, and he tells her he's going to say something once. **Lying:** he goes short. The man who talks in paragraphs gives three words ("Slept fine." "Boathouse. Nobody else has it."). **Wanting:** he goes quiet. Her name, a question, "Can I?" The man who never stops talking stops, and Beth notices.
  - *What he notices first.* Hands: what people do with them, what they're holding. Then where people stand, and who they look at before they answer.
  - *One habit of thought his alone.* **He hears everything the way a jury would hear it later.** He can't do anything without picturing Cole's lawyer in a good coat saying it back slowly to twelve people from Maine. It's why he stops the kiss, and it's what Beth argues him out of. *Second habit:* he keeps count (witnesses, kisses, her laughs).
  - *His words, never Beth's:* "Here's how this goes," "Talk to me," "Dollar says," "the government" as a person, "I'm keeping count," "fair," "my witness," "Ms. Calloway." One Massachusetts word per act at most ("the packie," "the rotary"). He never says "Marshal" about himself, "from away," "Fine." as a pivot, or "wicked" (he's from Massachusetts, but the book gives that word to Beth). See Flag 1 on "Okay," "for the record" and "I'd like it noted."
  - *Motif caps (jokes only; there is no cap on attraction):* "Dollar says," four in the book. The boots, three or four (Ch 01, one in the middle, Ch 18 "the right boots"). "Keeping count," three. "The government," three.
- **Ten lines of dialogue:**
  - *Flirting*
    1. "You swung an oar at a federal officer and then made fun of his shoes. I want you to know that's the best first meeting I've had in nine years of this job."
    2. "Dollar says you kiss me again before Tuesday. You don't have to take the bet. I just want you to know I saw it coming."
  - *Angry*
    3. "Ms. Calloway. I'm going to say this one time, slowly, so you can repeat it to whoever you call next. That man knew which house was yours."
    4. "You made three phone calls on a frozen staircase, and now everybody you love knows where you'll be standing at three o'clock. Tell me which one of them you'd like me to stop trusting first."
  - *Lying*
    5. "Yacht club boathouse. Tonight. Nobody else has it." (Ch 10 s3, to his boss, in front of Beth)
    6. "Slept fine."
  - *Afraid*
    7. "Here's how this goes. You stay on the floor, I stay between you and the window, and you keep breathing where I can hear it. You don't have to talk. I'll talk. I'm very good at it, ask anybody in Massachusetts."
    8. "I'm at the front of the second truck. It's dark, which is fine, because it's dark for him too. I'm still talking, which means I'm still fine. Keep the boat straight for me." (Ch 16, on the radio)
  - *Tender*
    9. "I'm in love with you. You don't have to do anything about it. I just didn't want to get you off this island with it still sitting in my mouth."
    10. "Can I kiss you? I'm asking. You told me to ask every time, and I'm going to ask every time, for as long as you'll let me."
- **Five lines of interior narration:**
  1. He could already hear Cole's lawyer saying it, slowly, in a good coat, to twelve people from Maine: and where did the deputy sleep that night?
  2. Forty-one witnesses, and he had never once wanted to make one of them laugh, and this one had laughed twice since breakfast, and he was keeping count.
  3. She went quiet when she was scared, which meant somebody had to talk, and he had been talking since Lowell.
  4. He wanted her so badly he'd stopped watching the window, and the window was his whole job.
  5. Marisol had told him that everybody who didn't know what they'd do instead wanted a boat, and he had never told anyone she said it, and now he was telling a woman on a lighthouse floor.
- **Three ordinary pleasures:**
  1. **Cooking too much food.** His mother worked nights, so he cooked from the age of twelve, and he still cooks as if the whole apartment building is coming. The tin of ham in Ch 08. The eleven-pound ham in Ch 15. The ham at Thanksgiving in Ch 18, too big, everyone fed.
  2. **Dry socks.** After the sea in Ch 07, and every time after, a pair of dry wool socks is the best thing that has happened to him all day, and he says so.
  3. **Watching someone very good at a job he can't do.** Beth with the generator, the steering, the boat in the dark. He stops talking to watch.

## SUPPORTING CHARACTERS (speaking roles)

### Ned Haskell (64, ferry captain; the traitor)

- **Body and habits:** Big and stooped, slow on land, quick and easy at the wheel. Short white beard, red face, reading glasses pushed up on his head (the test chapter). A green wool jacket older than Beth. A bad hip, worse after the pier in Ch 02. He brings coffee as an apology. **He always looks back:** when he leaves anyone, he turns once and lifts a hand. Planted early (Ch 02, once more by Ch 05). Paid in Ch 12 (walked up the road in cuffs, he looks back once) and Ch 16 (off the boat in cuffs, once more).
- **Age-true (64):** he reads his phone at arm's length, texts with one finger and signs his texts "Ned." The prepaid phone in the captain's cabin is the only phone he's ever kept charged.
- **What he wants:** to retire in June with Beth at the wheel, and to keep his son alive. For three years he has wanted to put Beth's name forward and waited for her to ask, while she waited for him to offer. His son in Portland is an addict. For two years Ned took Cole's cash to run Cole's trucks across at night, told himself it was lobster, and asked no questions, because he couldn't stand for it to be the other thing.
- **Wound, and what it makes him do:** Widowed at 50, he has carried everything alone since. *Behavior:* he decides who gets hurt and carries it himself. "Then you didn't see it. Not tonight." That reads as an old man's fear for her in Ch 01 and as something else in Ch 09.
- **How he talks:**
  - "Then you didn't see it. Not tonight."
  - "She'll go when she goes, Bethie. Boats don't read the calendar."
  - "I've got her, Bethie. Go." (Ch 16)
  - *(Slow understatement. He answers a hard question with the weather first. "Bethie" to Beth and no one else; "your marshal" for Dane. When he's hiding something he gives too much detail, which is the opposite of Dane, who goes short. ### Tess Marr (46, the island store and emergency director; Book 2 heroine)

- **Body and habits:** Short, solid, quick. Cropped salt-and-pepper hair she cuts herself. Reading glasses on her head with a pencil through them. A red work vest and fingerless gloves, in a store she won't heat above 58. A laugh you can hear from the road. She drives the island's one fire truck and is first to every fire. Her habit: she answers the phone before the first ring is done, and she hangs it up just as fast (Ch 05).
- **What she wants:** to keep the store out of the bank's hands. Cole lent her the money for the new fuel tanks, so his arrest puts her under ("and you just put my store under, Calloway"). *Second want:* to keep one thing in her life the island has no opinion on. That thing is Russ. She has been single on purpose for twenty years and the whole island has had something to say about it; Russ is two years younger and broke, and she won't have it look like she's carrying him.
- **How she talks:**
  - "Four seventy-nine. Good till next August."
  - "What was I supposed to do, ask for ID?"
  - "Calloway. Sit down before you fall down. That's free. The coffee's a dollar."
  - *(She announces in headlines. She calls everyone by surname: "Calloway," "Whitcomb," "Haskell," "Marshal." She uses "Beth" only when it's serious, so the reader hears it when she does. She talks prices constantly. Under stress she gets louder and busier. She's distinct from Ned, who answers with weather, and from Beth, who gives one-sentence verdicts.)*
- **What she's for:** the suspect the reader likes. Her visible lie (too fast about the Dyer call, the phone hung up) is true and innocent of the leak: it's Russ. She runs into the burning church for Beth (Ch 11), and Beth's test is what put her there. She doesn't forgive Beth in this book. The pie in Ch 18 and the "Calloway" are a start.
- **Surprising thing:** when the bank sold Beth's father's boat, Tess bid on it with money she didn't have, to keep it on the island, and lost. She has never told Beth. Kept for Book 2.

### Russ Whitcomb (44, the island's constable and a lobsterman; Book 2 hero)

- **Body and habits:** Middle height, wide, red beard going gray at the chin. Missing the tip of his left ring finger. A brown work jacket, a Red Sox cap, a badge on his belt he sometimes forgets. He takes the cap off and puts it back on to buy time, and leans on whatever is nearest. His lobster boat goes up on the rocks in Ch 02, and he watches it go.
- **What he wants:** to be taken seriously as a lawman by an island that thinks he's a lobsterman with a badge. To get his boat back off the rocks before spring. And Tess, out in the open.
- **How he talks:**
  - "Who'd I tell? Nobody. Who'd I tell." (Ch 05; the lie, said twice, the second time to himself)
  - "Where are you sleeping tonight? I'm not asking for me. I'm asking because somebody will."
  - "Well." (Ch 15, the whole line)
  - *(He asks a question and answers it himself. He says "somebody" for gossip. Slow and amused, including at himself. On the radio he's suddenly formal.)*
- **What he's for:** a suspect with a real lie (he told Tess where Beth slept; he was with her on the nights he can't account for). The armed man Dane can't fully trust until Ch 11, which is why Dane can't split from Beth. Shot in the thigh in Ch 14. The Book 2 hero.
- **Surprising thing:** he finished first in his police class at 41 and told no one. The certificate was in the cabin of his boat, and it went onto the rocks with her.

### Cole Varney (50s, the island's lobster dealer; the man in jail)

- **Body and habits:** Big, soft, well-liked, a good wool coat, a laugh people repeat. Six years of Thursdays driving the seafood truck onto Beth's ferry. He tips twenty dollars at Christmas and never once looks her in the eye.
- **What he wants:** to walk out on Monday. Then for everyone on the island to go on owing him.
- **How he talks** (in Beth's memory, or one line in the cab in Ch 01 if the beats want it):
  - "Merry Christmas, Beth. Don't spend it all on the boat."
  - "Tess, you pay me when you pay me. I'm not the bank."
  - "Nice night for it." (to Ned, every Thursday, about any weather)
- **What he's for:** the case, and the island's own betrayal. Everyone owes him something, which is why three of Beth's people are believable suspects.
- **Surprising thing:** he paid for the flowers at the funeral of the boy Beth taught to row. Beth remembers it in Ch 01 or Ch 02, and she can't stop remembering it.

### The kid (about 25; unnamed on purpose)

- **Body and markers:** thin face, a split lip, a black hoodie, and **his left heel drags** (the one marker a listener tracks him by). Sterned two summers on Cole's own lobster boat for cash, lived in a trailer Cole owns, and answered only to "kid," so nobody on the island knows his real name.
- **What he wants:** to be paid and gone, and not to be the one who goes to prison. He's more scared than Beth is on the first night, which frightens her more than anything.
- **How he talks:**
  - "You didn't see this." (Ch 01)
  - "Beth. Come on. Nobody wants to hurt you." (Ch 01, in her kitchen, calling her like a cat)
  - "It was Cole. Through the lawyer. The one in the good coat. I want that written down." (Ch 16, cuffed on the cabin floor)

- **What he's for:** the danger on the page. Every time he's close, the reader hears the heel. Beth finds him in the dark on the car deck by it (Ch 16), throws him the life ring when he goes over, and hauls him back, because he can testify too.

## RELATIONSHIPS

### Beth and Dane

- **The friction that isn't the plot:**
  1. **Talk against silence.** When she's scared she goes quiet and listens; when he's scared he talks. Each thinks the other's way is a little crazy. In danger the contrast is the scene: Dane talking low and steady, Beth silent and listening. It pays in Ch 16: "You came down." / "You stopped talking."
  2. **How it will look against what's in front of her.** He thinks about everything the way a jury would hear it later. She thinks about the thing in front of her and does it. "How's that going to sound in a courtroom?" / "It's going to sound like I did it." Neither is wrong, which is why it's funny and not only a fight.
  3. **Owing.** She pays back every favor on the spot: the coffee, the hand up, the coat. He lets favors ride and would rather be owed. "You're welcome." / "I didn't say anything." / "I noticed."
- **What they enjoy about each other before they admit it:** She likes being talked to in the dark, and that he loses an argument to her without sulking. He likes that she's the first witness in forty-one he can't predict, that she bosses him around, and that she's rude to him and right.
- **The relationship's own joke: the bet she won't take.** He bets a dollar on everything ("Dollar says Tess has us married by noon"). She answers with the outcome instead: "I don't bet on what I can see." Planted in Ch 06 s1 while she cooks. In Ch 15 she takes her first bet and wins. In Ch 18 she takes a bet on something she can't see (that his leaky boat floats till noon) and loses, happily. Running beside it: he keeps count (witnesses, kisses, her laughs), and she tells him to stop counting.
- **How they flirt (both POVs, obviously):** Every exchange between them has a line the listener can't miss. He teases her; she tells him plainly what she wants. Their fights are about real things (leaving the island, the kiss and the jury, the calls from the tower, the door, the program), happen face to face in the scene where they start, and leave both partly right. Most of them tip into flirting before the scene ends. Nothing between them is overheard, guessed wrong or kept back.
- **What each is afraid the other will find out:**
  - *Beth:* that she has never asked for the captain's job because she's afraid to be the one at the wheel when it goes wrong, since her father did everything right and lost anyway. And that she was glad, for one second, when the police boat left without her on Ch 02's first morning, because he stayed.
  - *Dane:* that the boat is a way out, and he has been thinking about quitting since Marisol without saying it to anyone. And that he stayed on the island in Ch 02 for her, not only for the case, and he knew it while he was telling his boss otherwise.

### Other pairs (one line each)

- **Beth and Ned:** her father in all but name, and the one person whose verdict she never questions. Ned waits for her to ask for the job; she waits for him to offer; she finally asks on the beach in Ch 09, and he says yes in the same breath as his confession.
- **Beth and Tess:** near-sisters, thirteen years apart. Tess walked her home from every dance and every wake on the island. Beth's test nearly kills her, and Tess doesn't forgive it in this book.
- **Beth and Russ:** four grades apart at the island school, teasing, never anything more. She trusts him on thirty years of knowing him, and he lies to Dane anyway, for Tess.
- **Beth and Cole:** six years of Thursdays and a twenty-dollar tip. She saw him in the cab. Her eyes are the case.
- **Beth and the kid:** he told her she didn't see it. She knows his walk. She saves his life in Ch 16 so he can testify.
- **Dane and Ned:** Dane likes him, sets him in front of him on the beach anyway, and is still surprised. Ned calls him "your marshal."
- **Dane and Tess:** one counter conversation (Ch 05). She prices his hands and his boots; he asks her about the Dyer call and watches her answer too fast.
- **Dane and Russ:** federal and local, wary. Russ lies to his face in Ch 05 and Dane knows it. Russ gets shot holding the cabin for him in Ch 14, and they're fine after.
- **Dane and his boss:** the one person in the office he'd trust with his life, and he still tests her in Ch 10 ("If I'm testing yours, I test mine").

## THE STRIP-THE-NAMES TEST

Run before saving on the twenty lead dialogue lines and the ten interior lines, with names and labels removed and the order shuffled. All thirty sorted without hesitation. The cues:

- **Beth:** short whole sentences, conclusion first; imperatives with no "please" ("Come here." "Move." "Hold this."); "Marshal" as a tease; "Fine."; one-sentence verdicts on people; wanting said plainly ("I want," "Come here"); hands and fixing things; in fear, two or three words and then silence.
- **Dane:** long sentences with "because" and "which" building to a dry turn; "Here's how this goes," "Dollar says," "I'm keeping count," "Ms. Calloway"; jokes against himself; how it will sound to a jury; asking permission ("Can I?"); in fear, more words, not fewer; lies in three words.

Lines that needed a rewrite to pass:
- **Beth's tender line 9** first read "Come here. You're freezing." Dane could have said that. "Let me be the warm one for once" is hers: it answers his coat in the snow in Ch 01, and only she owes him that.
- **Dane's afraid line 7** first ended at "you keep breathing where I can hear it." The self-mocking turn ("ask anybody in Massachusetts") makes it his.
- **Beth's interior 2** and **Dane's interior 2** both run on "and." Hers is a verdict about another person; his is a count about himself. Keep those shapes in any edit.

**For the dialogue doctor and voice editor:** an earlier voice editor flattened both leads toward one register. If a line could belong to either lead, it's wrong. The danger scenes are the test: Beth goes silent and Dane talks. The heat scenes are the second test: Beth says what she wants and Dane goes quiet. If both leads do the same thing, the scene has lost a character.

## FLAGS FOR THE ORCHESTRATOR

**Outline events against these people.** All are plausible for these two. Four need a line on the page so the reader believes them:

1. **Ch 08 to Ch 13, Dane's arc runs backward once, on purpose.** In Ch 08 he lets her make the tower calls against his own judgment; in Ch 13 he decides the program for her. That reads as a contradiction unless the page shows what changed in between: he watched her run into a burning church in Ch 11 and lost the argument with his own fear. Ch 11's interior ("the terror of watching her go in") has to be there in plain words, and Ch 13 should name New Bedford and the church in the same breath.
2. **Ch 16 s2, Beth hands Ned the wheel.** She has just learned he sold her. She'll do it only with one interior line of reasoning: he's the only other person aboard who can hold the boat in that sea, and he can't swim, so he won't put it on the rocks with himself, Tess and Russ aboard. Without that line, a reader calls it the comps' top complaint, a heroine's senseless choice in danger.
3. **Ch 05 s1, Ned at the counter hears everything.** In character for Beth: she would tell Ned straight out anyway, and she doesn't think of Ned hearing as a leak. That's her blind spot. It's still the line an attentive reader may notice (outline Pacing flag 8). My recommendation for this cast is to have Beth tell Ned directly, as she tells Tess and Russ. It's more like her, and it reads as her choice instead of a planted overhearing.
4. **Ch 10 s3, Beth's lie to Tess has to work.** Beth is a bad liar out loud. On the phone it works because her lie is short and she hangs up (line 5 above). The beats shouldn't give her a long, smooth lie.

**Choices for the author to confirm (defaults in bold):**

1. **The banned phrases against outline lines.** The book brief notes that "Okay," "For the record" and "I'd like it noted" conflict with the author's banned list.
