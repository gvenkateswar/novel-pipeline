# Ten Days Out: Full-Manuscript Reader Notes (v2)

Read as a Kindle Unlimited romantic suspense reader, in one pass, from manuscript.md only.

---

## 1. Verdict

**4 out of 5 stars.** (A reader shopping mainly for heat would give it 3.5.)

This is a tight, physical, very specific book. I believed the island, the boat and the weather completely. Beth's competence is the engine of the whole thing. Her bare hands on binders, the barometer readings and the counted steps across the bar make her one of the more convincing working-class heroines I've read in the genre. The suspense set pieces are excellent: the bar crossing on the flood, the bait shed shot, and the dark car deck. The prose is controlled and rarely tells me what to feel. Three things hold it back. First, the leak is flagged so early and so plainly that the middle third turns into waiting for Beth to catch up. Second, the heat is real but brief and arrives at about the halfway mark. Third, the style relies on a set of signature moves (hands-watching, "in order," ticking stoves, one-line chapter buttons) that you start to notice by Chapter 5. I would recommend it, and I would look for the author's next book.

## 2. Would I finish it? Where would I put it down?

Yes, I'd finish it. Chapter 1 hooked me inside a page: the sprung lid and Beth calmly pressing it shut is a great opening.

Where I'd be most likely to put it down:
- **Chapter 5, after the fish-house scene.** The long suspect inventory ("Friday it's three... From Saturday it's anybody...") goes back over who knew what, and I'd already worked it out. The paint-can list at the end of the chapter does the same job a second time. This is where my attention sagged most.
- **Chapter 3, the store scene.** It's long, it introduces six unnamed islanders plus Ned, Tess and Arsenault, and the ferry-cable retelling slows things down. I'd push through, because the "Tess knew I stayed" beat lands there.
- **Chapter 1's first two pages** could lose a reader who doesn't know what a binder or D-ring is. The jargon is part of the book's charm, but it's dense before I have any reason to care.

## 3. The romance

**Is the reconciliation after the notebook earned?** Mostly, yes. What makes it work is that Dane reports himself to Pike and Quist before Beth confronts him, and he names the costs without asking her for anything. The evidence that clears him is the best piece of logic in the book: the fish house was the one location he kept out of the log, and nothing ever came to it. "If it had been him, the fish house would have burned first" is a satisfying click. The scored-out line turning out to be *Subject and I* works too.

The weakness is that by the time Beth sits across from him in Chapter 8, she already knows it's Tess, because Ned has just told her. So the confrontation has no real danger in it. She isn't deciding whether to trust him. She's collecting an apology she has already half accepted. The reconciliation also happens in a single scene of about 1,200 words, and then they're straight on to tactics. I wanted one more beat of her staying angry about *assess*. She rules that "the parking lot's yours" and moves on very quickly.

**Does the ending land?** The Monday grand jury "Yes. I chose it." is the emotional payoff, and it lands hard, because it pays off the "you'll hear my answer first" promise. The epilogue works: Beth at the wheel, Ned asking the board without telling her, and the XtraTufs bought in Pruett "taken as the review." The "I love you" exchange at the binder is a bit abrupt and plain next to everything else, but the half-finished "Hand me—" saves it. The last line is good.

**Heat.** There are three scenes: hands-only in the fish house (Ch 5), full sex on the cot (Ch 6), and her own bed (Ch 10). The language is explicit and the POV is close, so it is genuinely open-door. For a book sold as "steamy," though:
- The first kiss is in Ch 4 and the first sex is in Ch 5, around the 50 percent mark. That's late, with little build-up of sustained physical tension beforehand. The Ch 3 shed moment and the Ch 4 hand-on-the-bag-strap moment are good but thin.
- Each scene is short, a few hundred words, and the Ch 5 one is interrupted by her suspect-thinking ("Someone had known exactly where to put a match").
- Every scene runs the same explicit consent check ("Don't make me give it twice," "You can stop it anywhere," "say so and I will"). It's fine once. Three times reads like a checklist.
- The scenes do change the relationship, which I liked: the "very disappointing kit" laugh, losing track of the door in Ch 6, and hearing her own voice out loud in Ch 10. The Ch 10 scene is the best of the three.

I'd call it a 3 on a 5-point heat scale. KU steam readers may call it "warm," not "steamy."

## 4. The suspense: when I suspected the leak, when I was sure

- **First suspicion, Ch 1:** Tess's float is lit after six on the one night of the drug run, and Tess's initials are on the bank bag. It's noticeable because the narration explains it away ("Cash after hours wasn't a crime").
- **Strong suspicion, Ch 2:** "The laugh came late," on the phone call where Beth tells Tess she's home, just hours before the latch is tried.
- **Effectively sure, Ch 3:** "*Tess knew I stayed.*" Beth thinks it in so many words, then pushes it away with the clock memory. From here on I wasn't guessing who. I was waiting for Beth.
- **Certain, Ch 6:** "Local emergency management" on the landing call, with Beth splitting kindling too hard. Ch 6's condom scene, where Tess never touches Beth, confirms it in hindsight.

**Did watching Beth resist it work?** Half. The psychology is clear and believable: "A man from away she could walk out on. Tess she would have to walk past every morning of her life." That line is the best justification in the book, and the Ch 7 opening ("If she had said a name on Wednesday, it would be a clean arm") shows the book knows her silence cost something. Taken as character tragedy, it works.

Taken as suspense, it frustrated me. From Ch 3 to Ch 8 the reader is a full step ahead, and the only real question is "when will she admit it." The Dane-as-suspect thread never feels like a live possibility, because the reader has seen him walk into the water for her. So Ch 7's walk-out comes across less as "is he the traitor?" and more as "she's running from the wrong person into the dark." That's tense, but it's the tension of watching someone make a mistake, not of not knowing. Russ is a decent red herring (the Jolene cousin reveal is funny), but he's cleared too lightly to count.

## 5. Does the leak's behavior make sense all the way through?

Mostly, with soft spots:

- **Motive before Thursday:** Tess says she first believed it was "scare you off... nobody hurt." But a man tried Beth's door at midnight on Day 1, the Ashby shed was burned on Day 4, and she still passed on the landing on Day 6. Keeping that belief through a fire is a stretch.
- **Her stake:** The ledger reveal (two winters of night diesel, three harbors, one mainland account) suggests she was a paid participant, not just someone being squeezed. The book never settles whether she was in it for money, from fear of exposure, or under threat. The threat to burn the *North Reach* with Ned aboard comes only on Thursday, and only in her confession afterward. Until then there's no lever on the page.
- **Why she helps at the end:** It works emotionally. "Water side—" cut off mid-word is a great moment, and the earlier held-key silence and "Okay, Bethie" set it up well. But it rests almost entirely on her after-the-fact confession, plus the reader's general sense that she loves Beth. We never see her choose. Seeing her once with Arsenault, frightened, would make the warning feel earned instead of just welcome. Right now it works because I wanted it to, not because the book proved it.
- **The $9,000:** It hangs together (envelope at 6:15 a.m., bag rides the morning boat), and the copier catching the pressed dents of her handwriting is a lovely detail. One question: Arsenault knew within hours that Beth specifically was the witness and how to frame her. The book implies the hooded man recognized her. That's fine, but it's never stated.

## 6. Plot logic that doesn't hold

- **The landing time going to local emergency management.** After two compromised locations, the Marshals' Ops calls the island's emergency director about a covert extraction, and Dane, a careful professional, says "Understood" without objecting. Beth knows exactly who that is and stays silent. Her silence is a character choice. Dane's acceptance is a plot convenience.
- **The notebook falling out.** All week Dane zips that pocket shut on the page. The one night it matters, it's left unzipped and drops open at her name. It's convenient, though the soaked-jacket staging helps.
- **The windshield at the end of the Ashby drive (Ch 5).** It's never resolved. Who was there, and who set the shed fire? I assume Doucette or Arsenault, but the book never closes it.
- **LifeFlight** is mentioned in Ch 2 and never discussed again as a way off.
- **The bait plan (Ch 8–9).** Dane's objection is correct: relief arrives by noon, and Beth's plan puts the witness and the case at risk. She overrides a federal deputy's tactical call and he gives in, and the plan only works because Tess warns them. I bought it emotionally (it's her ground), but Dane giving in that easily is the weakest of his professional choices.
- **Russ reaching Arsenault through the cellar bulkhead** is neat, but it means Arsenault spent the night in the store with Tess and a radio while a constable who suspected the store sat on the boat. Russ only goes because of the warning. Fine, but lucky.
- **Small things:** at 3 a.m. "The sea already allowed it," but relief arrives at noon. Dane's mother is dispatching "thirty years" in one place and "twenty-six years" in another (Ch 4 vs Ch 8). In Ch 8, his Lowell-bus anecdote follows his own previous line with no transition, so for a second I didn't know who was speaking. The cut-off "Not in front of—" at Pruett in Ch 1 is unclear (in front of Ned?).

## 7. Do the two leads sound different?

Yes, clearly. This is one of the book's strengths.

- **Beth** talks in clipped fragments with the weather in them. She drops subjects and gives instructions. Examples: "Wind's backed northeast. The glass is down four since breakfast." "Then ocean." "Left of me. Small steps."
- **Dane** talks in long, balanced, fully finished sentences, often with a dry turn at the end. Examples: "That's a narrow definition of waterproof." "It's a very disappointing kit."

The risk is that Dane's habit becomes a tic of its own. He often echoes back what she just said as a tidy summary: "So you wanted to see it twice before you put your name to it." "Then it was running Thursday, the same as always." "Then it's the people you told..." "So there's a stretch of the flood when..." After the fifth time it reads as a device. The side characters are distinct too: Tess (warm and profane), Ned (one-line statements), Russ (wry), and Quist (blunt, though thin).

## 8. Recurring tics and phrases

- **"In order" / "in the order":** Ch 1 (tide calendar), Ch 3 (to Russ), Ch 8 (twice: to Pike, then retold to Beth), Ch 9 (to Pike), Ch 10 (twice: the handover and the grand jury). This is the most noticeable one.
- **Watching hands, not faces:** Ch 2, 3, 4 (Russ's hands on the tailgate), 5, 6 ("He watched her hands, not her face"), 7 (same phrasing again), 8 ("laid out what he had, in hands"), 10 (twice). It's a character trait that gets overused.
- **Stoves, heaters and furnaces that "tick":** Ch 1, 4, 5 (three or four times, including "The stove ticked twice"), 8, 9, 10.
- **"Walk me through it":** Ch 4 ("Walk me through what the ferry pays"), Ch 5 (twice), Ch 9.
- **"Hiding gets noticed / Being seen is normal":** Ch 3 and Ch 5, almost word for word.
- **Tess's "Christ on a bike":** Ch 1, 6, 9. "Hell's bells": Ch 2, 3. These are fine as tags, but they arrive on cue.
- **Russ:** "as if [X] were optional" (Ch 3, Ch 10), and taking his cap off and looking into it (Ch 3, Ch 6).
- **Counted numbers:** eleven rungs, 34 steps, 14 steps, 41 to 63, "thirty-eight." It's a good motif, but it's everywhere.
- **"He didn't make her wait" / "She let it stand":** Ch 2, 3, 8.
- **One-line chapter and scene buttons:** "She didn't step out." (Ch 2), "His pen was still moving." (Ch 4), "She was keeping an exit again." (Ch 6, which restates the line before it), "He'd learned it." (Ch 10). Nearly every scene ends on a neat, quiet button, and by the midpoint that's the part that most sounds machine-polished.
- **"Not X. Not Y."** fragment pairs for emphasis, throughout.

## 9. The five changes that would most improve the book (ranked)

1. **Stop telling the reader it's Tess in Ch 3.** "*Tess knew I stayed*" hands over the answer at about 25 percent. Either keep Beth's realization off the page until later, or give Ned, Russ and Dane real, checkable evidence against them, so Ch 5–7 are suspense instead of waiting.
2. **Put Tess's coercion on the page before the warning.** Show one moment with Arsenault, or show Beth seeing Tess afraid, so "Water side—" feels earned instead of explained afterward. Settle whether she was paid or pressured.
3. **Make the heat arrive earlier and run longer.** Build more physical tension in Ch 2–4, give the Ch 6 scene more room, and drop the identical consent check in at least one of the three scenes. Move the suspect inventory out of the Ch 5 afterglow.
4. **Fix the convenient logic.** Have Dane push back on the landing coordination or have it explained. Resolve the windshield and the shed fire. Make Dane's surrender to the bait plan cost him more on the page.
5. **Cut down the tics.** Use "in order," hands-watching, ticking stoves and Dane's echo-summaries half as often, and let some scenes end mid-motion instead of on a button.

## 10. Is it clear what book 2 is about, and would I read it?

Mostly clear. Quist recruits Russ to work Tess's ledger ("three harbors... one account"), so book 2 is Russ and Quist chasing the money up the coast. Russ is the side character I liked most after Beth, and his "No. I'll have my traps out by Tuesday" while his hand closes on the envelope is a charming hook. What's less clear is whether book 2 is a *romance* between them. There's only one line of seasick-agent banter to suggest it, and Quist has had almost no interior life. A romance reader may not realize a pairing has been set up. Would I read it? Yes, for Russ and the island, with mild doubts about Quist.

## Best moment

The condom purchase at Tess's counter, Ch 6: "Good till next August... I'm happy for you. I'm not a charity." It's funny, warm and awful all at once. Tess checks the date like a mother would, adds the free Hershey bar, and never once touches Beth's arm. Reread later, it's heartbreaking. Close second: Chapter 1's "Lid's sprung. You'll lose your count."

## Book as a whole

- **Ending earned?** The romantic ending is, through Dane's self-reporting and the grand jury "Yes." The suspense ending is earned in action (the car deck is superb) but only partly in motive, because Tess's turn happens off the page.
- **Character I'll remember:** Beth, with her bare hands on the binders. Then Ned turning his hearing aid off at the news.
- **Where I'd most likely stop:** Ch 5's post-coital suspect list, then the Ch 3 store scene.
