# Manuscript Reader Notes: *Ten Days Out* (Chapters 1–10)

I read it cold, in order, in two sittings, the way a KU reader who grabbed it off a "romantic suspense, small town, Maine" carousel would.

---

## 1. Verdict

**4 out of 5 stars.**

This is a tight, specific, grown-up island thriller with a heroine who earns every scene she's in. The setting is the star: binders, bleed screws, half-tide bars, check-in nets, a town with no bank that sends its money across on the ferry. All of it is used, not just shown off. Almost every physical detail planted in chapter 1 (the eleven rungs, the loose binder that "broke ankles", the stern gate chain, the ring buoy, the ramp-light switch) gets paid off on the car deck in chapter 9, and that is very satisfying. The suspense plot is fair, but the culprit is easier to spot than the book thinks. The romance is believable but kept on a short lead: lots of hands that stop and breath at temples, not much talk between two people that isn't about the case. The prose is controlled and often excellent, but it runs on a small set of tics that pile up over ten chapters, and the hero thinks in nearly the same clipped cadence as the heroine. The ending is warm and earned on the suspense side. On the romance side it's slightly rushed. I'd recommend it, and I'd read the author again.

## 2. Would I finish it? Where would I have put it down?

Yes, I finished it, and I wanted to. Chapter 1 is a very strong hook: the sprung lid and Beth calmly pressing it shut ("Lid's sprung. You'll lose your count.").

The sag comes in **chapter 6, Day 5 and Day 6 (Tuesday and Wednesday morning)**. The ledger reading, the condom purchase, and then Beth pacing the bait shed back door ("fourteen steps") and rapping corrugated metal. Those scenes are obviously setup with no story pressure on them. The condom scene saves the Tuesday half. The Wednesday bait-shed walk is the moment my thumb hovered. If I were going to quit, it would be there, about 55–60% in. The landing ambush later in the same chapter pulls it right back.

A smaller dip comes at the end of **chapter 5**: the suspect-list conversation in the fish house goes in circles ("Three. And a counter nobody can name."), and it follows the sex scene, so the energy falls off a cliff.

Chapter 3's store scene is also long. Six islanders, Ned's bridge story, the booster, Arsenault. It's a lot of introductions in one room.

## 3. The romance

**Does it earn the ending?** Mostly. The suspense half of the ending is fully earned. The romance half (the "I love you" exchange on the rail, three weeks later) is earned in principle, but it comes in summary. The real reconciliation happens in chapter 8, in a very compressed sequence: she reads the notebook at 11 p.m., confronts him the next morning, and is in the bunk with him that afternoon. Her line "The parking lot's yours. You carry her. Not me." is the best line in the romance, and it does a lot of work. But the forgiveness comes so fast that the betrayal in chapter 7, which is the book's biggest emotional swing, feels like it gets settled on schedule.

**Where it works:**
- Chapter 4, the generator shed. Teaching him to bleed the line ("Your hands. Not mine."), the Lowell/Ponce backstory, the father who asks about the horse, "Once." / "She asked for ten minutes." Then the kiss, and him stopping because the door is behind him. "That's not no" is a good, adult refusal.
- Chapter 5, the bar crossing. Her fist on his belt holding him in the surge is the real love scene. The sex afterward works because of it.
- Chapter 6, the condom purchase at Tess's counter. It's funny, specific, and full of character ("I'd have asked for a receipt."). Of all the romance beats, this is the one I'll remember.
- "Come on the Friday boat." / "Which Friday." / "Any of them." That's a good HFN line.

**Where it's thin:**
- Before chapter 4, the attraction is almost all proximity beats: the towel, the shed light, his breath at her temple. They barely have a conversation that isn't operational. I believed they'd sleep together. I wasn't sure they *liked* each other until the horse joke.
- Dane has almost no flirtation or humor of his own after the boots jokes. "It's a very disappointing kit" is the high point, and it's in chapter 5.
- Three weeks are skipped between the grand jury and the final scene. Lowell at Christmas, the XtraTufs, and the "I love you" are all told to me rather than shown. I wanted one more real scene between them as ordinary people.
- Beth's "Hand me—" before "I love you" is cute, but it reads like a device.

**The heat, for a reader who bought it as open-door:**
- There are three scenes: chapter 5 (hands only, fish house), chapter 6 (full sex, the cot), chapter 8 (daylight, the bunk). Each one changes the relationship. In chapter 5 she chooses him knowing he'll report it. In chapter 6 she loses track of the door, and he tells her about Marisol. In chapter 8 they have sex "in the record," in daylight, with nothing hidden. That design is genuinely good.
- They're short, though: a page or two each, cut through with suspense thinking ("Someone had known exactly where to put a match."). For a reader who picked this up expecting real heat, I'd call it a solid 3 out of 5. Explicit vocabulary is used sparingly and a little abruptly ("clit" and "cock" each appear once, in otherwise restrained prose). I didn't feel cheated, but a spice-first reader would say the first scene is foreplay-only and the book is "open door, but short."
- The chapter 8 bunk scene is the best of the three: the upper berth too low, the arm on the rail, "I said after." The chapter 6 scene's best beat isn't physical at all. It's her not knowing where the door is.

## 4. The suspense

**Is the leak fair?** Yes, very. Every clue is on the page: the bank bag with Tess's initials (ch 1–2), Tess as emergency management director (ch 2), her laugh coming "late" (ch 2, 9), the check-in log written down before the back door was tried (ch 2–3), the cell booster in the back office (ch 3), Tess squaring the ledger (ch 3, 6), the blank lines after *Island Girl 3:52* (ch 6), and Tess no longer touching Beth's braid (ch 8). That last one is the best clue in the book. I didn't notice it was missing until Beth did, which is exactly how it should work.

**When did I know?** I was suspicious in **chapter 3** at "The booster. Of course." That line all but points at Tess. I was fairly sure in **chapter 5**, when Beth defends Tess at length ("If Tess were selling me, she'd have kept it in her head."). Defending a character that hard is the genre's own tell. I was certain in **chapter 6**, when Dane says "local emergency management" and Beth's narration explicitly decides "nobody in Portland phoned Tess." That line is the author underlining the clue for me. So the reveal in chapter 8 confirmed what I already knew instead of landing as a punch. The red herrings are weak by comparison. Ned's reefer lie is good. Russ (the cap, the short ring finger, the Jolene cousin) never becomes a real suspect, and the ring finger detail goes nowhere. Dane as the leak is the strongest misdirection because of the notebook, and that's where the book's real twist energy lives.

**Does the second half escalate?** Yes, steadily: the fire and the bar crossing (ch 5), the ambush at the landing with Dane shot (ch 6), the notebook and Beth walking out (ch 7), the confrontation with Ned and the reveal (ch 8), the trap on the car deck (ch 9). No slack after chapter 6.

**Does the climax pay off the setups?** Very well. This is the book's best structural achievement. Beth in wool socks on a dark deck she knows blind. Thirty-four steps. The welded patch from the winter her father died. A shin striking a binder. The stern gate chain at thigh height. The ring buoy bracket. Dane freeing himself one-handed from the binder she put his hand on before dark. Then she hauls the shooter out of the water instead of letting him drown, and the "gasp" lesson from chapter 5 comes back. Tess's "Water side—" over channel 9 pays off the *Ruthie C* call sign. Russ arresting Arsenault offstage is a little convenient, but it gives Russ his moment. The final turn of the night (ramp sodiums coming up "dull pink and then orange") is a great image.

## 5. The heroine

She's competent all the way through. That's the book's main pleasure. She reads weather, tide, gait, latches and people, and the plot runs on her knowledge, not on luck. She misses the clues about Tess, but the book makes that a point about love and habit, not a lapse in intelligence. The moment in chapter 8 when she has it ("Only one pair of hands closed that bag.") is earned.

Choices I didn't fully believe:
- **Chapter 7, leaving the fish house for Ned.** This is the big one. Her reasoning for leaving Dane is laid out carefully and I mostly bought it. But her destination is Ned: the one man she *also* has on her list, who held the Ashby key, and who lied about the reefer. The chapter even admits it at the end ("Ned had known the Ashby place."). She goes anyway, without a plan for what she'd do if Ned is the leak. For a woman who walks every way off a float before she needs it, that's out of character. It reads as "the plot needs her on the ferry."
- Also in chapter 7: switching off his alarm so he misses the Pike call. That guarantees Portland panics, and she's smart enough to know it.
- **Chapter 9, sending Russ (the only other gun) away and then going down to the dark deck in socks.** I believed the socks; it's set up beautifully. I believed sending Russ to save Tess. But Dane agreeing to use the grand-jury witness as bait, one day after being told "Act like it," stretched me.
- **Chapter 6, Beth dismissing "local emergency management."** That's the author steering her, not her own reasoning.

## 6. The hero

**Is he a person?** Yes, more than most romantic suspense heroes. Lowell, a father who drove a bus and came up from Ponce, a mother who was a police dispatcher, Spanish he sounds like a tourist in, the horse joke, the wrong boots that become XtraTufs, the knuckle scar ("A door. It won."). His habit of reading people's hands is a good, consistent trait. His POV sections (ch 8 open, ch 9 on the deck) are strong, especially "he would not fire into a dark she was somewhere in."

**Does the secret land?** The notebook works well as a twist: "Subj." all week, the upside-down second book, "Do not disclose to subject," the D6 line scored through. "She had been Subj. all week." is a gut punch. His explanation in chapter 8 is coherent (coaching taints testimony, and he doesn't trust himself to choose which rules to bend after Marisol). "Both were true. One went in the book." is a good line.

**Does the cost land?** Mostly. He's relieved on the pier and hands the notebook over himself, and his hand finds the empty pocket lining afterward, which is a lovely touch. Suspension, a letter in his file, a transfer. But the cost is delivered in summary in chapter 10 ("about where he'd figured"), and it resolves very neatly: the transfer he wanted comes through and he's riding the Friday boat within three weeks. Marisol Duarte is a strong idea that's underused. We get two lines and a parking lot.

One belief problem: he sleeps from late afternoon until 4 a.m., through her switching off his alarm and lifting the door bar. Fatigue and blood loss are offered, but a protection deputy sleeping that soundly bothered me.

## 7. Tess, Ned, Russ

**Tess** has the most distinct voice in the book ("Christ on a bike," "I'm happy for you. I'm not a charity," the free Hershey bar). Her betrayal hurts because we've had the braid-tug, the hot chocolate the day Beth's mother left, and channel 9. Her redemption beat, warning Beth mid-word over the radio, is the emotional peak of the climax, and "Okay, Bethie." after the held carrier is devastating.

**Does she get her due?** Almost. Her confession in chapter 10 is short and moving: the board under the booster shelf, "I add it twice," "They said they'd only scare you." But her motive is thin for the scale of what she did. She passed on an extraction time knowing armed men were waiting, and the tank loan alone doesn't quite cover that. She and Beth never get a real scene alone after the reveal; "You warned me" is all Beth gives her. That restraint is probably right for Beth, but Tess is the character I most wanted ten more minutes with. The Ned/Tess unspoken relationship ("Tess doesn't count," the foot of cold air between their sleeves) is beautifully underplayed.

**Ned** has real weight. Silent, lying about the boat's business to keep the winter run alive ("Not a damn dime."), handing over the freight book, turning his hearing aid off at news. Him telling her "Take her off" in chapter 10 is a quiet, earned payoff. The hearing aid gesture is overused, though (see tics).

**Russ** is the lightest of the three. Good lines ("make sure the summer people's pipes freeze in an orderly way"), a nice arrest, but he spends most of the book as an underpowered red herring. Then the last page suddenly makes him the book-2 lead with the envelope. I wasn't prepared to care about him that much.

**Quist** shows up late and is sharp ("I did. Now I don't."). The "cleanest ledger" line is good.

## 8. Prose

**At its best,** it's spare, physical, procedural writing that trusts the reader: the binder routine in chapter 1, the latch that "lifted and waited" in chapter 2, the bar crossing in chapter 5, the car deck in chapter 9. Chapters 1, 5 and 9 are the strongest-written.

**What reads as AI or mechanical:**
- **Neat closing lines on almost every chapter and scene.** Ch 1 (switches off the light), ch 2 ("She didn't step out."), ch 3 (boots toes-out), ch 4 ("His pen was still moving."), ch 5 (reads it upside down), ch 6 ("She was keeping an exit again." This explains the line right before it), ch 7 ("read his own handwriting," which is also the chapter's title), ch 8 ("began to report"). The rhythm becomes predictable by chapter 4.
- **Explaining the feeling after showing it:** ch 5 ("What went through her wasn't his name. It was that…"), ch 6's last line, ch 7 ("She'd taken that for tenderness."), ch 8 ("Rage would have fed it to the stove. Squared, it said…").
- **Fragment stacks** used for emphasis everywhere: "Steel. Lit. A fixed VHF…" (ch 7), "Gravel. The lane. Fourteen steps." (ch 6), "Boots. Bags." (ch 5).

**Recurring tics (with chapters):**
- Hands that rise and **stop** short: ch 2 (his hand on "lot"), 3 (hand came up and stopped), 4 (fingers went still), 5 (hands at her hem, stopped), 8 (an inch short), 9 (Tess's hand at the braid), 10 (Tess's hands an inch toward Ned's sleeve). It's the book's main gesture and it gets worn out.
- **Palm flat** on a surface to read it: ch 1 (reefer, the wall), 2 (window glass), 4 (granite), 5 (his breastbone), 6 (shed wall), 7 (his chest), 8 (table), 9 (console, deck plate), 10 (his chest). This one is deliberate and pays off in chapter 10, but it's too frequent.
- **Breath at her temple / warm breath:** ch 3, 4, 5, 7.
- **"Put it where she put things" / "filed it":** ch 1, 2, 3 (twice), 6.
- **Barometer tap with a reading:** ch 1 (twice), 2 (five times), 8.
- **Ned turns off his hearing aid:** ch 1, 3, 6, 7, 8 (halfway, then leaves it on), 10. The variations are clever, but six times is too many.
- **Russ takes off his cap, looks into it, puts it back on:** ch 3, 4, 6.
- **"Right side clear":** ch 2, 4 (twice), 5 (twice).
- **Dane looks at people's hands, not faces:** ch 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10.
- **"Walk me through it":** ch 5 (twice), 9.
- **"You were right about the water" / "I'm not arguing":** ch 2, 6.
- **Counting** steps, strokes, seconds: every chapter. It's a character trait, but it's the default way scenes get filled.
- **"The way she'd [nautical task]" similes:** ch 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8.
- **Tess's "Christ on a bike":** ch 1, 6, 9. Tess's late laugh: ch 2, 9.

**Where the two leads sound alike:** Dane's dialogue and his POV narration use Beth's exact cadence: short declaratives, repeated noun lists, no contractions in stressful moments. "Knees. Ninety seconds. Then ocean." (ch 5), "Lane. Back door. Look first." (ch 6), "Arm down. Hand soft." (ch 7). His POV in chapter 8 inventories the room the same way hers does ("One door, landward… The loft hatch shut."). With the names covered, I couldn't always tell whose head I was in. The Lowell material is the only place he has a voice of his own. Ned, Tess and Russ are well differentiated by comparison.

## 9. Continuity errors I noticed as a reader

- **The money questions and "hands on me."** Ch 7 says he asked about money "with his hands on her," and in ch 8 Beth says "Your hands were on me. You were asking about the money." In ch 4 the money questions come while they're bleeding the generator, before any touching. The kiss comes after. Either her memory is distorted on purpose (it isn't signaled) or it's an error.
- **Ned's surname "Haskell"** appears for the first time inside Dane's notebook (ch 7, "Key via Haskell"). I stopped to work out who it was.
- **Russ's surname "Whitcomb"** first comes from Dane (ch 5) without Beth ever having said it.
- **Ch 10 handover calls Beth's house "the cottage on the Harbor Road."** It's been a Cape all book. Minor, but noticeable.
- **Ch 10 final scene:** "only the three of them" (which three?), and Quist says "you know every boat in this bay by its exhaust note" while handing the envelope to Russ. That's Beth's line from ch 5. I couldn't tell whether the hook was aimed at Russ or Beth.
- **Ch 10:** Dane, freshly removed from the case and reporting to Portland, is in the courthouse lot reading the indictment to the witness. Plausible, but it sits oddly with the IA referral.
- **Ch 10:** the suspension is apparently served and the transfer granted within three weeks. Fast.
- **Ch 4:** Russ's short ring finger is noticed pointedly and never used.
- **Ch 1:** "Not in front of—" breaks off and is never resolved (Ned? Tess?).
- **Ch 7:** she leaves around 11 p.m. and reaches the ferry at 2 a.m., with one hide behind the church in between. The time gap felt long for the distance, though hiding may account for it.
- What *did* hold up, and impressed me: the dates (Thursday Nov 12 → Sunday the 22nd → grand jury Monday the 23rd), the tide times drifting later across the week, the notebook entries matching the times in earlier chapters, and the boots.

## 10. The five changes that would most improve the book (ranked)

1. **Fix the logic of Beth's escape in chapter 7.** She runs to Ned, whom she suspects as much as Dane. Either give her a reason Ned is safer that she can actually check, or send her somewhere that fits her own rules. This is the only point where the heroine's competence breaks.
2. **Stop underlining Tess.** Cut or soften "The booster. Of course." (ch 3) and Beth's explicit dismissal of "local emergency management" (ch 6). Let Russ or Ned carry more real suspicion, so the reveal in chapter 8 surprises instead of confirming.
3. **Slow the reconciliation and dramatize the HFN.** Give the ch 8 confrontation more room before the bunk scene. Replace some of the three-weeks-later summary in ch 10 with an actual scene between them, so "I love you" lands in a moment instead of a montage.
4. **Give Dane his own voice.** Break his fragment-list cadence in dialogue and in his POV chapters. Let him be warmer, funnier, or more verbose than Beth somewhere other than the Lowell anecdote.
5. **Thin the tics and neat endings.** Cut the stopped hands, hearing aid, cap, palm-flat, breath-at-temple and "put it where she put things" repeats by at least half. Delete the explanatory last lines (ch 6's especially). And clarify who the final book-2 hook is aimed at.

## 11. Would I buy book 2?

Yes, I'd at least sample it. The world and the island are the draw. "Three harbors. One account." is a real hook, but it's fuzzy about whose book it is. What I'd want: either Russ as the lead (a constable whose family is in it, with Quist from away as the love interest and the mainland account as the case), or Beth and Dane again with the defense lawyer using the relationship against her at trial. Whichever it is, I want Tess's story continued. Her ledger is literally the evidence, and she's the character I want back most. I would *not* buy a book 2 that's just another Beth/Dane protection plot on a different island.

---

## The book as a whole

- **Did the ending feel earned?** The suspense ending, absolutely: the car deck is the best chapter. The romance ending, mostly. It's right, but it's told more than shown.
- **Character I'll remember:** Tess. "Okay, Bethie." over an open carrier. Then Beth, and the image of her in wool socks on a dark steel deck.
- **Where I'd most likely have stopped:** Chapter 6, Day 6 morning, the bait-shed pacing scene.
- **Best moment:** Chapter 1, the sprung lid. Beth sees fentanyl under live lobsters, says "Lid's sprung. You'll lose your count," presses it shut, cinches the strap and raps the trailer twice. It's the whole character in one paragraph.
