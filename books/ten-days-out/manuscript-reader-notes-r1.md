# Manuscript Reader Notes: Ten Days Out (revision r1)

**Draft read:** `books/ten-days-out/manuscript.md`, Nov 6 revision r1, 17 chapters. I read the whole file in one sitting and nothing else in the book folder.

---

## Verdict up front

- **Would I stop?** No. I finished it, and the last four chapters pulled harder than the middle.
- **Where I would most likely have stopped:** Chapter Eight, at the phone calls from the tower. Beth tells Tess, Ned and Russ where she'll be for the second time, right after the first time burned a house down around her. If I'd quit, that's where. My second likeliest stop is Chapter Twelve, which is all walking interrogation, phone calls, and a hold explained over speakerphone.
- **I'd keep reading, but:** the middle (Chapters 5 to 10) runs one loop. Hide somewhere, a few trusted people learn where, a night attack, a flight over snow or rocks in socks or bad boots, a new shelter, sex. It happens at Russ's house, the north-side house and the beach, and the dentist's house is a softer version. By the third pass I could feel the machine turning.
- **Buy:** yes, for a friend who likes snowbound romantic suspense with heat. I wouldn't hand it to someone who wants a twisty mystery.

## Where I got bored

- **Ch 2:** "The truck was clean when the troopers stopped it. Cole's cold storage on the Pruett wharf has been hosed down. All we have is a little powder in the cracks of one crate. Her eyes are the case." It's a briefing read aloud. The boss is a voice with no face and no name, and she does this all book.
- **Ch 5:** "Three islanders know it: Tess Marr, Russ Whitcomb and Ned Haskell. I want the phone records for all three." From here on the book keeps a running count of who knows where she sleeps, and the characters recite it to each other. I started skimming those lists.
- **Ch 6:** The paragraph that starts "Tess Marr had walked her home from every dance and every wake..." followed by the Russ and Ned paragraphs. It's the suspect list recapped as three matching paragraphs. I already knew all of it.
- **Ch 8:** The phone calls. Tess, then Ned, then Russ, then the boss reading the names back. Same beat four times.
- **Ch 8:** The generator false alarm. I knew it was nothing, and it ends on a Connecticut joke.
- **Ch 12:** Ned's confession as a walking interview: "Why my Thursday?" / "The truck was clean because you called him." / "Did you know he'd send somebody to my door?" Question, answer, question, answer. The emotion in Ch 11 was huge; here it turns into a deposition.
- **Ch 14:** "The bag was dry and the battery near full. Friday night's spray had iced everything else on this deck. Tess had watched the harbor from the store steps all Saturday..." I had to stop and work out what day it was and why it mattered.
- **Ch 15:** The kid's speech up the ladder ("It was Cole. Through Cole's lawyer. The lawyer paid me. He met me himself, in Portland, in his car, behind the bus station...") is the plot tied off in one breath.

## Where I got confused

- **Ch 2, the skiff timeline.** The ferry docks at Pruett and the kid walks off. Beth gets home. A skiff leaves Pruett at 10:30, she calls at midnight, and the door gets kicked in at three. I had to work out that the ferry went back to Cutter after Pruett. I got there, but it took a second pass.
- **Ch 2:** "Six hours, and I'm already 'the witness.'" Nobody on the page had just called her that.
- **Ch 4:** "Good." / "Russ went out to that call," she said. Two lines in a row and I couldn't tell who said "Good."
- **Ch 5:** The kid lived on the island two summers in Cole's trailer and worked Cole's boat, and "nobody ever asked his name," and Russ "never once saw him walk on land." On a 240-person island I didn't buy it. It reads like a reason to keep him nameless.
- **Ch 10:** "The key's on the ring in my pocket. It went in the sea with me. Saturday I'd have had to walk you out the east road in daylight..." I couldn't parse what this was explaining.
- **Ch 11, the knife.** "The blade came out over Beth." Was Ned going to cut the bandage, or was he there to kill the boy? The chapter frames it as a threat, and the book never says. Cole's later text ("go finish it") makes me think it might have been murder, but Ned brought gauze and his good blanket. I still don't know, and it matters, because it decides how I feel about Ned at the end.
- **Ch 12, the September photo.** Cole asked Ned who'd have the boat after him, so the kid took Beth's picture "so I'd know the girl on the boat." Why would Cole need a teenager to know her face two months before she'd seen anything? The book raised this as a big mystery in Ch 5 and 6, and the answer felt thin.
- **"Late" / "Later than that."** It took me until Ch 17 ("she looked at Russ, and he looked at her") to be fairly sure Russ and Tess are together. That might be on purpose. I just wasn't sure I was meant to get it.
- **Ch 14, days.** Friday night the ferry is held off. Saturday morning comes the repair talk. Then "the ham in the oven by three," the bait truck "by eight," and departure "at ten." I had to work out that the ham was Sunday. "While he held the light for her at the co-op vise" on Saturday also confused me, since the rod was already back in on Friday night.
- **Ch 15:** The father's name, Alton, first appears in Ch 15. He's been "her father" all book. When Ned said "Alton's way" I had a beat of "who?"
- **Grand jury vs. jury.** Dane keeps imagining "twelve strangers" and "a jury box," but the room in Ch 16 has about twenty people. I think he's picturing the trial, but it blurred.

## Where I stopped believing

- **Ch 5:** Dane says "Nobody hears where you sleep tonight. Nobody." Ten minutes later Beth announces it in the store, and he tells his boss on speaker on the steps. Then in Ch 8, after the first leak burned the house, she tells all three the beach hour. The boss calls it "the test," but a marshal using his only witness as cheese, with a Marine Patrol officer shot as the result, gets one line of reckoning in Ch 12 ("a man got shot in the shoulder at three"). That isn't enough.
- **Ch 4, Russ:** "Late wasn't a time. Russ always gave you a time, to the minute." That trait arrives exactly when the plot needs it.
- **Ch 8, the boss's "And me."** She was never a suspect for me. She has no name, no face and no motive. It's a chapter button, not a suspicion.
- **Ch 13:** Ch 12 sets up the pantry-door pencil line, and two chapters later it saves them. Planted late and paid fast, so it felt convenient. Ch 15 still works because Ned is at her shoulder for it.
- **Ch 16:** Shot along the ribs before midnight, carried off on a stretcher, and by early afternoon Dane has signed himself out, is sitting on a cold bench, and then has daylight sex with her on top. It's genre licence, but I noticed it.
- **Ch 16, the lawyer:** "I hear the marshal's been keeping you warm." That's stock villain dialogue, and he walks into the courthouse where he's about to be arrested. The "I'll want my coat." / "You've got it on." exchange was a nice callback, though.
- **Dialogue no person would say:**
  - "A jury doesn't get a vote on who kisses me. I do. I voted." (Ch 3, too composed for a woman mid-kiss)
  - "It's the one thing my job can still do for you, and I've done it." (Ch 12)
  - "Her eyes are the case." (Ch 2)
  - "So you're the cheese, and I'm the man standing next to the cheese with a gun, and we're calling that a plan?" (Ch 8, funny, but this is the fourth or fifth line in the same construction)

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Gloss after gloss.** The book stops to define plain things in apposition: "the wheelhouse, the little room up top where he steered"; "the float, the floating dock below the stone pier"; "the car deck, the open deck where the trucks park"; "the island's constable, its one part-time police officer"; "The grand jury... is a room of ordinary people who decide..."; "the Marine Patrol boat, the state's marine police"; "the ledges, the rocks just under the water at the harbor mouth"; "the upper deck, the open roof behind her"; "The rod from the wheel down to the rudder, the steering blade under the boat." One or two would be fine. This many make it read like it was written for someone who isn't trusted to keep up. It's the most noticeable tell in the book.
- **Every chapter ends on a one-line button:** "I know a place." / "Hold my hand and do exactly what I do." / "And me." / "Which one?" / "Why?" / "They've got a boat." / "Dane took the rifle to the back window." Cliffhangers suit the genre, but all seventeen hit the same cadence.
- **Stated feelings:**
  - "She loved him. She had known it since the rock... Now it sat under her breastbone, warm and much too big." (Ch 8)
  - "under every word was how much he loved her, and that whoever walked down that road tonight would be somebody she loved too" (Ch 11)
  - "that was a terrible thing for a marshal to know about himself" (Ch 3)
  - "He was in trouble. He knew it right then" (Ch 1)
- **Tidy irony lines:**
  - "Three of them would die for me, she had told him yesterday. Now it sounded like what a woman says right before she finds out she's wrong." (Ch 6)
  - "He had said it first, into his boss's phone on Saturday, and this was the bill." (Ch 16)
- **The voices mostly don't sound alike, which is a real strength.** Dane is wordy and jokey with long clauses, Beth is terse and in charge, Ned talks weather, Tess says "Calloway" and quotes prices, and Russ answers his own questions ("Can he stay? He can stay."). The one voice that slips is Dane's: his jokes lean on the same construction until it becomes a tic (see the listener pass).

## Repeated lines and images a listener would notice (listener pass)

On audio these would stand out, in rough order of how loud they are:

1. **"his boss."** The boss is never named, so the phrase repeats dozens of times. Every phone call is "his boss said." On audio it becomes a drone.
2. **Ned's weather deflections.** "Wind's still coming round" (2), "It'll blow two more days" (5), "Wind's going round to the north" (8), "Sea's going down" (9), "Wind's going round to the west" (11), then "Snow's set in for the day," "Wind's going round west by dark," "Snow's letting up" and "Wind's backed west" all in Ch 12. It's devastating in Ch 11 and worn out by the end of Ch 12.
3. **The kid's tracks:** "one clean print, then the long smear," "the bad foot plowing a line," "a heel dragging," "firm, then a scrape." I counted ten or more appearances. It's the right signature for the villain, but it gets described the same way every time.
4. **"bad ___."** Bad hip, bad knee, bad foot, bad leg, "my bad arm now," "his bad right knee." Every character's injury is "bad" something.
5. **Grabbing a collar or coat to save someone.** Ned hauls Beth by the collar (2), Beth hauls Dane by the collar (7), Dane's fist in her coat on the tower (8), Dane hauls the kid by the hood (10), Dane's fist in her coat in the ditch (12), Beth comes up the step on two fistfuls of his coat (13). It's a motif, but six times reads like the book only has one rescue gesture.
6. **The report joke.** "that I could put in a report" (2), "When this goes in a report" (2), "which will look great in my report" (8), "Is that going in my report too?" (8). Plus Dane's "..., which ___" add-on jokes: "which wasn't the plan," "which tells you about my day," "which I'm going to hold against you," "which isn't how they teach it," "which is harder than it looks with a rifle."
7. **Cole's lawyer "in a good coat" telling "twelve people from Maine."** Ch 3, 5, 11, 14 and 16. Same imagined scene, same phrasing.
8. **"politely and slowly" / "polite and slow."** Dane's "Ms. Calloway" voice, at least three times.
9. **"I'm asking" / "Ask me."** "Ask me again in ten days," "Ask me in five days," "I'm asking for it," "Can I kiss you? I'm asking," "Will you stay? I'm asking," "Ask them," "Ask me every night." It's the book's theme, and by Ch 16 it's being hammered.
10. **"flat."** She said, flat; her voice went flat; hand flat on his chest; palm flat; fists flat. Very high frequency.
11. **Standing "between her and" something.** The window, the trees, the door, the field. Constant.
12. **Gray faces.** Ned's face is gray (1, 2), Russ is gray in the face (4), Dane's face is gray (6), the kid is "young, scared, and gray" (10).
13. **"I want you."** It's said or thought by both of them in almost every chapter from 2 to 16. Desire gets stated more often than it gets shown.
14. **Consent check-ins.** "Can I?" "Still yes?" "Tell me you're sure." "Tell me." "Beth?" "Will you come to bed with me?" Good that it's there, but it falls into the same rhythm in every scene.
15. **"since she was six" / "since she was nine."** Russ, Tess, the knot, the hatch.
16. **"the one light"** for Mercer's boat, many times in Ch 13–15.

Callbacks that **worked** on the ear: the boots ("for a different ocean" leading to "Those are the right boots" and "Full"), "Dollar says" in Ch 6 and Ch 17, "the other side" in Ch 14 to 16, and "Are you any good at your job?" leading to "Ask me every night."

## Whole-book questions

**Is the romance obvious and satisfying?**
It's obvious from the first scene, maybe too obvious. Both of them say "I want you" almost every chapter, and they're in bed by night two. What makes it satisfying is the banter and competence: she reads machines, the sea and him; he talks to keep her steady; each of them saves the other in a way that costs something. The hand opening on the rock in Ch 7 is the real turn, and the book is smart to have her name it as the moment ("I have since the rock, when you opened your hand"). I believed they love each other. The courtship rests on danger and desire more than on getting to know each other. I learned little about Dane beyond the boat, the bus-driver father and the mother who worked nights, and there's one hook that never pays off ("It was the nicest thing anyone had said to him in years. He didn't tell her why."). Each sex scene is shaped by its situation (cold loft with no condoms, skinned palms in the dark, turning around so he can watch the door, daylight), so they don't feel interchangeable. The Ch 10 door beat is the best of them.

**Do the breakup and the repair feel adult and earned?**
Mostly, yes. The conflict is a real values clash: he goes around her to keep her alive, and she won't give up her life for safety. He tells her to her face before a judge can. Neither of them is stupid or sulky. "I'd lose you to keep you alive." / "You will." is a real cost. The repair is adult too. He withdraws the hold, puts "I'm involved with her" on the record himself, and they trade terms ("you ask me. Every time." / "Then ask fast." / "When I think you're wrong, I say so"). What's thin is *why* he changes his mind. Nothing new happens except that he watches her hold a wheel with the skin coming off. That's a feeling, not information. I accepted it, but he flips on a single image. The breakup also lasts about three days in the same rooms, so it's more a cold spell than a separation. For a novella that's fine.

**Does the danger move and have a face?**
The kid is a great face: the scared thin face, the split lip, the dragging heel, washing her dishes one-handed at her sink, nineteen years old with a name at the end. The danger moves, closing in every night. Cole, the actual villain, is a man in a truck cab in Ch 1 and is never seen again. The lawyer gets one lobby scene. Mercer arrives in Ch 13 of 17 with a gold tooth and a new float coat, which is borrowed shorthand, though "That coat's never been wet" is a great line. So the danger has one face for most of the book and a second one late. The middle loop also means the danger escalates in scale but not in kind until the boat shows up.

**When did I first suspect the traitor?**
- **First flicker, Ch 1:** Ned's "Then you do what's right, from your own kitchen" and him moving her out of the window. On a reread both are clearly him.
- **Real suspicion, Ch 6:** when Beth lists the three, Ned's defence is only physical ("That hip couldn't get him up the harbor road without a rest"). Meanwhile Tess and Russ get loud, genre-standard red herrings: the phone turned face down, "Late." Loud red herrings point away from themselves, which pointed me at Ned.
- **Sure, Ch 9:** "My boy in Portland's on that stuff, Bethie." Motive confirmed. After that the only question was whether he'd break.

So the reveal isn't a surprise, but it is a heartbreak. Ch 11 works because I knew. The suspense of who walks down that lane is replaced by dread, and dread suits it.

**Does the ending pay off?**
Emotionally, yes. The boots, Ned's letter ("No sorry. Nothing asked"), Tess writing the board and still refusing to speak to her ("The rest isn't in it"), and Dane asking the island if he can stay ("I've had worse juries") all feel earned. On plot it's an HFN with loose threads: Mercer is free, someone on Cutter planted the phone, the trial is in spring, and Cole is never confronted on the page. The planted phone reads like a sequel tag. It's intriguing, but it also partly undercuts Ned as the sole betrayer, and I'm not sure the book wants that. The grand jury vote arrives by text on a bench, so the legal climax happens offstage. That's on purpose, but a little deflating after a book that made "her eyes are the case" the stakes.

**Which character will I remember?**
Ned. A man who sells the girl he raised for his addicted son, wades into water too deep for him to stand in front of her, walks an hour through a blizzard to tend the boy sent to kill her, and later cuffs himself to the rail without being asked. Tess is a close second ("She'd save your life and charge you for the gas").

**What I'd tell a friend:**
"It's a snowed-in Maine island, a ferry deckhand who saw a fentanyl load, and a funny marshal in the wrong boots. It's steamy, with four or five open-door scenes. The best parts are the sea: a wave on the rocks and a night run through a gap in the ledges. You'll guess the traitor, but the reveal still hurts. The middle repeats itself a bit, hide, get found, run, so push through chapters 8 to 10. The ending is warm and the last line is good."
