# Ten Days Out — Full-Manuscript Cold Read

**Draft read:** `books/ten-days-out/manuscript.md`, revision round 1, 30,375 words. I read it start to finish in one sitting. I opened nothing else.

Locations are given as chapter, scene and an approximate percentage through the book.

---

## Would I stop?

No. I finished it, and I wanted to. If I had stopped anywhere, it would have been **Chapter 8, the wheelhouse scene (about 77%)**: "County rang Tess Wednesday," through "Ned's the boat." It's a game of passing keys back and forth. Who holds the float-box key, who holds the store key, which ring hangs on which nail, and Tess's radio cutting in on top. It was the only stretch where I skimmed and then had to go back. Second most likely place to stop: **Chapter 10, Quist questioning Tess (about 92%)**. That came from confusion, not boredom.

**I'd keep reading, but:** the last chapter is weaker than the six before it. The climax lands in Chapter 9. Chapter 10 then explains things, gets through a lot of procedure quickly, and closes on a nice image.

## Boredom ledger

- **Ch 2, the pier before the bridge falls (about 11%).** "Calloway. Net's tonight. Aboard or ashore for the roll?" Tess writing names on her hand, "Like the dog," the D-battery price. Charming once. I was waiting for something to happen, and luckily the tower did.
- **Ch 3, store after the fish-house chase (about 22–24%).** "Mop's a dollar. Floor's free." / "Restroom's customers only." / "Coffee's two. Buy one, you're a customer." Three Tess price jokes in a row. Then the blocking: "Beth and Dane went out the front, round to the side wall. Russ stayed inside at the counter… Dane stopped at the front corner, where he could see her and the moorings and was too far off in the wind to hear." I was tracking where people stood instead of what they wanted.
- **Ch 4, opening of the bleed-screw scene (about 30%).** "It had run dry and sucked air, and the last of the fuel can was in it now. She had the bleed screw cracked a quarter turn." Brief. The man appearing on the drive pulled me back hard.
- **Ch 5, Tess's hand and the 22:40 entry in her book (about 37%).** I lost track of what Tess was proving and how. I'd forgotten the ten-second flashlight from Chapter 2. That's my fault partly, but the scene relies on it without reminding me.
- **Ch 6, the impeller swap (about 54%).** "He got the first bolt in, then the second." The mechanics go on too long. Ned's "I thought it was shorts, Bethie" rescues it.
- **Ch 6, second sex scene (about 56–58%).** Not boring exactly, but I knew how it would go. See AI tells below.
- **Ch 8, wheelhouse key deduction (about 77–78%).** The worst stretch. "Town ring hangs by her safe. Second key to the float box is on it." / "That ring has hung there since before she bought the store…" / "Ned Haskell's phone works in one place on this island, the store's back step, and he carries his own key to the door beside it. Who has a key when she's out?" I skimmed, lost the logic, and went back.
- **Ch 10, Quist and Tess (about 92–94%).** Q-and-A about impellers, truck keys on a back step and a key that never left the nail. I couldn't follow what each question proved.
- **Ch 10, grand jury corridor (about 97%).** Rushed rather than dull, but my attention dropped because nothing was at stake by then.

## Confusions

- **Ch 1:** "Hundred and ninety. The place on Route One that sells fudge." It took me a beat to realize she's pricing his boots. Good once I got it.
- **Ch 1 to Ch 2 seam:** Chapter 1 ends with him heading for the back of the house with his hand on his hip. Chapter 2 opens with "She had sent Reyes out through the mudroom to look at the back steps." So was the hip-reach a threat or an errand? The cliffhanger lets the air out of itself.
- **Ch 3:** "They waited out the afternoon in her kitchen with the door locked. The north road went up over the spine of the island in the dark…" Two sentences and we're suddenly outdoors at night. I had to reread to see that time had jumped.
- **Ch 3:** The phone line "*Firm at thirty-one. Saturday noon.*" I worked out it was the boat, but only because I remembered the Webbers Cove. It was a good misdirect.
- **Ch 4:** "She gave him the *Ruthie C*, a picnic boat now." "Gave him" means she told him the story, but it reads for a second like she handed over a boat.
- **Ch 6:** "What about Tess?" / "I sent the fire up." It took me a second to read "sent up" as "reported."
- **Ch 7:** "They missed the one they'd paid." I don't know who this means. Her, because they planted the money on her? Or Dane, as if he's the one bought? I read it three ways. It's meant to be a big line, and it fizzled because I couldn't parse it.
- **Ch 7:** The notebook shorthand. "*D5 0150 report. No indication.*" / "*D6 am. Q: CU written rpt. Pouch: two bags. Seal noted: red.*" I decoded it, slowly. The narration does decode it, but I had to hold the code in my head for a paragraph first.
- **Pike's gender.** I pictured Pike as a man until Dane says "whatever you tell her now" in Chapter 8. Not a problem, just a jolt.
- **Ch 8:** "It's pencil," Dane says, when she writes Tess's name. Does he mean she can erase it, or that pencil doesn't count as an accusation, or that he won't carry it for her? I liked the moment but couldn't say what it meant.
- **Ch 9:** The "Where's your cop" logic. I followed it on the second pass: she'd said on the air she was alone, so only someone who knew Russ was sent would expect a cop. But it goes by fast and is half-explained in the store.
- **Ch 10:** "An impeller bearing your price sticker was taken from that shelf at noon on Monday. Was it missed?" I don't know what this proves. That Ned was in the store Monday noon, when the truck key went onto the back step? I had to build that myself, and I'm not sure I built it right.
- **Ch 10:** The ferry sits at Pruett with nobody to take it home while she's in Portland. So the island is boatless again? Kenny's "Who's taking her home?" seemed to say the problem matters, and then it was dropped.
- **Ch 10:** The time jump to "The Friday before Christmas week" is clear, but the line about the district office ("The week after Portland she had walked into the district office herself and asked for the boat") arrives as a summary. I wanted to see it.

## Where I stopped believing

- **The bridge falls and the hull cracks on the same night (Ch 2).** That's the premise machine and I accepted it. But Ned saying "Ten days" right there felt like the title stepping onstage.
- **"Steel doesn't burn. Everything in it does." (Ch 2).** The plant is so neat I knew there'd be a fire on that deck. It paid off in Chapter 9, but I saw it coming from seven chapters away.
- **Homeland Security keeping hands off the dragger after a deputy marshal is shot (Ch 7–8).** A smuggling boat, a hunted federal witness, an arson, a shooting, and the order is still "observe only" so the buyer runs. The book argues it, but I didn't buy that nobody, the Coast Guard or anyone else, comes for a shot marshal. The lifeboat just turns around and goes home.
- **Beth leaving a wounded, sleeping Dane behind a door nobody can bar from outside (Ch 7).** She knows men are hunting the island house by house, and that he can't lift his arm. Anger, betrayal, fine. But she's the most careful person in the book, and she walks out into the snow. The open Pelican case is a nice touch, but it doesn't make the choice believable. I believed she'd go. I didn't believe she'd leave him like that.
- **Arsenault in the store (Ch 6).** A drug runner paying with folds of hundreds in front of the federal witness and making a veiled threat in public: "A store like this lives on the boat." He's too careless for a man running a two-year operation.
- **The hood walking the island bare-faced in daylight (Ch 4).** "He didn't care who on the island saw that face." The constable is on the hill watching, and nobody acts. Russ "staying off that boat" is funny, but island constables don't let an unknown arsonist stroll around.
- **Ned's confession in the store (Ch 9).** It comes out clean and in order, with Tess cutting in on cue ("Mine are yellow, Haskell"; "Save it for Whitcomb"). Someone who has betrayed the girl he raised for nine thousand dollars wouldn't talk in tidy installments like that. And "So the teller had written down the store's bag, and the red on it was Ned's" explains it to me as if I hadn't got it.
- **Quist's dialogue (Ch 10).** "That will need to be repeated, Ms. Marr. To which question is the answer being given?" / "Goddamn," Quist said to the page. Nobody talks like that. She's the one character who sounds written.
- **The dash to the boat (Ch 10).** "I've given him twice the limit." A stock rom-com sprint to the departure. Cute, borrowed.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Run-on "and… and… and" sentences at every climax.** The ramp (Ch 1), the bank slide "and the snow went out from under her and she was on the weed and…" (Ch 7), the *Teresa Marr* sentence (Ch 8), the blacked-out deck crossing (Ch 9). Each works on its own. By the fourth I could feel the device switch on.
- **Sensation coming up through the feet, over and over.** "Diesel through her soles." / "the idle came up through the floor into her." / "Its hum came up through the floorboards into Beth's feet." / "The idle came through the water and the steel into her spine." / "the diesel coming up through the soles of her boots." / "the cold came up into them from the steel." Six or seven times. It's a tic now.
- **Every section ends on a quiet gesture button.** "She bent to the next coil." / "She found the blanket on the planks by feel and pulled it back up round her shoulders." / "He held out a hand for the rag." / "He didn't look back." / "She reached across him and set the bad arm straighter on its pillow." Almost every scene break lands the same way: a small physical action, no dialogue, a held breath. Elegant, and so regular I started predicting it.
- **Dane narrates under fire, every time.** Chapter 2 cable ("That's my knee by your hip, sorry about the knee"), Chapter 2 kitchen, Chapter 7 shot ("First time all week I've done what you said…"), Chapter 9 deck ("I'm getting up. I've changed my mind about getting up. I'm here."). The first was delightful, the fourth was a formula. The narrator tells me so outright too: "a man who talked to anybody who'd stand still."
- **Dane's politeness formula.** "Ms. Calloway," then something "very polite," then "I'd be grateful if…" and "I'd like to be wrong." "I'd like to be wrong about it from a distance" (Ch 2), "would like to be wrong" (Ch 5), "my guess, and I'd like to be wrong" (Ch 7), "he'd have given this boat and the trailer to be wrong" (Ch 10). The narrator keeps announcing it: "Very polite." / "as politely as he'd said anything all day" / "slow, and very polite."
- **Tess only speaks in prices.** "Wrong's free, Marshal. Right goes on your tab." / "Mop's a dollar. Floor's free." / "Coffee's two." / "Nine fifty a gallon, and I'm not sorry." / "Four seventy-nine. Good till next August." / "Free with purchase." Great character idea, but it becomes the only note she has until her lip gets split.
- **Repeated catchphrases.** "Nobody but me and God" (three times). "Ned's the boat" (three). "Glass is going down/up" (nearly every Ned entrance). "Bethie." These read as deliberate, and some pay off ("Ask me sober"), but the volume makes them feel installed.
- **Everyone on the island talks in the same dry quip.** Ned: "Well. That's the bridge." Russ: "I'm not a complicated man." / "I said I'd look for one… Found one." Tess: price jokes. Beth: fragments. They're funny people, but it's one sense of humor in four mouths. Russ and Ned in particular are hard to tell apart by voice.
- **The three sex scenes share a skeleton.** A consent exchange ("Do you want—" / "Yes." / "Do you?"; "Yes," before he could ask; "Here?"). She gives orders ("There. Like that. Slower." / "Again." / "Harder." / "Now. Yes. Now." / "Look at me."). One-word replies from him. Cutaways to the room ("The stove ticked," "The drum ticked down," "Downstairs the refrigerator came on"). She comes first, he comes second, then a quiet "After." By the third I knew every step. The humor beats (the "very disappointing kit," the jeans that "have taken a side," the stuck sweater) are what keep them alive.
- **Narrated neat interpretations.** "The county would ring the island. The island had one person to ring." / "Witness, on Pike's line. Subject, in his pencil." / "She had heard men say yes." Some of these are strong. Together they're a habit of telling me the point right after showing it.

## Listener pass (heard, not read)

**Works aloud:**
- Fragment strings in suspense: "Gravel. A right boot. The left heel after it, dragging. Nearer." That would be terrifying in audio.
- The mudroom knock passage in Chapter 2. The repetition ("and nothing, and nothing, and nothing") is made for the ear.
- Dane's under-fire monologues work better heard than read.
- The long run-on climaxes carry well spoken.

**Fails aloud:**
- **The notebook entries.** "*D5 0150 report. No indication.*" / "*D6 am. Q: CU written rpt.*" A narrator reading "D-six a-m, Q colon C-U written r-p-t" will lose every listener.
- **Radio channel numbers.** "nine," "sixty-eight," "thirteen," "one-six and one-three." On the page I could just about keep "nine" as Beth and Ned's channel. In audio "Bethie. Water side." on nine will not register as the shock it's meant to be.
- **Nautical jargon in clusters.** Binder, D-ring, brow, dolphin, transfer bridge, sill, gut, riprap, forepeak, impeller, bleed screw, painter, transom, quarter ladder. Readable in print. Heard at speed, it's fog.
- **"He" ambiguity.** In scenes with Dane, Ned and the hood all present (Ch 4 shed, Ch 9 deck), "he" switches referents without names. Readers can glance back; listeners can't.
- **The unsignalled POV switches to Dane** (Ch 9 lane scene, Ch 10 final scene). On the page the paragraph break and "Dane sat in the dark lane" just about do it. In audio, after nine chapters of Beth only, the listener won't know whose head they're in for several sentences.
- **Quist's scene in Chapter 10.** Fast untagged Q-and-A between Quist, Tess and Beth. In audio I would not know who said "Never left the nail."
- **Long no-dialogue-tag stretches in the Chapter 8 notebook argument.** It works on the page because the lines are short. Aloud, with a single narrator, Beth's "My bed." / Dane's "I went to bed with you" would need very good voicing.

## Predictions

**When I first suspected Ned, and how sure I was:**
- **Ch 1, about 3%:** Ned takes both bags, the fare bag and the store's, and tells her to go home. A plant, I noted. **15%.**
- **Ch 2, about 16%:** "Nobody but me and God." It's too cozy. Whoever is the island's grandfather in a book like this is suspect by genre law. **30%.**
- **Ch 3, about 25%:** Ned gives her the Ashby key. Next chapter the hood is checking Ashby. **50%.**
- **Ch 4, about 33%:** Ned walks up to the shed right after the hood leaves it, fuel cans in hand. **60%.**
- **Ch 6, about 58%:** Dane says "Nobody. Not Ned." Beth asks Ned about the float anyway. At the end of the chapter the float lights are dark. **85%.** From here it was mostly confirmation.
- **Ch 8, about 78%:** Dane lays out the case. The book then tries hard to unconvince me with the ham, the yearly proposal and Beth writing *Teresa Marr*, and it partly works. I wobbled to about **75%**. Then "He didn't look back" put me at **95%**.

**Did Tess work as a red herring?** Partly. The hundreds in her vest (Ch 2), the truck, the ledger, the hand-writing, and "Don't you look at my book like the bank does" gave her real shadow. But she's so loud and on the page that she never felt like a hider. Ned did.

**Did I suspect Dane?** Briefly, in Chapters 2–4 (the phone glow on the step, the door trick only he'd been shown). The *Log daily / Do not disclose* text made me think he'd be compromised rather than crooked. That was right in a way I liked.

**What surprised me:**
- The **red seal** mechanism. The red tag Beth ties on Dane's wrist in Chapter 4 ("Now you're the boat's"), the teller's "Seal noted: red," then "Mine are yellow, Haskell." That's the best piece of plotting in the book, and I didn't see it.
- **Ned warning her on nine.** I expected a clean betrayal, and he turned out to be a man who went one step past what he could live with.
- **"No indication" at the foot of every page.** I was braced for the notebook to be damning, and it was quietly a love letter.
- Tess's own book of Lyle's margins. She'd been building a case the whole time.
- **"Tell him yes."** I did not expect the proposal running joke to land in the middle of an arrest. It hit. It's also a touch much.

**What I want to know next (book 2):** Who was at the wheel of the *Dorothy M.*? Does Dane survive the Arlington referral? Does Beth hold the boat? Does Tess visit Ned? The *Dorothy M.* is the only open plot question, and it came too late (Chapter 10) for me to care about it. Ned and Tess pull at me more than the series hook does.

## How the ending lands

Mostly earned. The real emotional ending is **Chapter 9's last image**: Ned holding his wrists out across the counter "where he had bought his coffee since Beth was in diapers," and the safe dial going round behind the curtain. That's the climax, and it's strong.

Chapter 10 then does four jobs (Quist, the love scene, first boat, grand jury) plus a coda, and the middle two are the weakest. The love scene and the "I love you" / "Since when?" / "You jumped" exchange are sweet and set up from Chapter 1, but the scene felt owed rather than needed. The grand jury answer, "The captain. Edward Haskell," is a great small payoff on the bags from page one, and then it's over in a paragraph.

The coda works. Ned's gloves she won't wear and can't give back, the dollar bet she finally wins, *Pier Pressure*, the ham ("there are two of us" / "Three"), and her hand giving the binder its last quarter turn, which echoes the plumber's binder in the opening paragraph. That symmetry made me smile. Switching to Dane's POV for the last scene is a choice I'd accept if it had been set up. It wasn't, so it reads as a cheat to show me Beth from outside.

## Would I buy book 2?

**Probably yes, conditionally.** I'd buy it if Beth and Dane are in it, or if it's Russ and Quist on the task force and the island stays the setting. The *Dorothy M.* alone wouldn't pull me. It's a boat I met eleven lines before it became a mystery. What I'd come back for is the island's voice and Beth at the wheel.

---

## Book as a whole

- **Did the ending feel earned?** The resolution of the betrayal: yes, fully. The romance: yes, mostly. The trust arc (the notebook, Giglio, "No indication") is set up and paid. The last chapter is overstuffed.
- **Character I'll remember:** **Tess Marr.** Prices for every feeling, names inked on her hand, "Ask me sober, Haskell," and "Tell him yes." Ned is a close second for the gloves and the wrists. Beth is the best-built character, but Tess is the one I'd describe to a friend.
- **Where I'd most likely have stopped:** Chapter 8's wheelhouse key deduction, about 77% in. Not a likely stop, given how close the climax is, but it's the softest point.
