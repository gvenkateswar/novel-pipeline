# What happens in the first three chapters

**Chapter 1.** Ellen Daly is fifty-eight. She spent thirty-one years guiding planes into Boston until the government retired her with a sheet cake. Six hours ago she bought Kettle Lanes, a ten-lane candlepin alley in a New Hampshire lake town that empties out after the summer people leave. At 5:15, as she's re-labeling the light switches, a tall man in a hardware-store jacket knocks on her front door. He is holding a key to it. He knocks anyway.

He is Ray Gagnon. He has opened this door on every league night since 1996, and he isn't handing the key over: the woman who gave it to him can ask for it back. Ellen calls the only locksmith in town. The locksmith, his son tells her cheerfully, is his father, who is standing in her doorway.

Then the night goes wrong in public. The league heckles her lane calls in fake pilot-talk. Her ceremonial first ball hops into Ray's lane and flattens the pins he was saving for his last shot. The machine on lane 7 dies mid-match. Ray has kept these machines alive for twenty years, and he informs her it's not his alley. At the snack bar, Ellen quietly asks Arlene, the 84-year-old who sold her the place, whether she could have a month's grace if it came to that. No: Arlene's room at the retirement home goes to the next name on the list if she isn't at the door with her money on March 1. At closing, the counter woman shows Ellen a sheet from the hardware store where the town is betting on the date she'll fail. The terms are written on it. Ellen owes $250,000 on March 1. The bank will only lend her that if the winter makes $30,000. If it doesn't, she has to sell, and Ray gets first crack. Every date is taken except March 1, and Ray won't buy it.

**Chapter 2.** From Ray's side. Ellen walks into his hardware store at 7:30 and plays him a recording of her broken machine. He names the part with his eyes shut while the old men by the coffee pot call it like a ballgame. Then he kneels at her door and changes her lock, cutting his own key out of her building. While he's down there he makes her an honest offer: he'll buy the place for exactly what she paid, so she walks away whole. Her daughter calls in the middle of it and tells her to take it. Ellen tells Ray no. This is the one thing in her life she picked. "Then I'll see you March first," he says. That night she leaves the back door unlocked so he can fix lane 7 for the Thursday league. The next morning she makes him take her money and gives him a key of his own, on one condition: he writes down every visit.

**Chapter 3.** A nor'easter traps thirty-one bowlers in the alley overnight. Ellen runs it like a shutdown at a busy airport. She hands out sleeping spots in order and charges for bowling, which the plow-truck crew will not forgive her for. Then the power dies, and she and Ray end up in the dark behind the lanes, setting pins by hand while the room heckles them. He tells her about his late wife's record on the wall. She tells him about the one night in her career she nearly lost two planes. Then he asks her for one thing. The league championship, which carries his wife's name, stays the way it has always been. "It stays," Ellen says, "unless the numbers say otherwise." The room roars at a strike, and she watches him miss the second half. She doesn't say it again. At four in the morning she writes down what she did, and files it. At dawn, digging out the alley's half-dead neon sign, they kiss. Then the plow's headlights come up the drive.
