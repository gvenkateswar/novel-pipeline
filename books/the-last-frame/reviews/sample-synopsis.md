# What happens in the first three chapters

**Chapter 1.** Ellen Daly is fifty-eight. She guided planes into Boston for thirty-one years until the government retired her with a sheet cake. This morning she bought Kettle Lanes, a ten-lane candlepin alley in a New Hampshire lake town that empties out once the summer people leave. At 5:15 a man knocks on her glass front door. He has her key in his fist, and he knocks anyway.

He is Ray Gagnon, the widowed hardware man who has coached the alley's league for thirty years, and he isn't handing the key over. They argue in the doorway with snow coming in past him. He's taller than she'd planned for, and to her annoyance she'd like him to stay right there and keep arguing. He wants her to hear it from him before she hears it at the hardware store. She sold her house to buy the place, and Arlene, the old owner, is carrying the rest: a quarter of a million dollars, due March first. Arlene would only agree to that if Ellen signed a paper. If Ellen can't pay on the day, the building goes to Ray that afternoon. "You know what you signed." She does. "At what it's worth," he says. "Not a dollar under. I'm not buying your mistake cheap." Then he goes in past her to coach his league.

She calls the only locksmith in town to change the locks. His son answers: the locksmith is standing in her alley. Then her ceremonial first ball hops into Ray's lane and flattens the pins he was saving. He walks it back to her himself and doesn't let go of it right away. "Nice arc. Wrong lane." She laughs before she can stop herself, and she catches him looking at her mouth. At closing, the woman at the counter shows Ellen the betting sheet from the hardware store, where the town is wagering on the date she'll fail. Every square is taken except March first. Ray won't buy that one.

**Chapter 2.** Now from Ray's side, and he's in trouble from the moment she walks into his store and pulls off a glove with her teeth to play him a recording of her broken machine. He names the part with his eyes shut while the old men by the coffee pot call it like a ballgame. Then he kneels at her door and changes her lock, cutting his own key out of her building. She holds the flashlight close enough that he can feel her breath on his wrist.

While he's down there he makes her one honest offer: he'll buy the place for exactly what she paid. But it means putting up the store his father nearly lost once, and he'll ask the credit union one time only, Thursday at five. She tells him the bank will lend her the money the day this winter makes thirty thousand. "Nobody's made thirty in this building since 1979." Her daughter calls in the middle of it, and Ellen swears to her that the only money in the building is the house. Ellen tells Ray no. The next afternoon, at two minutes to five, she walks into his store, puts one of the new keys on his counter without a word, and the two of them watch the clock go past five.

**Chapter 3.** A snowstorm that was supposed to be over by nine traps thirty-one bowlers overnight. The town's plow boss had warned her at three o'clock, and she went with the forecast. Now she has to own it, so she runs the night like a shutdown at a busy airport and, to the plow crew's lasting fury, charges for bowling. When the power dies, she and Ray end up in the dark behind the lanes setting pins by hand while the room heckles. He tells her about his late wife's record on the wall. She tells him about the one night in her career she nearly lost two planes. Then he asks her for one thing: the championship that carries his wife's name stays the way it has always been. "It stays," Ellen says, "unless the numbers say otherwise." The room roars at a strike, and she watches him miss the second half. He's looking at her as if she's given him something. She doesn't say it again. At dawn, digging out the alley's half-dead neon sign, they kiss. Then the plow's headlights come up the drive.
