# Chapter One: Cleared to Bowl

The deed had carried Ellen's name since nine-forty that morning, and by twenty to four Arlene's tape was coming off the switches in curls.

A row of ten lane-light toggles ran along the inside lip of the counter, each wearing a scrap of masking tape gone the color of weak tea, with a number in green ink and nothing else. Ellen peeled them left to right. Under number 6 she found an older scrap, and under that a third, in a hand that wasn't Arlene's.

She wrote new ones in block capitals, five columns, the way she had once written flight strips: lane, time, league or open, count, remarks. 04 / 1830 / LG / 4 / —. By lane 7 she had the spacing right. By lane 10 she wanted to redo the first three.

Beside the shoe cubbies she screwed a square of pegboard to the paneling, dead level, and pushed one index card onto a peg at eye height. TUES LEAGUE / 1830 / 40 / ALL LANES.

At 4:30 the vestibule door opened, and somebody stamped salt off a pair of boots, four times, hard.

Denise Lavoie came around the end of the counter in a parka over a red polo faded pink at the shoulders. She slid a folded newspaper and a ballpoint pen onto the shelf under the register, crossword up. Then she put on a pair of cat-eye readers and bent to the switches.

She read all ten, then the foul row, and straightened.

"What's 'remarks'?"

"Anything that doesn't fit in the first four columns."

"Three's got a dash."

"Lane three has no remarks."

"Lane three's got a dead spot on the approach, and the settee's got a crack that'll pinch you right through your slacks. Ask Bev Dubois. She's a ninety-four, and she won't sit on that settee anymore, she stands the whole night like she's waiting on a bus." Denise folded the readers. "But sure. Dash."

"Noted."

"Uh-huh." Denise looked at the pegboard. "That's new."

"Since ten past four."

"Forty." She tapped the card with one finger. "You count the subs?"

"Forty is the number Arlene gave me."

"Arlene gave you forty because forty's round and Arlene likes a round number. Some nights it's forty-three with the subs. Forty-four if Gerry Pelletier's hip is better, which it isn't. Arlene know you're doing all this?"

"Arlene signed six hours and fifty minutes ago. She's at the pharmacy. You could ask her."

"I've been asking Arlene things for twenty-two years, and what I get back is a wall. A wall with a cardigan on and a tissue up the sleeve." Denise hung her parka on the hook behind the cubbies and looked Ellen over the long way, from shoes to haircut. "So who's working the fryer while you label things? Because it isn't me. I've got the counter and the shoes, and that's two hands' worth already, and the phone makes three. Thursdays I anchor the Lakeview Diner on top of it."

"I am. From six-fifteen."

"You ever run a fryer?"

"I've read the manual. Twice."

"Twice." Denise reached past Ellen's elbow and found the lane 4 toggle by feel, without lowering her eyes. "These've had numbers on them since before you had that haircut. I could do them in the dark. Some Thursdays I about have."

She flipped it. Down the building, the lights over lane 4 hummed and came on, and Denise went to start the coffee urn without waiting to see what Ellen would say.

The FAA had picked her shifts, her airspace, and finally the month she stopped. This building was the first thing she had ever chosen on nobody's schedule but her own. She had come in on a Sunday in September for a cup of coffee, because the sign out front said ETTLE LANES and FOR SALE BY OWNER, and she had stayed two hours watching Arlene run a nine-year-old's birthday party with a pencil, a bad hip, and no list at all. Twelve children, ten lanes, one adult who could not keep up. She had driven home already doing the sequencing in her head.

That week she read up on candlepin, which was the only kind of bowling anyone up here seemed to mean by the word. The ball was four and a half inches across with no holes. You got three balls to a turn, which they called a box up here, and every pin you knocked down stayed on the lane where it fell, for your next ball to use or trip over. Forty people in one room on a league night, every one of them needing to go somewhere in order. Somebody at the counter to put them there.

* * *

Three knocks on the door glass at 5:15, a second apart.

Ellen came around the counter. Through the glass she saw a pickup parked by the sign pole and a tall man on the step in a brown canvas jacket, snow on his cap and sleeves. GAGNON & SON was stitched over the pocket. His right hand was closed at his side, and an orange paper tag showed between two knuckles.

She opened the door. Cold came in low across the linoleum and went for her ankles.

"Mr. Gagnon."

"Evening." He didn't move off the step. "I open on league nights."

"Arlene mentioned it."

"Lights and coffee. Tuesdays and Thursdays." He looked past her, down the length of the lanes, and back. "Since ninety-six."

Ellen put out her hand.

He considered it. The fist stayed where it was. "She gave me this herself."

"Arlene gave me the building."

"Sold you."

"Yes. Sold me. The key comes with it."

"If Arlene wants it back, she can ask. She knows where the store is."

"The only thing Arlene is going to ask me for, Mr. Gagnon, is the quarter of a million dollars on her note."

He looked at her for a moment, and then at the snow starting to collect on the sill between them. "She'll ask on time."

"You can wait inside," Ellen said. "You can't open anything."

He wiped his boots on the outer mat, left and then right, and stepped over the sill onto the rubber mat in the vestibule. He went no farther.

At the counter, Denise uncapped her pen. "Ray. You've got half the lot on your hat. Cold out?"

"Some."

Arlene's rotary phone sat on the corner of the office desk, close enough to reach without losing sight of the mat. Ellen looked at the stitching on his jacket and dialed.

"Gagnon and Son." A younger voice, level.

"This is Ellen Daly at Kettle Lanes. I need a locksmith. Rekey all exterior doors. There are three."

On the other end, a fingernail tapped glass. "Ms. Daly, the locksmith's my father."

She didn't answer.

"If you need him sooner than tomorrow," the voice went on, "try your front door."

Ellen looked at the vestibule. Ray Gagnon was standing on her mat with his hands at his sides and his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, examining the corkboard of lost mittens.

"Say again."

"The locksmith," the voice said, at exactly the same speed, "is my father. Ray. Six-one. Brown jacket."

"He's here."

"Figured. This is Danny." More tapping. "Kettle Lanes. Those doors are Schlage, I think, unless Arlene's still got the old Corbin on the bulkhead, in which case I'm bringing a cylinder, because nobody's made that pin since Reagan. Rekey, three exterior, eight a.m. tomorrow. Three keys a door. More?"

"No. Three."

"Three a door. Invoice to Kettle Lanes, attention E. Daly." He said it the way he would type it, punctuation included. "Anything else?"

"Nothing."

"Good luck tonight." Danny hung up first.

At the counter, Denise's pen made one short stroke.

Ellen took a fresh index card and printed LOCKSMITH / 0800 / GAGNON / EXT DOORS. She pushed it onto the pegboard under the league card, at a tall man's eye height, in line with the mat.

Ray read it from where he stood. He didn't need to lower his glasses.

"He's punctual," he said.

* * *

The PA hummed for two full seconds before it would carry a voice. Ellen let it hum, bent the gooseneck down, and counted the room. Forty bowlers at the ball racks and on the orange settees. Two boys at the snack bar waiting on a parent. Denise. Herself. Forty-four.

"Good evening. Tuesday league." She took the pairings off Arlene's list in order, with two seconds of air between each. "Lanes one and two. The Legion and Lakeside Plumbing."

"Roger, tower," somebody called from lane 2.

"This is the front counter," Ellen said. "The tower is the steeple on Main Street. Lanes three and four. Ambrose Pharmacy and Taylor's Marina."

From lane 3, through cupped hands: "Tower, say our altitude."

"Five hundred and twelve feet above sea level. It's on the survey."

Somebody on 3 said, "She looked it up." Somebody else said, "Course she did."

She read the rest down to nine and ten. The leadoff on lane 4, a man in a Bruins jacket, stood at the line with a ball in his hand, looking back at the counter as though he expected something.

"Lane four, cleared to bowl."

He bowled. So did everyone else, without waiting for her, and the wood went down the length of the building in a rattle like a silverware drawer pulled out too far.

From the far end: "Kettle Tower, requesting a hot dog."

Ellen leaned to the microphone. "Hot dogs are on the roller at the snack bar. Relish is in the squeeze bottle. You'll need to walk over."

"Five out of ten," Denise said from the register. "That's Paulie Roy on ten, he's a ninety-one, he's been working on that one at my counter since Thanksgiving. He's done better. Not lately, but he has."

A big man in a blaze-orange watch cap came to the counter and waited until she looked at him. He had a white beard trimmed square and glasses mended with electrical tape. He took the cap off and studied her over the top of it. Then he put it back on.

"Hollis Pike. You fly?"

"No. For thirty-one years I told the people who do where to go. Then I turned fifty-six and the government gave me a sheet cake."

She was fifty-eight now, two years into a retirement she hadn't chosen. The cake had been marble.

He nodded once, as if she had shown him a valid license. "Arlene threw the first ball every opening night since 1971. Tonight's yours. You going to throw one?"

"I've never bowled."

"Think Arlene ever hit anything? Fifty-five openings. Head pin twice. I kept a count." He looked down the building, past the middle pairs, to the tall man in the brown jacket at the line on lane 6. "Lane five suit you?"

"Lane one is closer."

"Lane one's got the Legion on it. Ray's on six." He said it the way he might give a wind speed. "You want him to hear about you, or see you?"

Lanes 5 and 6 belonged to the Ambrose Fire Auxiliary and Gagnon and Son. On 6, Ray stood at the line with his third ball, studying what was left: three pins standing toward the back and one lying across the deck at an angle that looked arranged. On 5 the machine had set a fresh rack.

The big man waved the Auxiliary bowler back to his seat and put a house ball in Ellen's hands.

The ball was the size of a grapefruit and heavier than it looked. Sixty feet of honey-colored maple ran away from her shoes, dented near the line. The pins at the far end were as thin as their name. She swung her arm back and let go late and high.

The ball came down well past the painted line ten feet out, struck the boards once, and jumped the gutter with a crack like a dropped plate. It ran into lane 6, where it went through Ray's three pins and the fallen one he had angled across the deck. All of it went into the pit.

For a second nobody made a sound. Then forty bowlers did, and her ears went hot.

Her face would be showing nothing. On the worst night of her career she had sounded perfect on the frequency, and nobody in the room knew anything was wrong until she wrote it up herself. Half this town gave her until spring. If they were right, her face would look exactly like this up to the morning it happened.

Ray stood at the line on 6 with his third ball in his hand, looking at an empty deck.

Ellen walked back up to the counter and bent the gooseneck down. "Lane six, stand by."

When she came back down the building, the big man and the Auxiliary's captain stood over the scorer's table between 5 and 6, and a tiny woman in a lavender parka zipped to the chin stood with them, a three-ring binder held against her chest.

"Touched down fourteen feet out. Four past the lob line," the big man said. "Crossed into six at about thirty-five feet. Verna, is that interference?"

"Hollis, it's a ball in Ray's lane." The Auxiliary captain pointed down 6. "Call it what you want."

The woman in lavender looked at the empty deck, then at Ray, then at Ellen. "The secretary rules interference. Lane six will re-rack and re-bowl the box in full. Lane five's ball is a lob and is not recorded." She wrote one line in her binder and shut it.

On 6, the machine set a full rack. Ray bowled the box again from the beginning. Three balls took down seven pins and left nothing lying where it could help him. He walked to the scorer's table and wrote the 7 into the box, pressing hard enough that Ellen could see the groove from where she stood. He looked at no one.

Ellen opened her mouth. The whole league was listening. She closed it.

Ray came back up the approach and passed her on his way to the settee. "Lob line's ten feet out, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms.," Ellen said.

He sat down. Behind him, the ball she had thrown came back up the return on 6 with a long hollow rumble, rolled to the end of the rack, and stayed there. Nobody on either team reached for it.

* * *

At 8:10, in the second string, Ellen was squaring the house balls on the rack behind lane 8 when lane 7 lost its rhythm.

She had been timing the machines since six-thirty without deciding to. Seven seconds a cycle, never more than seven and a half. Lane 7 took nine. Its next cycle took eleven, and the sweep paused at the bottom of its stroke before the table moved at all.

Ellen took out her phone and started recording.

The next ball took out the headpin and two others. The sweep came down and the table followed, slower than it should have, and stopped twelve inches above the deck, canted to one side, pins scattered underneath. The motor hummed, then quit. A smell drifted up the approach like an iron left face down on a shirt.

One by one, the four bowlers on 7 turned around on the settee and looked at the counter.

She stopped recording, walked up the aisle to the PA without hurrying, and bent the gooseneck down. When she spoke, she used the voice.

"Lane seven is unable. Lane seven, you'll finish on lane eight with your opponents. Bring your sheets."

They brought their sheets. Somebody on 3 laughed, and then didn't.

She threw the breaker marked PINSETTER 7 in Arlene's green ink, took the flashlight from under the counter, and went through the steel door by lane 10.

At lane 7 she put the light on the table where it hung, the belt gone still, one pin against the curtain at the back of the pit. She looked for a full minute and touched nothing.

Arlene's card file sat at the end of the counter, a tin recipe box with a rooster on the lid. Under M, one card. MECHANIC, in green ink, and below it R.G. and a phone number.

Ray was on the 5–6 settee with a pencil behind his ear. Ellen stood in front of him and held out the card.

"Lane seven. Second string, eight-eleven. The table stopped twelve inches above the deck on its way down. The sweep is down. The motor ran four seconds after the table stopped, then quit. There's a burned smell at the back. I cut the power at the panel. I touched nothing."

He read the card. He didn't take it. "Arlene's mechanic was me."

He took his key ring out of his jacket pocket and turned it over once in his palm. The keys jingled against the orange tag. Then he closed his fist on the ring and put it away.

"Not my alley," he said.

Beside him, Hollis studied the ceiling tiles.

"Understood," Ellen said, and folded the card into her vest pocket.

Ray stood and walked to the spare rack by lane 1 and back. Going by the trophy case he touched the glass with one knuckle, without turning his head. Ellen saw where: the center shelf, a trophy with a brass plate reading LINDA GAGNON MEMORIAL and beside it a framed card, WOMEN'S HOUSE HIGH SINGLE, 187, LINDA GAGNON, FEB. 1998.

He sat down and took the pencil from behind his ear.

Ellen stayed on her feet at the counter. Denise watched her from the register.

"Nine lanes," Ellen said. "And seven gets its lineage back." Lineage was what each bowler paid the house for a night's three strings, seven-fifty a head.

"That's the Grange team. They'd have finished on eight and said thank you. They say thank you to the plow guy when he buries their mailbox. You start handing back money on night one, and every time a table hiccups from here to March somebody's going to be standing at my counter with their hand out."

"They paid for a lane. They got half of one."

She sat down on the stool, opened the register, and counted it out into three small piles. The fourth bowler on 7 was the league secretary, and the secretary bowled free.

Ninety days until March 1, when Arlene's note came due. The first of them would close below zero.

She took the piles down to lane 8 herself and put each one into a hand.

"You don't have to do that," said a man in a plaid hunting jacket.

"It's done."

At the scorer's table, the tiny woman with the binder had watched all three piles go by. Her name was printed at the top of the score sheet: V. TIBBETTS, SEC. She opened her binder and uncapped a pen.

"Let the minutes reflect," she said, writing, "that on the first of December the house refunded lineage for mechanical failure. The secretary is aware of no precedent since 1979."

* * *

At 10:55 the lanes were dark and only the counter lights were on. Denise worked down the last row of rental shoes with the spray can, toe and heel, two counts each, and passed sentence on every pair.

"You'll live. You'll live. These, I'd burn."

Ellen squared the score sheets and fed the night into the adding machine, refunds and all. The last line came out in red.

"How bad?" Denise said.

"Below zero."

"How far below?"

"Far enough that the machine changed colors."

Denise set the spray can under the counter, zipped her parka, and took out her phone. She thumbed through it and turned the screen toward Ellen.

The photograph showed a sheet of paper tacked to a wall behind a cash register. The sheet was hand-ruled into squares, one date to a square. Most held initials. Across the top, in marker: KETTLE LANES. NEW OWNER. HOW LONG?

"Honey," Denise said, "this town's had a pool on you since word got out Arlene was selling. I'm just the one holding the sheet."

"That's the hardware store."

"Best foot traffic in town. Everybody's in there for salt this time of year, and they stand in line ten minutes with nothing to look at but that wall. It filled by lunch."

"How much is a square?"

"Five dollars."

"Who has tonight?"

"Nobody had tonight, which I'll be honest surprised me. Gerry almost took it and then went with the twelfth, because that's his anniversary and he's sentimental. Most people figured you'd make it to New Year's." Denise scrolled with her thumb. "You want to know who's got Christmas Eve?"

"Negative."

Denise looked at her over the cat-eye readers. "Normal people say no."

"I said no."

"If you miss," Denise said, "you have to sell, and Ray gets first crack."

"I'm aware. I signed it."

Arlene had made it a condition of the sale, recorded with the deed: if Ellen ever sold, Ray could match any offer she accepted.

Ellen took the phone out of Denise's hand, put two fingers on the screen, and spread them. She walked the photo down to the bottom right corner. The last square said MARCH 1. Nothing was written in it.

"Ray won't buy one," Denise said.

"Why not?"

"Ask him. It's his wall." Denise took the phone back and put it in her pocket. "Back door first. Arlene always did the back door first, don't ask me why, she just did, forty years."

"I have a list."

"Course you do." The vestibule door shut behind her, and the cold came across the floor and went away again.

"One," Ellen said, to the building.

Lane 6's score sheet lay on top of the stack. Ray's 7 had gone through the paper and left its shape on the sheet underneath.

Ellen did the building in order. The fryer, off and cooling. The back door, then the front, with the lock Ray's key would open until eight in the morning. She tried the handle twice.

The sign hung lit red over the plow bank for whoever drove by, which at that hour was nobody.

Ray had gone out with the last of the league at ten-thirty, but his pickup still sat beside the pole, engine running, a thread of exhaust going up past the cab. She brushed the snow off her windshield. The pickup stayed where it was. Her engine caught on the second try, and she switched on her headlights.

Across the lot, the pickup's lights came on. It rolled down to Kettle Road and turned toward town, and its taillights got smaller until the trees took them.
