# What happens in the first five chapters

*For the sample reader. Built from outline v3.2 (Oct 30, 2026). Chapters 1–3 are already written; Chapters 4–5 are planned.*

**Chapter 1.** Ellen Daly is fifty-eight. She guided planes into Boston for thirty-one years until the government retired her with a sheet cake. This morning she bought Kettle Lanes, a ten-lane candlepin alley in a New Hampshire lake town that empties once the summer people leave, and she sold her house to do it. The old owner, Arlene, who is eighty-four, is carrying the rest: a quarter of a million dollars, due March first, the day her room opens at the retirement home. At 5:15 a man knocks on Ellen's glass front door with her key in his fist.

He's Ray Gagnon, the widowed hardware man who has coached the alley's league for thirty years, and he won't give the key back. He also has a deal with Arlene: if Ellen can't pay her on March first, Ray pays instead, and the building is his by suppertime. "Not a dollar under," he says. "I'm not buying your mistake cheap." They argue in the doorway with the snow blowing in past him. He's taller than she'd planned for, and to her annoyance she'd like him to stay there and keep arguing.

She calls the only locksmith in town to change her locks. His son answers: the locksmith is already standing in her alley. Her ceremonial first ball hops into Ray's lane and wipes out his pins. He walks it back to her and doesn't let go of it right away. "Nice arc. Wrong lane." At closing, Denise from the snack bar shows her the betting sheet from the hardware store. The town is betting on the date she'll fail, and every square is taken except March first. Ray won't buy that one.

**Chapter 2.** Ray's side. He's in trouble from the moment she pulls off a glove with her teeth to play him a recording of her broken machine. He kneels at her door and changes her lock, cutting his own key out of her building, while she holds the flashlight close enough that he can feel her breath on his wrist. On his knees he makes her one offer: he'll buy the place back for every dollar she paid, even if it means putting up the store his father nearly lost. The offer stands until five the next afternoon. She says no. Her bank will lend her Arlene's money if this winter brings in thirty thousand dollars, and nobody has done that in this building for decades. He waits for her reason. "I bought it to hear all ten going at once," she says. "Last night I heard nine." Her daughter Kate calls in the middle of it, and Ellen swears the savings she has left will never go into the alley. The next afternoon, two minutes before his offer runs out, she walks into his store and lays one of her new keys on his counter without a word. She won't sell to him, but she won't lock him out either, and she won't ask him to come in. They both watch the clock go past five.

**Chapter 3.** A snowstorm that was supposed to be over by nine traps thirty-three bowlers overnight. The town's road boss warned her at three o'clock and she went with the forecast instead, so now it's on her. She runs the night like an airport in a shutdown and, to the plow crew's lasting fury, charges them to bowl. When the power dies, she and Ray set pins by hand in the dark behind the lanes. He tells her about his late wife's score on the wall. She tells him about the one night in her career she let two planes get too close, her voice perfectly calm the whole time. Then she tells him why a woman like her buys a bowling alley. Last winter, unable to sleep, she walked in on a league night and heard all ten lanes going. "A pin is standing or it's down. The whole room can see which. Nobody can sound fine about a pin." He asks her for one thing: the championship that carries his wife's name stays the way it has always been. "It stays," Ellen says, "unless the numbers say otherwise." A strike roars over the last words, and she watches him miss them. At dawn, digging out the alley's half-dead neon sign, she kisses him. Then the plow's lights swing up the drive, and every face in the building is at the window.

**Chapter 4.** Ray again, the next morning. The whole town had the kiss by noon, and when the old men at his store asked whether Ellen would take his wife's day, Ray told them she'd promised it would stay. Now Ellen walks in before he can say good morning and tells him he heard only half her sentence, and he's been repeating that half all over town. He asks whether she knew, under the sign. She says yes, and tells him why she kissed him anyway. He says his piece once, "It stays," and then offers to fix her bowling, his hand on her wrist.

That afternoon his store's Christmas party is on her last two lanes, as it is every year. At the cake Ray stands up, unasked, and says what he has put off for two winters: on March first the store goes to his son Danny, and he'll come in Saturdays if Danny asks him. Danny toasts his father's last winter behind the counter. One of the old men finishes the toast for everybody: "Then he can go stand behind hers." The lanes laugh. Ellen hears it from the counter, and Ray watches her hear it. He doesn't laugh, because it isn't wrong. For two winters he meant to hand Danny the store and spend his evenings at this alley, and she got up the drive eight days ahead of him.

At dusk, under the dead letter of her sign, he offers to have it lit by Christmas. She tells him to send her a quote. She heard the old man too. She won't have the town saying Ray fixes her machines because he expects to own them, so from now on everything he fixes, he bills her for. He points out that every dollar she pays him is a dollar she has to make again. "Then I'll miss by an honest bill."

**Chapter 5.** New Year's Eve. Kate calls to announce a decision she has already made, and Ellen hears herself in it. Then Kate mentions "the voice," the one Ellen had on the morning she sat in the car in the driveway after the worst night of her career, and Ellen doesn't answer. At midnight the whole house throws on the count, every lane at once, and Ellen goes back behind the lanes to hear it from where the pins land. Ray is there, off the clock, fixing a machine. All ten lanes hit together. "That's what I bought it for," she says. "I know," he says. "You told my lock." This time they kiss with nothing hidden.

Two days later Denise gives Ellen the town's verdict on midnight like a weather report: "Clearing from the west, with a good chance of Ray." Then she tells her something Ellen didn't know. Since his wife died, Ray can't stand his own house after supper, and this alley was where he meant to go. Ellen asks Denise one question: if his wife's name were on an open tournament, who would come? "Everybody she ever beat. Which is everybody. Don't you touch Linda's night." Denise has come second in it four times.

At two minutes to five Ellen walks into Ray's store. His son is counting the drawer, and Ray, for the first time anyone can remember, keeps his hands flat on the counter and doesn't count it after him. Ellen asks Ray for his wife's name: an open tournament on her day, her name on the cup, anyone welcome. If the winter goes the way winters go here, Ellen will fall short by about the whole of January. Then the alley goes to Ray, Arlene's room gets paid with his money, and the house Ellen sold is gone for nothing. He says no, and why. It's his wife's name, and he won't put it on a cup strangers win for money. Then he says what his no is worth, coming from the man who gets the building if she fails: nothing. He starts to offer his savings. She won't take them; that money is what pays Arlene if Ellen fails. "Then I'll make my no cost me," he says, "what yours costs you." He goes out to his truck, and she doesn't know what he means.
