# What happens in the first five chapters

*For the sample reader. Built from outline v3.1 (Oct 30, 2026). Chapters 1–3 are already written; Chapters 4–5 are planned.*

**Chapter 1.** Ellen Daly is fifty-eight. She guided planes into Boston for thirty-one years until the government retired her with a sheet cake. This morning she bought Kettle Lanes, a ten-lane candlepin alley in a New Hampshire lake town that empties out once the summer people leave. She sold her house for the down payment. The old owner, Arlene, who is eighty-four, is carrying the rest: a quarter of a million dollars, due March first, the day her room opens at the retirement home. At 5:15 a man knocks on Ellen's glass front door with her key in his fist.

He's Ray Gagnon, the widowed hardware man who has coached the alley's league for thirty years, and he won't give the key back. He also has a deal with Arlene: if Ellen can't pay her on March first, Ray pays instead, and the building is his by suppertime. "Not a dollar under," he says. "I'm not buying your mistake cheap." They argue in the doorway with the snow blowing in past him. He's taller than she'd planned for, and to her annoyance she'd like him to stay there and keep arguing.

She calls the only locksmith in town to change her locks. His son answers: the locksmith is standing in her alley with a bowling shoe in his hand. Her ceremonial first ball hops into Ray's lane and wipes out his pins. He walks it back to her and doesn't let go of it right away. "Nice arc. Wrong lane." At closing, the counter woman shows her the betting sheet from the hardware store. The town is betting on the date she'll fail, and every square is taken except March first. Ray won't buy that one.

**Chapter 2.** Ray's side. He's in trouble from the moment she pulls off a glove with her teeth to play him a recording of her broken machine. He kneels at her door and changes her lock, cutting his own key out of her building, while she holds the flashlight close enough that he can feel her breath on his wrist. He makes her one offer: he'll buy the place back for what she paid, even if it means putting up the store his father nearly lost. She turns him down. Her bank will lend her Arlene's money if this winter brings in thirty thousand dollars, and nobody has done that in this building for decades. Her daughter calls in the middle of it, and Ellen swears the savings she has left will never go into the alley. On Thursday, two minutes before his offer runs out, she walks into his store and lays one of her new keys on his counter without a word. They both watch the clock go past five.

**Chapter 3.** A snowstorm that was supposed to be over by nine traps thirty-three bowlers overnight. The town's road boss warned her at three o'clock and she went with the forecast instead, so now it's on her. She runs the night like an airport in a shutdown and, to the plow crew's lasting fury, charges them to bowl. When the power dies, she and Ray set pins by hand in the dark behind the lanes. He tells her about his late wife's record on the wall. She tells him about the one night in her career she let two planes get too close. Then he asks her for one thing: the championship that carries his wife's name stays the way it has always been. "It stays," Ellen says, "unless the numbers say otherwise." A strike roars over the last words, and she watches him miss them. At dawn, digging out the alley's half-dead neon sign, she kisses him. Then the plow's lights swing up the drive, and every face in the building is at the window.

**Chapter 4.** Ray again, the next morning. By Friday noon the whole town knew about the kiss. When the old men at his store asked whether she'd take his wife's day, Ray told them she'd promised it would stay. Now Ellen walks in before he can say good morning and tells him he heard only half her sentence, and he's been repeating that half all over town. He asks whether she knew under the sign. She says yes, and tells him why she kissed him anyway. He says his piece once, "It stays," and then offers to fix her bowling, his hand on her wrist. On the alley's busiest Saturday they run the building over walkie-talkies, and she won't let him quit. Then the snack-bar radio sticks on during the hardware store's Christmas party, and everyone hears his son's toast. This is supposedly Ray's last winter behind the counter. What he wants is to hand Danny the store properly and stop opening it at six every morning. One of the old men finishes the toast for everybody: then Ray can go stand behind hers. Ellen and Ray both hear it. At dusk, under the dead letter of her sign, he offers to have it lit by Christmas. She tells him to send her a quote. From now on, everything he fixes in her building, he bills her for.

**Chapter 5.** New Year's Eve. Ellen's daughter calls to announce a decision she has already made, and Ellen hears herself in it. At midnight Ray is behind the lanes, off the clock, and this time they kiss with nothing hidden. Two days later Ellen counts December with the counter woman. If the rest of the winter goes the way winters go here, she'll come up short by about the whole of January. Borrow it? A loan isn't a winter; the bank counts only what the building earns. She phones her banker, who is out ice fishing. Ellen's own savings can lower the bar, but slowly; all of them wouldn't cover half the gap, and she swore them to her daughter anyway. If she fails, Ray gets the alley, Arlene gets paid by him, and Ellen keeps a rented camp and none of what she came for. So the woman who has never asked anyone for anything walks into Ray's store after closing and asks him for his wife's name: an open tournament on her day, anyone can enter, his wife's cup as the prize. He says no and tells her why. Then he says what his no is worth, coming from the man who gets the building if she fails: nothing. He starts to offer his savings. She stops him, because that money is what pays Arlene if she fails. "Then I'll make my no cost me," he says, "what yours costs you."
