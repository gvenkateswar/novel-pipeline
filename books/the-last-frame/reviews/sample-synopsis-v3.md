# What happens in the first five chapters

*For the sample reader. Built from outline v3 (Oct 30, 2026). Chapters 1–3 are on the page already; Chapters 4–5 are planned.*

**Chapter 1.** Ellen Daly is fifty-eight. She guided planes into Boston for thirty-one years until the government retired her with a sheet cake. This morning she bought Kettle Lanes, a ten-lane candlepin alley in a New Hampshire lake town that empties out once the summer people leave. At 5:15 a man knocks on her glass front door. He has her key in his fist, and he knocks anyway.

He's Ray Gagnon, the widowed hardware man who has coached the alley's league for thirty years, and he won't hand over the key. They argue in the doorway with the snow coming in past him. He's taller than she'd planned for, and to her annoyance she'd like him to stay there and keep arguing. Ellen sold her house to buy the place, and Arlene, the old owner, carried the rest: a quarter of a million dollars, due March first. Arlene only agreed if Ellen signed a paper, and both of them know what it says. If Ellen can't pay, Ray pays Arlene, and the building is his by suppertime. "Not a dollar under," he says. "I'm not buying your mistake cheap."

She calls the only locksmith in town to change her locks. His son answers: the locksmith is standing in her alley with a bowling shoe in his hand. Then her ceremonial first ball hops into Ray's lane and wipes out his pins. He walks the ball back to her and doesn't let go of it right away. "Nice arc. Wrong lane." She laughs before she can stop herself, and catches him looking at her mouth. At closing, the woman at the counter shows her the betting sheet from the hardware store: the town is wagering on the date she'll fail. Every square is taken except March first. Ray won't buy that one.

**Chapter 2.** Ray's side. He's in trouble from the moment she pulls off a glove with her teeth to play him a recording of her broken machine. He kneels at her door and changes her lock, cutting his own key out of her building, while she holds the flashlight close enough that he can feel her breath on his wrist. He makes her one honest offer: he'll buy the place back for exactly what she paid. It means putting up the store his father nearly lost, and he'll ask the credit union once, Thursday at five. She tells him the bank will lend her the money if this winter makes thirty thousand, and nobody has made that in the building since Arlene's late husband. Her daughter calls in the middle of it, and Ellen swears her savings will never go into the building. At two minutes to five on Thursday she walks into his store, lays one of her new keys on his counter without a word, and the two of them watch the clock go past five.

**Chapter 3.** A snowstorm that was supposed to be over by nine traps thirty-three bowlers overnight. The town's road boss warned her at three o'clock and she went with the forecast, so now she owns it. She runs the night like an airport in a shutdown and, to the plow crew's lasting fury, charges for bowling. When the power dies, she and Ray set pins by hand in the dark behind the lanes. He tells her about his late wife's record on the wall. She tells him about the one night in her career she let two planes get too close. Then he asks her for one thing: the championship that carries his wife's name stays the way it has always been. "It stays," Ellen says, "unless the numbers say otherwise." A strike roars on the last words, and she watches him miss them. At dawn, digging out the alley's half-dead neon sign, she kisses him. Then the plow's lights come up the drive.

**Chapter 4.** Ray again, two days later. Before he can say good morning, Ellen tells him straight out that he didn't hear the end of her sentence, and what it means: if the winter comes up short, his wife's championship day is the biggest day she has, and she'll want it. She makes him repeat it back to her. He asks whether she knew under the sign. She says yes. He gives her his answer just as plainly: it stays. Then he teaches her to bowl anyway, his hand on her wrist, and spends the alley's best Saturday trading radio calls with her across the building ("Intentions are to quit." / "Unable."). At closing she says the hard part out loud: if she misses, he gets the building and the championship both. He knows it. She tells him that in January she's going to ask him for it.

**Chapter 5.** New Year's Eve. Ellen's daughter calls to announce a decision she has already made, and Ellen hears herself in it. At midnight Ray finds Ellen behind the lanes, and this time they kiss knowing everything. Two days later she and the counter woman count December: if the rest of the winter goes like a normal winter, Ellen finishes about four thousand dollars short. So the woman who has never asked anyone for anything walks into Ray's store after closing and asks him for his wife's day: an open tournament, anyone can enter, his wife's cup as the prize. He says no, and tells her why, and admits his no costs him nothing, since he gets the building if she fails. Then he tells her he'll make it cost him.
