# Sample read: synopsis v3

**Draft read:** `books/the-last-frame/reviews/sample-synopsis-v3.md` ("Built from outline v3 (Oct 30, 2026)"), the one-page synopsis of Chapters 1–5. I read nothing else except `research/chosen-angle.md`, and only to build the persona.

**Persona (built from `research/chosen-angle.md` only):** Donna, 63, a retired school-district payroll clerk in western Pennsylvania, twice a grandmother, widowed six years ago. She reads on her phone, mostly on Kindle Unlimited, three or four clean romances a month. Last month: two later-in-life small-town books, one with a Vermont inn and one with a bakery. She finished neither; both were a widow healing over coffee while a kind handyman fixed her porch. She is tired of dead spouses kept on a shelf, of sweet men with no edge, and of chapters where people sit down and explain their feelings. The last book she finished was a $4.99 romcom with an actual fight in it, and she would pay that again. She likes numbers that add up, and she skims numbers that repeat.

---

## 1. Where I stopped

> "At closing she says the hard part out loud: if she misses, he gets the building and the championship both. He knows it."

Chapter 4, about 80% of the synopsis.

In one sentence: the misheard line under the strike was the reason I turned the page, she clears it up in the first breath of Chapter 4, and then the two of them sit and say the same thing to each other twice more before closing, so I put the phone down.

## 2. Buy: no

The line that decided it was the one above. I had already heard it at the start of the same chapter and again in Chapter 3, so "He knows it" made me think I know it too and I don't need to pay to hear it again.

**Would skim:** Chapter 4. Apart from the radio calls it is all explaining.

## 4. Do I care about these two people yet?

No. I care about Ellen: the sheet cake, the near miss with two planes, and the fact that she went with the forecast and now owns it. Ray is mostly a man saying no about a dead wife's trophy, and I've read that man twice this month.

## 5. Did anyone make me laugh?

Yes:
- "the government retired her with a sheet cake."
- "His son answers: the locksmith is standing in her alley with a bowling shoe in his hand."
- "to the plow crew's lasting fury, charges for bowling."
- "Nice arc. Wrong lane."

I didn't get "Intentions are to quit." / "Unable." I could tell it was meant to be a joke, but I'm not a pilot.

## 6. Did anyone make me want?

Yes. "while she holds the flashlight close enough that he can feel her breath on his wrist." He is cutting his own key out of her building while she does it, which is why it landed. I also liked "He walks the ball back to her and doesn't let go of it right away."

## 7. The three most boring passages

1. **About 37% (Ch 2):** "He makes her one honest offer: he'll buy the place back for exactly what she paid. It means putting up the store his father nearly lost, and he'll ask the credit union once, Thursday at five. She tells him the bank will lend her the money if this winter makes thirty thousand…" By this point I'm keeping track of a quarter million, March first, Thursday at five, a credit union, a bank and thirty thousand. I stopped tracking.
2. **About 80% (Ch 4):** "At closing she says the hard part out loud: if she misses, he gets the building and the championship both. He knows it. She tells him that in January she's going to ask him for it." This is the third time in two chapters.
3. **About 92% (Ch 5):** "Two days later she and the counter woman count December: if the rest of the winter goes like a normal winter, Ellen finishes about four thousand dollars short." It's an adding-up scene, and the number it lands on is too small to scare me.

A runner-up at about 12% (Ch 1): "Arlene only agreed if Ellen signed a paper, and both of them know what it says." I read it twice and still had to work out who pays whom.

## 8. What I think is going on

Ray says yes to an open tournament on his wife's day. "He'll make it cost him" means he enters it himself, or puts up the cup or his own money, so that her winning hurts him. The tournament packs the alley, she makes the thirty thousand by a hair, and somebody bowls near his wife's record. At the end Ray buys the March first square on the betting sheet, or they hang the sheet on the wall. The daughter is about to do something impulsive, like quitting a job or moving, and Ellen gives her blessing because she just did the same thing. I suspect Arlene wrote Ray into that paper on purpose to push the two of them together. There's no secret left. Everything was said out loud by Chapter 4.

## Forced answers the orchestrator asked for

- **Buy:** No. The deciding line was "At closing she says the hard part out loud: if she misses, he gets the building and the championship both. He knows it."
- **Where I'd put the book down:** at that same line, Ch 4, about 80%.
- **Most want to read:** Ch 3. **Would skim:** Ch 4.
- **Ending I expect:** He agrees to the open tournament, it saves the alley by a small margin, they end up together running it side by side, and the neon sign comes back on. The betting sheet gets a final joke.
- **What I can't predict:** what "he'll make it cost him" actually is, what the daughter decided, and whether the neon sign or the plow driver at the end of Ch 3 turns into anything. Nothing else.
- **Would one honest conversation fix the couple's problem?** No. They have that conversation at the start of Ch 4, and the problem is still there because it's about money and his wife's day, not a misunderstanding. What's left is whether he says yes, and once nothing is hidden I didn't find a yes-or-no question enough to keep me reading. The four-thousand-dollar shortfall also made me ask why someone in that town doesn't just lend it to her.
