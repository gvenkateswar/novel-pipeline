# Manuscript Reader Notes: *The Last Frame*, Draft 2

Cold read of chapters 01–10 `final.md`, in order, stopping at "## Edit log" in each.

---

## Overall verdict

This is a good book. It is specific and dry, and it knows its world: candlepin, a hardware store, ice derbies, a retired air traffic controller who treats a bowling alley like a sector. It doesn't read like a generic small-town romance. The two leads are adults with jobs and habits, and the conflict is about money and a promise, not a misunderstanding someone could fix in one sentence. The plot is solid. The money plot is unusually clean. The ending pays off nearly every planted object: the sanction card, the HELD account, the fallen-pin shot, the ETTLE sign, the orange-tag key, the wedding row.

What holds it back from five stars is the romance. On the page, the warmth between Ellen and Ray is rationed. They kiss on Jan 16, have one dinner, kiss again on Jan 27, fight on Jan 28, and break up on Feb 2. For chapters 7 through 9 they are mostly apart or doing business. A romance reader will feel that.

**Would I recommend it?** Yes, to readers who like quiet, competent, older-protagonist romance (people who liked *A Man Called Ove* for its texture, or Mary Kay Andrews without the beach). I would not recommend it to readers who want heat or a lot of on-page longing.

**Amazon rating: 4 stars.** I'd write "Loved the world and the heroine, wanted more of them together."

## Ellen and Ray: believable? Enough warmth?

**Believable: yes.** They're well matched. Both are precise, both are rule-bound, and both show care through work instead of words. The early beats are the best part of the book: the readback, the after-hours card, Ray writing his line in her five columns, "Traffic, twelve o'clock, one mile." Their fight is fair on both sides. Ellen's "you do not hear anything that would change that league" is true, and cruel, and she knows it.

**Warmth: not quite enough, and it's in the wrong places.** Nearly all the tenderness sits in chapters 4–6, roughly two weeks of story time. After the breakup at the truck (ch 7), the reconciliation runs almost entirely through gestures made offstage or on paper: the Pine Ridge match she hears about secondhand, the release he signs alone, the key laid on a desk. Ch 9's reconciliation is moving, but it's a transaction ("I'd like to be asked in"). We don't get another moment of the two of them simply enjoying each other until the pit in ch 10. The two "I love you"s in ch 10 are each bolted onto the end of a list ("I'm sixty-one. I eat supper at a quarter past five... I love you."). That's in character, but it's a little neat, because both declarations have the same structure.

Small believability snag: Ellen's whole flaw hinges on a condition ("unless the numbers say otherwise") said under a pinsetter sweep. The book plants it (ch 3), but it's still a lawyerly device, and a skeptical reader may call it a gotcha.

---

## Is the money easy to follow? Does the win feel earned?

**Mostly yes.** This is the book's quiet strength. Judy's two circled numbers ($30k NOI, $25k cash on Feb 28) are clear and get repeated just enough. The HELD account is set up in ch 3 and pays off in ch 10. Ray's equity math in ch 7 actually checks out (smaller note, smaller payment, lower bar). Ch 9's labeled tape (24,300 + 3,900 + 2,650 = 30,850) and ch 10's 21,920 + 3,680 = 25,600 reconcile.

**One soft spot:** the projection jumps between ch 7 and ch 8. After the meeting she's at "Twenty-two thousand in income... Seventeen in the bank." Eighteen days later she's only $1,800 and $1,250 short *before* the Open. Vacation week (3,900) and the two teams coming back don't obviously cover that much ground. Most readers won't run the numbers, but the ones who like this kind of book might.

**Earned? Yes, with a footnote the book chooses to leave open.** She builds vacation week herself, refunds the evening at real risk ("The evening she had handed back across the counter in envelopes would have covered either"), and the HELD discipline is what wins over the underwriter. Ray's Pine Ridge match also brought back two teams, so "could I carry it" is never fully answered. Ch 8 says so outright ("she would never know"). I think that's honest, but some readers will want Ellen to state where she landed on it.

The underwriter caves too fast. One explanation, then "I'll take it." The book's last real obstacle folds in a single paragraph.

## Repetition that still grates

- **"block capitals" / "in her capitals" / "square capitals."** Fifteen or more times. By ch 8 I was noticing it every time.
- **ATC phrases.** "Negative," "Stand by," "Unable," "Say again," "Readback." Delightful in ch 1–4. By ch 8–9 ("Stand by," Ellen said, to Denise; "Ground stop on the snack bar") it's a tic more than a voice.
- **Denise's "honey."** On almost every line she has. Her other tics ("You'll live," "toe, heel") are motifs and fine, but together they make her a set of catchphrases.
- **"Pine Ridge is forty minutes."** Said or thought at least five times.
- **The thesis line.** "The first thing she had ever chosen" comes back in ch 1, ch 2 (dialogue), ch 5, and ch 7. Once is plenty. The reader got it.
- **Recurring texture.** The baseboard ticking (5+ times), cold coming across the floor (3), an engine catching on the second try (2), someone nodding once (many).
- **Scene-ending buttons.** Nearly every scene closes on a crisp, quiet one-liner: "She sat down in the desk chair." "Ray pulled the door shut." "Hollis leaned out the window. 'You going home now?'" Individually good, but collectively the pattern shows, and it's the most AI-patterned thing left in the book.

## Continuity contradictions

Dates and arithmetic are very clean. I checked weekdays against 2026–27, the 17- and 18-day gaps, the Pine Ridge totals (84+117+133=334), the ice-out margin, and the cash reconciliation. All of them hold. What I found:

1. **Ch 1, internal.** "Half this town gave her until spring" contradicts, a page later, "Most people figured you'd make it to New Year's."
2. **Ch 6, bob-house.** "Ellen stood and gave them..." and then later "Ellen sat down on the bait bucket." The committee had the two buckets and Ray had his pail, so where was she sitting before she stood?
3. **Ch 8.** Gil's audience is "eight bowlers, Denise, and Ray" (ten). Denise later says Gil called it "to eleven people." Trivial.
4. **Ch 7 → ch 8 projections.** See the money section. Not a contradiction exactly, but an unexplained jump.
5. **Ch 9.** "The right one shook once, the way hers had shaken on the drop light that morning." Since Linda's ball is in his lap, "hers" reads as Linda's on first pass.
6. **Ch 10.** "March first, nobody. All winter, nobody." This briefly reads as "nobody bought squares," which contradicts ch 1–2's full sheet. It means nobody won.
7. **Ch 6.** "About what a plow truck costs. With the blade." This is said of a $24,500 boiler, and a new plow truck costs far more. Readers up north will notice.
8. **Ch 10.** "Fourteen minutes, Mrs. Daly." It took me a moment to work out what was being timed, and why she lets the "Mrs." go after making such a point of it in ch 6. The intent (a wink toward marriage) arrives late.

## Does the ending pay off?

**Yes.** Ch 9 is the real climax and it's excellent. Ellen asks for help ("I cannot hold the floor by myself today"). Ray reads it back. Her December sanction card saves his team. She makes the fallen-pin shot he taught her ("The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."). And Ray's team *loses*, with Denise taking Linda's trophy, which is the bravest and best choice in the book. The release dated Feb 16 is a fine reveal. "Both words" is a perfect last line: the ETTLE sign from page one, fixed.

Ch 10 is softer. The underwriter, Arlene's "Why me?", and the pool sheet all get tidied in a row, like an epilogue checklist. Arlene's answer ("You asked me what the league paid") is good, but we already got it in ch 8 from her own mouth, so it lands as a repeat.

**Characters I'll remember:** Denise first, then Verna ("The chair will not go into the brother"), then Ellen. Ray is solid but quieter than everyone around him.

**Best moment:** Ch 4, in the pit by flashlight.
> Ellen was squaring the 10 pin on its spot. She didn't look up.
> "Traffic, twelve o'clock, one mile."
> He laughed until he had to put a hand down flat on the boards to stay sitting up.

A close second is ch 7, the key in the ignition: "Six minutes, twenty-eight seconds. Merrimack had been four."

---

## Top 3 remaining revisions

1. **Put one more warm, non-transactional Ellen–Ray scene between ch 7 and ch 9**, or give ch 9's office scene one beat of plain affection before the paperwork. Right now the romance goes quiet for about three chapters.
2. **Cut the tics.** Trim "block capitals" by about two-thirds, ration the ATC phrases after ch 5, thin out Denise's "honey," drop the repeats of "first thing she had chosen" and "Pine Ridge is forty minutes," and vary a few scene-ending buttons.
3. **Tighten ch 8 and the front of ch 10.** Ch 8 hops locations and loses momentum. Ch 10's underwriter folds too easily, and Arlene's "why me" repeats ch 8. Also fix the small continuity items above, especially the ch 7→8 money jump and the plow-truck price.

---

## Ready to publish?

**Yes, after a copyedit and a light tic-trimming pass.** The structure works, the continuity is close to airtight, the money plot reconciles, and the ending pays off. What's left is polish, plus a deliberate choice about how much on-page warmth a romance reader will want. That's a matter of taste, not a broken beat. Nothing here would earn it one- or two-star reviews. The likely 3-star complaint is "wanted more romance," and the likely 5-star praise is "Denise."
