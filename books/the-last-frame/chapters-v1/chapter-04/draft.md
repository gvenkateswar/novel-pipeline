# Chapter Four: Ground Stop

The blade had dug in at an angle, and the truck had swung around on it like a gate on a hinge.

Ray heard it from lane 6. First the scrape of steel on the drive, steady, then the engine climbing with nothing under it. A plow that's moving drops its voice on a downgrade. This one climbed and stayed up there. He had his coat half on by the vestibule and left his hat on the settee.

Hollis's truck sat crossways at the foot of the drive with its nose toward the lake and the blade buried. The amber light went around on the cab roof. It turned the falling snow orange and then gave it back. Wind came off Great Kettle sideways and filled the tire tracks while Ray looked at them. Up the hill behind him the sign buzzed in the cold, and from down here it read ETTLE LANES, the way it had for seven years.

He rapped on the driver's window. Hollis cranked it down an inch.

"You hurt?"

Hollis leaned forward and looked up through the windshield at the sky, which was nothing but snow coming at the glass. Then he looked at Ray.

"No."

He climbed down, pulled a tape from his coat, and ran it into the snow on the hood. He read it by flashlight.

"Eleven. Two an hour, and it hasn't made up its mind about stopping."

From his chest pocket he took a little green notebook, the waterproof kind, and wrote the number down with a golf pencil. Then the time.

"Town pulled its trucks off Kettle Road at eight-thirty," he said. "Till the wind drops. Nobody tows tonight."

Ray had a shovel in his hands. He had taken it from the vestibule on the way out, and nobody had asked him for it.

He had stood at the bottom of this drive in every storm since 1996. League nights he'd come early with Arlene's key and open up before the town got around to Kettle Road. Linda drove herself to league in worse than this. She used to call him at the store from the counter phone to say the roads were fine, and he could hear the vestibule door banging behind her and knew to the inch how fine they were.

A flashlight came down the drive, and Ellen behind it, parka zipped over the fleece vest. She kept to the edge where the snow hadn't packed. She didn't slip once.

"Anyone hurt?"

Hollis checked the sky again. "No."

"Is Kettle Road closed?"

"It is."

"Can a car get past you?"

"Not past me."

"Then nobody leaves."

Hollis nodded. That was the whole meeting. Ray stood a yard outside it with the shovel and watched two people settle thirty-odd lives in four questions. He had no reason to mind that, and he minded it.

"What does this much snow do to the ice?" Ellen said.

"Slows the making. Snow on new ice is a blanket. Keeps the cold off it." Hollis put the notebook away. "I've got a derby the last weekend of February."

"I know."

"Same weekend as your championship."

Ray noticed the *your* and couldn't tell which of them it was for.

"How many people come to a derby?" Ellen said.

"Twelve hundred, a good year."

She didn't write it down. She wrote down the temperature of the fryer. She had written down the misses on her own first ball. Ray watched her keep the number in her head and walk on to the next question, and he knew she had it for good.

"How many inches by morning?"

Hollis took off his cap.

* * *

Ellen counted the room at 9:20. Ray watched her lips move from lane 1 to lane 10 and back. He counted with her and came out where she did. Thirty-six.

Every lane was lit and nobody was bowling. The Thursday league sat along the orange settees in their coats with their boots dripping salt onto the floor. Denise had the forty-cup urn going behind the snack bar. Verna Tibbetts, in her lavender parka zipped to the chin, had taken the scorer's table at lane 1 and set her binder square on it.

From the office came the rotary dial, going around and coming back. Ellen's voice through the half-open door was level. Thirty-six people. A heated building with food and water. Nobody hurt. "Understood. We'll stay put."

She came out to the counter and bent the gooseneck down. The PA hummed before she spoke.

"As of now this is a ground stop. Nobody leaves the building."

"Kettle Tower, Highway Two, requesting permission for the men's room," a voice called from lane 9.

She didn't answer him. She picked up a clipboard.

"Sleeping arrangements. If you came with your husband or your wife, you're on the lane 3 and 4 settees. Highway Department, 8 and 9. Lakeview Diner, 6 and 7. Everyone else, 1 and 2, and 5." She turned a page. "Mr. Gagnon and Mr. Pike, lane 10, by the pit door. In case."

"In case of what?" somebody said.

"In case." She set the clipboard down. "I'll be on the counter stool."

"The secretary does not sleep during a session," Verna said, to no one, and uncapped her pen.

Denise leaned over the snack bar. "Two hot dogs a head till the roller's empty, and I counted, there's sixty-one in the case. The last two bags of fries stay in the freezer till midnight. Coffee's free all night. That was her idea, so don't thank me."

A Lakeview Diner bowler stood up from the lane 7 settee with his keys already in his hand. "Ray. The Tacoma's got four-wheel. Think she'd make Kettle Road?"

Ray thought about it. "Fella I knew had a four-wheel-drive he said could climb the town hall. Spent the night of the '78 blizzard on top of a snowbank on the Gilford road with all four wheels going."

"Which fella?" Ellen said from the mic. There was nothing in her voice.

"Merle Tardif. Gilford."

Two of the Diner bowlers laughed. They were looking at Ray when they did it. The man with the Tacoma sat down and put his keys away.

The PA hummed again.

"The lanes stay open all night as a marathon. Strings at the usual rate. Snack bar at the usual prices. Sleeping is free, and so are coffee, water, and the phone. For every string bowled tonight, the house adds a set amount to the league prize fund."

Verna had her finger in the binder before Ellen finished. She found the page and read it without lifting her eyes.

"Strings bowled tonight are not league play and will not count toward averages," she said. "The contribution will be entered in the minutes as a gift of the house."

The Highway Department got up first. Four of them went down the settees to the counter with their wallets out, and the Diner followed, and then nearly everyone. Denise rang strings and ran the register from memory.

"Charging us to sit out a blizzard," a man on lane 4 said. Nobody answered him, and after a minute he got in line too.

Ray stood at the lane 10 settee with his coat on. He didn't buy a string. Denise looked across the building at him once, between customers, and went back to the register.

Ellen came around the counter to lane 1.

"Thank you for the ruling, Mrs. Tibbetts."

Verna was already writing. "The chair will be here at six, Mrs. Daly. So will the minutes."

* * *

The office door was half closed. Through it came the first marathon strings, ten lanes of wood going down and getting swept, over and over.

Ellen stood at the steel desk running the night on the adding machine. The tape curled off the back and over the edge. Ray came in and stopped at the door. The top hinge had a screw backed out about a sixteenth. He took a dime from his pocket and set it in the slot.

"You're charging them."

"I'm not charging anyone to be stranded." She tore the tape off. "Sleeping is free. Coffee, water, the phone. Bowling costs what bowling costs."

He gave the screw a quarter turn. "February of '78 Arlene opened the snack bar and all ten lanes for three days. Nobody paid a dime. The league still talks about it."

"I've heard about it."

"Arlene never charged the league for a lot of things."

He meant it well, and it came out that way. Ellen's eyes went to the green ledgers on the shelf above the desk and came back to him.

Out front the popcorn machine kicked on. The space heater under the desk quit with a click, the desk lamp went with it, and the office went black.

"Sorry!" Denise called from the counter.

Ellen kept talking. "The storm takes Friday from me. It takes Saturday." He heard her cross three steps in the dark, the panel door creak open, a breaker snap over. The lamp came up. The heater ticked and started to hum. She was back at the desk with her hand on the adding machine. "Those are my two best nights. Tonight is the only thing it's giving back."

He had been coming into this office for thirty years. He would have had to feel along the wall for that panel.

"Off people who can't leave, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms." She took her hand off the machine. When she spoke again it was slower, and quieter, and he had to lean in to hear it. "Nobody has to bowl. I am not charging anyone to be stranded. I am charging for bowling."

Ray put the dime back in his pocket.

"If it were yours tonight," she said, "what would you do?"

"Open it all up. Free. Eat the cost. That's what the place is."

"And in March you will still be open."

"Yes."

He knew exactly what he would do with the building on March second. Nothing. The settees would stay orange and the league would bowl Tuesdays and Thursdays the way it always had, and the case would stay on its wall with the same card in it. He had spent six years planning to change nothing. And here she was in Arlene's office changing things while he stood at the door.

"On whose money?" Ellen said.

The store was Danny's to run and his to own. He had a bank letter folded behind his license. She had a pension and thirteen weeks. He opened his mouth, and the door swung in against his shoulder.

"Ray." Denise had a hot dog in a paper boat in one hand. "The Diner wants to know have you got one about a fella and a moose. I said probably. Don't make me a liar."

He went out past her. Behind him the adding machine started up again.

* * *

Hollis came in at 1:52 with snow to the tops of his boots and said "Nineteen" to nobody in particular. The scanner on his belt crackled. A pine down across the lines on Kettle Road, by the beach.

The lanes went dark in the middle of a string. Somebody's ball was still rolling down lane 8, and everyone listened to it hit.

Under the floor the boiler's burner cut out, and then the circulator wound down like a fan somebody had unplugged. Ray heard both before anyone thought to say it was going to get cold. The baseboard along the wall stopped ticking. Over the doors the exit signs came up red on their batteries.

Ellen had a flashlight on the counter before the ball on lane 8 stopped rolling. With the PA dead she raised her voice.

"The marathon ends at the last full string. You paid for machines. Anything bowled from here on is free. The house is closed. This is a building with people in it."

Somebody on lane 3 clapped twice and stopped.

Ray walked up to the counter. "You want lane 5 set by hand?"

"Yes. And I'm coming back with you."

"Pit's no place for somebody who's never been hit by a pin."

"It's my pit."

He didn't say it twice.

"Five's table stopped high," he said instead. "Clears the deck. We can work under it."

On the way down the building in the dark he passed the trophy case and touched the glass with one knuckle. The steel door by lane 10 stuck, as it always did, and gave on the second shove. He went along the catwalk with the flashlight to the pinsetter panel and threw lane 5's breaker off. A Bowl-Mor stopped dead in an outage. It also started again, all at once, when the power came back, whoever had their hands in it.

Ellen tore a strip of masking tape off the roll in her vest, wrote on it against her knee, and pressed it over the switch. HANDS IN PIT.

The deck had ten spots painted on it, worn down to gray circles. Above the cushion the table hung with its jaws open. There was room under it for two people sitting up on the side boards with their knees drawn in.

"Pins stay down between balls," Ray said. "We clear after the third. While the ball's coming, you're up on the boards with your feet up."

"Then the bowler calls before every throw. Out loud. 'Ball.'" She raised her voice down the lane. "Denise. Every throw, you call 'Ball!' first."

"Ball!" Denise called, and threw before Ellen had her feet up.

The pins went everywhere. One came off the kickback a foot over Ellen's head and dropped behind the cushion. She looked at where it had hit, then at Ray, and pulled her knees in another two inches.

They set by flashlight. Out front somebody held a beam on the deck from the settee so Denise could see what was standing. Every delay got booed, the whole room at once, the way they booed the scorekeeper at the championship.

Between strings the requests started coming down the lane.

"Ray! One about a dog!"

He set the head pin on its spot. "Fella I knew in Sanbornton taught his dog to fetch the paper. Dog fetched every paper on the road. Fella paid for eleven subscriptions."

"Which fella?" Ellen said. "And what year."

"Ernie Plourde. Eighty-six."

"Ice!" somebody called.

"Fella I knew drove out on Winnisquam in March to show his brother-in-law it'd hold. Held him fine." Ray dropped back up onto the board. "Held the truck till Tuesday."

"Ball!"

Wood flew. They came down and cleared it and set ten more. It was colder now. Ellen's breath showed in the flashlight beam when she leaned over the 7 spot.

"Do one about a fella who bought a bowling alley," a voice called from the Highway Department end.

The building got quieter, a settee at a time.

"Don't know him," Ray called back. "Next ball."

Ellen spoke low, only to him. "Which fella?"

"Nobody you'd know."

"Ball!"

The next ball took out the front of the rack. A pin came off the deck, ran along the side board, and dropped across Ray's knees.

Ellen was squaring the 10 pin on its spot. She didn't look up.

"Traffic, twelve o'clock, one mile."

He laughed. He laughed until he had to put a hand down flat on the boards to stay sitting up, and out front somebody shouted to know what was so funny back there, and that set him off again. Ellen set the 10 pin and waited, with her hand still on it.

When he had his breath he rolled the pin off his knees and onto its spot.

"I picked five," he said. "Table didn't have much to do with it."

She waited.

"February of '98. A Thursday. This table quit in the ninth frame of Linda's string. I came back and set her tenth by hand. Sitting right here." He touched the board under him. "That was the 187. I never saw it. I heard it from back here. Three balls. I could tell by the wood."

This pit was the closest he had been to that night in twenty-eight years. Same ten spots, a shade grayer. Same dark behind the cushion. He had never told anyone that he hadn't seen it, not Denise and not Danny.

Ellen didn't say anything for a while. She straightened the head pin a quarter inch it didn't need.

"My last day on the scope was a day shift," she said. "The month I turned fifty-six. I worked it to the minute. Signed off position at three o'clock and handed the headset to a man I'd trained. There was a sheet cake in the break room." She lined up the 2 pin with the 1. "They spelled my name right."

"What kind of cake?"

"Marble."

A flashlight beam swung off the deck and found the two of them sitting there under the table.

"You two setting pins or visiting?" Denise said.

"Ball!" somebody was already calling from the foul line, and they both went up for the boards.

* * *

At 5:40 the glass-block windows along the front had gone gray. Hollis's battery lantern sat on the counter. Down the settees the league slept in parkas, in rows, a few of them with their hoods up.

Verna sat at the lane 1 scorer's table where she had sat down eight hours before. She looked at her watch and wrote one line in the binder.

"The session stands adjourned at five forty-one," she told the room, which was mostly asleep, and closed the binder.

The adding machine was dead with everything else. Ellen had a score sheet turned over on the counter and was running the night down the back of it in pencil, columns ruled with the edge of a menu. The urn beside her had gone lukewarm.

Denise came past zipping her coat. She didn't look at the sheet. "The Diner bought fries twice, honey. Second time was after the lights went."

Ellen erased a figure and wrote a new one.

Ray stood across the counter from her. He had been reading invoices upside down across a hardware counter for forty years. The night had made money. Before the lights went it had beaten any Saturday on her board. At the bottom of the sheet the running total said $11,800, and she drew a line under it.

She stood up and took the rotary phone off the office desk and stood in the doorway with it, the cord stretched as far as it went. She dialed the utility. Ray could hear the recording from where he was. Kettle Road, Ambrose. Estimated restoration Saturday evening.

With the receiver back in its cradle, Ellen sat down on the counter stool.

Then she reached up to the strip board and took down the Friday glow strip and the Saturday one. She printed CLOSED across each in block capitals and pinned them back.

His eye went along the board while she did it, out to the far end. 02/27 / 1800 / LGM / 10. REMARKS was blank. Nothing to remark on.

He did her arithmetic without meaning to. The two best nights on that board, gone, and the power company in no hurry. He minded it. He had been minding things like it for weeks. Fridays since the middle of December he had stood at the back window of the store and counted the cars in her lot across the cove, and he had never told anyone the number.

"Denise," Ellen said. "The Friday party list."

Denise took it out from under the crossword and slid it across.

Ellen picked up the receiver and put her finger in the wheel. Ray was halfway down to lane 10 for his coat before the dial ran back.

* * *

The snow had stopped. The sky over the lake was low and the color of a pewter plate, and the lake itself was gone under new white on the far side of Kettle Road.

Hollis's truck sat where it had come to rest, drifted to the door handles on the lake side. They dug the blade out without talking much. When it was clear, Hollis laid the tape on the hood one more time.

"Twenty-two. Done." He wrote it down.

The town plow came along Kettle Road throwing a wave off its wing. It didn't turn in. The drive was private. The driver lifted two fingers off the wheel going by, and Hollis lifted two back.

Ray's phone had been in his coat since yesterday afternoon. He took it out to check the time and there was an email from the bank. The commitment letter was extended through March 31. He had asked for that on December 14, the morning of her first lesson. He read it standing in her driveway with the shovel under his arm.

"That the bank?" Hollis said.

"Yep."

"Still want to lend you a bowling alley?"

"Through March."

Hollis checked the sky. "She know?"

"She knows."

Hollis climbed in. The engine caught. He backed the truck off the bank of snow, swung it around on the slope, and dropped the blade to start on her lot. He leaned out over the scrape.

"You going home now?"

Ray put the phone in his pocket. He started up the driveway on foot, toward the building, with the shovel still under his arm.
