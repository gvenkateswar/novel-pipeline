# Chapter Nine: The Last Frame

At 4:10 the radar on Ellen's phone showed the storm as a green comma with a yellow core, and the core sat over Great Kettle Lake. Under the lot lights the snow went sideways. The glass-block band along the front was going white from the bottom up, one row at a time.

The handheld on the counter clicked on channel 2.

"Kettle Lanes, Pike. You've got eleven inches at the town garage and—" The wind took the rest of it, and the plow's engine with it.

"Say again."

"Eleven down at the garage. Eight to ten more by dark. Gusts to forty on the flats, and from the access I can't see the bob-house rows." The radio hissed. "Outdoor day's off. Indoor events go to you. Friday's fish get weighed on your lane ten."

"Plan B."

"Plan B. I'll run the weigh-in between passes. I won't be bowling."

"Thank you, Hollis."

The whiteboard leaned against the settee at lanes 1 and 2. Ellen circled B in red. At the foot of every column, A through D, the line that had read MX: for a week now read MX: R.G. in her capitals. She had made that call herself on the twentieth, with Denise watching, and booked him by work order at the agreed rate.

"Fryer first." Kate came out of the snack bar in scrub pants and a Kettle Lanes sweatshirt she had taken off the merchandise hook without paying. She had driven up Friday on her days off to see the thing, and her body still ran on nights. "Thermostat says three-fifty and it's running hotter, you can smell it on the basket. Second, the women's room has one hand dryer and it's the loud kind. Third, your burn gel is from 2014, which, fine, it doesn't really go bad, but it looks bad." She turned to the whiteboard. "Who's R.G.?"

"The mechanic."

"The one who wanted to buy it."

"Yes."

Kate read the board column by column, the way she read a chart.

"Mom. Scale of one to ten. How much of today is one person?"

Ellen looked at the radar. The comma had not moved.

She had bought the building for days like this one, a room that would fill with people who needed putting in order, and she had picked it herself off a sign on a Sunday in September. She had never told Kate how her calm behaved on a bad day. It sounded right on every transmission, right up to the moment it was too late, and nobody listening could hear the difference, including her.

"Ask me at six," Ellen said.

Kate gave her one more second, then went to the 40-cup urn. She pulled the basket, looked into it, and slid it back.

"Filters?"

"Second shelf under the register. Left of the cups, behind the napkins. Stacked bottom side up."

* * *

At 10:40 Ellen came out of the office and counted on her way to the counter. A hundred and forty-seven, in snow boots. Coolers stood three deep at the end of lane 10. Every coat in the building was wet at the shoulders, and the floor mats had gone gray with salt. The Open's early rounds ran on all ten lanes, and the waiting list on the strip board was three cards deep.

The Tuesday teams were paying in for the league round. The 112 laid his money on the counter one bill at a time.

"Twelve-fifty," he said, "to bowl in a blizzard."

Denise didn't look up from the register. "Gil charged you forty minutes each way and you thanked him for the carpet. Shoes on your left."

Behind her the fryer had three tickets hanging and the popcorn machine was going. So was the space heater under the counter. Ellen had heard that pair trip the breaker twice this winter.

"Ground stop on the snack bar," Ellen said. "No new orders for fifteen minutes. Popcorn off."

Denise switched it off with the back of her wrist and pulled the next ticket. "That's the first thing you've said all winter I'd have said myself."

Down on lane 9 the table hung on the respot. A man in a Highway Department jacket stood at the foul line with his ball and looked sixty feet down the lane at it.

The steel door by lane 10 stuck. Ellen put her shoulder into it on the second try.

In the pit the drop light hung on its hook over lane 3. Ray had the cover off and one arm inside the machine to the elbow. Masking tape in his square capitals labeled every nail along the wall, and the coffee can of bolts sat on the bench with a socket wrench across its mouth.

"One hundred forty-seven in the building," Ellen said. "Lane 3, clutch slipping since nine-fifty. Lane 9, table hanging on the respot, twice since ten. Snack bar stopped until five past eleven. Hollis on the back roads since three. Weigh-in at noon on lane 10. Final at six on 5 and 6."

It came out in the voice. Every item stood in its place, and nothing in it shook. She had given a relief briefing at the end of every shift for thirty-one years and had never once left anything out.

Ray took his arm out of the machine and wiped his hand on the rag from his back pocket. He waited.

She had sent his check back to the bank from her own parking lot to find out what she could carry. After today she would never know. If she missed Judy's lines by a single dollar, the building went to the man in front of her, and she was going to ask him anyway.

"I cannot hold the floor by myself today."

It had gone. She heard it go, somewhere around "floor," and she could not find where it had been. Out on the lanes someone threw a strike and a dozen people shouted about it.

Ray didn't move.

"At the Center they call it the D-side." She said it slowly, one word after the other, the way she had once read numbers to a pilot who was lost. "We borrowed their words when ours were not good enough. It is a second controller on the same scope. Same traffic."

The drop light buzzed.

"I am asking you to share the position. Today. And after today, whatever Judy says Monday."

Lane 9's sweep came across the deck on the last four words, and the thunk went through the catwalk boards and up into her bad knee. She did not say them again.

Ray hung the rag on the bench.

"You can't hold the floor by yourself today," he said. "I share the position. Today, and after. Whatever Judy says Monday."

He took the drop light off its hook and held it out to her by the handle.

"Nine first. Three holds another hour if nobody throws hard." He was already walking down the catwalk. "Then three."

She carried the light to lane 9 and held it where he pointed, over the table, while he got the cover off. Her hand wasn't steady, and the light moved on the steel, and he worked in it anyway. Out front the PA hummed.

"Ellen to lane ten," Denise said over the whole house. "The fish are here."

* * *

The derby scale stood on lane 10's scorer's table. Coolers lined the approach: lake trout laid on snow, two cusk in a bait bucket, a pickerel a boy carried in his arms like a cat. The treasurer sat on the settee with his gray cash box on his knees, and the raffle woman wore her ticket roll on her wrist. On lanes 7 and 8 the kids' division bowled free, at volume.

Hollis had come in off the plow with snow standing on both shoulders. Judy Ostrowski, in a red snowmobile suit, set her lake trout on the scale.

"Seven pounds, six ounces," Hollis said.

"Twenty-six and a quarter inches."

"Depth?"

"Thirty-eight feet, off the second point. Live smelt."

"Thirty-eight." He wrote it in the waterproof notebook with the golf pencil. Then he wrote the whole fish again on the next line, weight, length, depth, point, and smelt.

Neither of them said anything about it.

Judy unzipped the top of the suit and nodded to Ellen. "I'm here as a fisherman today."

The radio clipped to Hollis's coat spoke in the town dispatcher's voice. He turned it up and listened to the whole of it before he said anything.

"Four-foot drifts at the Dock Road bend, and building. Wind's filling the cut faster than one truck can clear it. Town's pulling its trucks off Kettle Road at four." He looked at Ellen. "I'm back for your lot at five-thirty and not before. I don't want anybody in a car after four."

Ellen looked at the whiteboard on its settee. Every column on it started with the doors still locked.

"Half your six o'clock people are already standing in this building," Denise said. She had come down from the counter with a pencil behind each ear. "Keep the evening. They slept on those settees in January and lived."

"The raffle's drawn at six." The treasurer put a hand flat on the cash box. "It says six on eleven hundred tickets."

"Kids out first," Kate said. "Now, while the road's open, before anybody gets tired and stupid."

Ellen keyed the handheld. "Ray. Lanes 3 and 9."

"Three and nine hold till four." The pit made his voice small. "Not past four."

He said nothing else.

At seven that morning the tape had cleared both of Judy's lines, with the evening and Sunday on it. Without them she could not say which side of either line she would land on, and she would not know until she ran it tonight. She uncapped the red marker.

"The final bowls at two-thirty on 5 and 6," she said. "Raffle at two. Departures begin at two-fifteen, and the building is empty at four. Every six o'clock ticket is refunded in full. Sunday is closed."

She took the 02/27 strip off the board, crossed out 1800, and wrote 1430 above it. REMARKS she left as it was.

The treasurer stood up.

"Five to six is our door!" It went down the lanes and stopped two games. "That's the year, Hollis! The whole year is that hour! You called our ice at four o'clock this morning and now you're calling our draw!"

Hollis said nothing. He closed the notebook.

"The draw time is my building's call," Ellen said. "Mr. Pike called the ice."

"Then who pays the club for that hour?"

She had nothing to give him. He was right about the hour, and everybody on lane 10 knew it. She held his eye until he sat back down with the box on his knees.

Hollis put the notebook in his coat and turned for the door. "That's Plan E."

"Plan E with a final in it."

She pulled the gooseneck down.

"Kettle Lanes, your attention. Families with children, you're first. Coats on now, and stop at the counter on your way out. Halsey Falls, you're number two, right behind them. Everyone holding a six o'clock ticket: your ticket says six o'clock, and nobody is getting six o'clock today. Mrs. Lavoie has your money at the counter. All of it."

When she got back to the counter Denise was already licking envelopes, the refund sheet pinned under her elbow.

"That's eleven hundred and forty dollars walking down Kettle Road," she said.

"Then label the envelopes."

* * *

The raffle came off lane 7's ball return at two. The raffle woman dropped the stubs into the return's well by the handful, and a girl in a purple snowsuit reached in to the shoulder and came out with one. A man by the vestibule with his boots already on heard his number and came back in for his ice auger.

The counter phone rang. Ellen picked it up.

"Kettle Lanes. We close at four today. Nobody drives after four."

She hung up. Ray had come up the side of the snack bar from the pit door. He walked around the end of the counter onto her side, where he had stood on the second of February, and leaned down to her gooseneck.

"Kettle Lanes, Gagnon. Request."

It came out of every speaker in the building. Ellen was a foot from him. She leaned in to the same microphone.

"Gagnon, say request."

Thirty heads turned to look at two people a foot apart. Somebody on lane 6 laughed and got an elbow for it.

"The Linda Gagnon Memorial goes to the Open," Ray said into the mic. "This year and every year after. Anybody who pays the entry."

He straightened up and gave no reason.

"Yes," Ellen said.

At the card table by lane 5 Verna wrote it down. "The chair records it as a gift," she said, as she had recorded the house's money in January, "to the Open, in perpetuity."

Then she stood, binder open to a page marked with a strip of paper, and came to the counter in her lavender parka zipped to the chin.

"Gagnon and Son shows three bowlers. Under the bylaw adopted February 1983, there is no blind score in a championship final, and a substitute must be a sanctioned bowler on no other roster this season." She turned the page. "The bylaw followed a final in which the Legion substituted a cousin from Laconia who had bowled that same afternoon in Laconia under his brother's name. The chair will not go into the brother."

"Everybody sanctioned in this building's on a roster," Ray said. "Gagnon and Son forfeits."

Verna uncapped her pen.

Ellen opened the drawer under the register. Denise had filed the sanction card there in December, behind the first-aid kit, and it was still there in Ellen's block capitals. She laid it on Verna's binder.

"Gagnon and Son doesn't forfeit."

Verna tilted the card to the light and read the date. "The tenth of December. Sanctioned, and on no roster." She wrote. "The substitute will bowl at the league's average for a bowler with none, as set in 1991."

Ray looked at the card, then at Ellen, and said nothing.

She was an owner with an average nobody had ever written down, about to bowl in front of everybody left in her building, with sixty people still lined up at the counter for coats and envelopes. She knew exactly what that was.

"I don't leave the counter in a rush," Denise said, before anyone could ask her. "Twenty-two years."

Kate came around the end of the counter, slid the refund sheet out from under Denise's elbow, and moved her aside with one hip. "Go win something."

Ellen handed her daughter the departure list. Kate looked up from it at Ray, all of him, boots to crew cut, the way she would look at someone coming in on a stretcher.

"You're R.G."

"Yes."

"Your color's good, and you came up the side out of the traffic, so you know how a room moves." She went back to the list. "Fine. She put you on the strip board in her own capitals. I'm thirty-one. I have never been on the strip board. I've been on a refrigerator."

Ray didn't answer.

Ellen took a house ball off the rack behind lane 8 and walked down toward lane 5. Behind her the counter phone rang, and Kate picked it up without looking.

"Kettle Lanes. We close at four today. Nobody drives after four."

* * *

Thirty-one people were left on the burnt-orange settees, coats in their laps. The Linda Gagnon Memorial stood on the scorer's table between lanes 5 and 6, and Verna kept the official sheet beside it with her watch face up. Gil Arsenault had paid an entry for the morning round and stayed in his overcoat to watch.

Ray unzipped the blue bag on the settee and bowled the final with Linda's ball.

In the first string Ellen's opening ball sailed past the lob line and came down twice. The foul light came on and the pins didn't count. Denise, waiting her turn on lane 6 for the Lakeview Diner, graded it to the room. "Pretty height. Doesn't count. You'll live." Ellen finished the string at sixty-one and did not write anything down.

In the second string she brought her hand down six inches without anybody touching her wrist, and the ball stayed on the lane. Across the pair Denise converted every spare she left, all four of them, without once looking pleased about it. By the third string Gagnon & Son was behind by more than any one bowler could make up, and everyone on the settees could do that arithmetic.

Ellen's tenth frame came last. Her first ball chopped the right side off clean. Her second took two more and left two pins standing at the far left corner, with one fallen pin lying across the deck in front of them, its head pointed at them.

Everything she had done in this building so far she had done well in front of people, or done at night where nobody saw. This was the first thing she had done badly in front of all of them, and she had chosen it, at the counter, on Verna's binder. She picked up the third ball.

"The wood stays on the lane," she said, under her breath, to nobody. "You play what's left."

She put it low, at the fallen pin's foot. The pin went in head first and took the corner pin, and the corner pin took the one beside it, in that order.

Nobody said anything about it. Denise bowled her tenth on lane 6 and picked up her spare with the second ball.

Verna ran the column twice. "With handicap, the Lakeview Diner, by twenty-three pins."

Ray lifted the Memorial off the scorer's table in both hands and held it out to Denise. She took it.

The thirty-one stood up off the settees and clapped, the Highway Department loudest from the back, and they kept at it. Denise held the trophy against her faded red polo.

"Well, it's a nice enough—" She stopped and tried again. "It always did look better than the—"

That one went too. She put her free hand flat over her mouth and kept it there.

Gil came past the scorer's table on his way to the door, last of the Halsey Falls cars, coat buttoned.

"Mrs. Lavoie. Second ball, eighth frame. I counted it right this time."

He was through the vestibule before she could take her hand down.

Ellen had put her own daughter on the departure list at 3:58, the last line, in Denise's car, because Denise lived past the narrows and the camp was on her way. Kate zipped her parka to the chin at the vestibule door.

"You were great," she said. "You were annoying, but you were great."

Out in the lot Denise leaned on the horn.

At 4:40 Ellen stood at the steel desk in the office and ran the tape with the refunds out and Sunday at zero. She ran it again from the top. Both runs came out at $30,850 and $25,600, over both of Judy's circles on paper, and Judy would read the paper Monday with the underwriter's rules open beside it.

She stayed on her feet. She took the 02/27 strip down from the far end of the board and printed under REMARKS: WX. MOVED FROM 1800. LAKEVIEW DINER / D. LAVOIE. Then she pinned it back where it had hung since December.

* * *

It had taken Ray an hour to put lane 9's table right properly, now that nobody was waiting on it. The lanes were dark. Snow still ticked against the glass block, and the building had gone quiet enough to hear the baseboard cool.

At the pegboard by the pit door he wrote on the card. 02/27. 0640. LANE 09. He held the pencil over REASON.

"Your reason column's blank," Ellen said from the office door.

"Didn't have one that fit."

"You wrote 'Not mine. Checked anyway' about a light bracket."

"That one fit."

He left it blank and followed her into the office. The tape lay torn off beside the adding machine. The door half of the raffle cash sat squared in a stack next to it, rubber band and all. Hollis was due at five-thirty for the lot, and nobody else was coming.

He took his key ring out of his coat. The back-door key came off under his thumbnail, and he laid it on the steel desk. Arlene's brass key stayed on the ring with its orange tag, and the ring went back in his pocket. Beside the key he laid his copy of the release. The registry's stamp sat in the corner with its book and page, and the date over his signature was February sixteenth.

"I signed off the first refusal," he said. "It's been at the registry eleven days."

She put her glasses on. She read the date, then the stamp, then the date again, then the stamp.

"This is dated the sixteenth." She set each word down by itself. "I asked you this morning. I asked you in the pit believing you would get this building if I missed."

"I know. I read it back."

At ten-forty he had stood in that pit with this paper in his coat. She had a hundred and fifty people to get down Kettle Road, and a stamp from the registry would not have put one of them in a car.

She took her glasses off by the cord and let them hang against her vest. Her eyes had gone wet. Her voice had stayed level through all of it, and he had listened all day, on the radio and through the pit wall, to what it cost her to keep it there.

Ray kept quiet.

"I'm through wanting to own it," he said finally. "I want to be asked."

She picked the back-door key up off the desk, put it in his palm, and closed his fingers over it with her own hand.

Linda's trophy had gone down Kettle Road at two minutes to four, in the back seat of Denise's car, and he had handed it over himself. There was something he meant to say to Ellen. It would keep until she had slept.

She sat down in the desk chair. He had not seen her sit once all day.

She slid the rubber band off the raffle cash, split the stack with the edge of her hand, and pushed half of it across the desk to him.

"That's the derby's half. It goes in the HELD bag tonight. Count it twice, please, because the treasurer is going to count it three times."
