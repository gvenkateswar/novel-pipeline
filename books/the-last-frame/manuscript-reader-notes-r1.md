# Manuscript reader notes, round 1

**Draft read:** `books/the-last-frame/manuscript.md`, Oct 29, after revision round 1. Ten chapters, read start to finish in one pass. I read nothing else in the folder.

**Verdict first:** I finished it. I finish everything, so that proves nothing. A reader with other books on her phone, who has already been bored by two books like this, gets through Chapter 3 now, because the storm and the first kiss arrive by about 26%. She drops in Chapter 4 or 5, when the book turns into lineage rates, bylaws and a bank letter. After Chapter 3, the only things pulling me forward were the misheard clause and the "third item." The money plot never pulled me, not once. I never doubted she would make the number.

**Warning ("I'd keep reading, but..."):** I would keep reading past Chapter 3, but only because I could tell the misheard condition was a bomb, and I wanted to see it go off. Between Chapter 3 and Chapter 6 the book spends its time on procedure while I wait for that.

## Where I would have quit

**Most likely, for good:** Chapter 4, the office scene after party Saturday (about 35–37% in), at:

> "At closing I inherited Arlene's September agreement with the league, along with her fuses. It adopts the bylaws, and whatever the January meeting votes holds to the end of the season."

That's the third piece of finance or procedure in one chapter, after Danny reads the Concord letter aloud and the $700-a-week lineage figure. The radio scene, which was the reason to be here, has just ended. What the chapter promises next is a league meeting. This is right where the author quit the earlier draft (29%), and the Concord letter at about 30% is the first drift:

> "Says here the bank in Concord'll lend you most of whatever the appraiser puts on the alley, if it comes to you buying it, and the rest comes out of your own pocket. Says that's good through New Year's Eve, and then it's gone."

**Second likeliest:** Chapter 5, the vote (about 42–44%), at:

> "The secretary will read the bylaws in full," Verna said, and did, dues and nights and lob line.

The reader just sat through the bylaws in Chapter 4 and is now hearing them read in a meeting. Ray's speech that follows is a recap of the plot so far: "The credit union would've lent me against the store to buy this place off her clean, and I let their five o'clock go by on the third of December..."

## Where I got bored (quoted, with position)

1. **Ch 4, office scene (about 35–37%).** From "Then you will want to know what your league costs me," through "Will they vote the way you stand?" Rates, a 2004 notice, an inherited agreement, a bylaw clause, who moved it. The one human beat ("I did") is buried in it.
2. **Ch 5, the vote (about 42–44%).** Verna reading bylaw text verbatim ("The lineage and the house's contribution to the prize fund shall hold at the rates of the September agreement until the January meeting has voted upon them. Adopted January, two thousand five, on the motion of R. Gagnon."), then Ray's recap speech, then Verna reading his words back as minutes. The vote result isn't in doubt, because he already said "Mostly."
3. **Ch 8, the counter (about 75–76%).** "Agreed. Nine teams entered for Linda's cup alone... That leaves me a hundred and fifteen dollars under the number, with every lane of the Open full. Page two refunds the Open in cash if weather stops play..." and then the notary paragraph. This is the reconciliation scene, the emotional payoff of the fight, and half of it is arithmetic.
4. **Ch 7, Verna re-reading the January minute word for word (about 62%):** "Mr. Gagnon states that the owner gave him her word, on the night of the storm, in the pit behind lane five..." I'd already heard it in full in Chapter 5. My eyes skipped it.
5. **Ch 7, the hood (about 59%):** "Every eight dollars of this that goes on Arlene's note the day you close takes a dollar off what they need you to make." I took it on faith and moved on.
6. **Ch 9, the refund line (about 85%):** "She said it to the second captain in the same words. The third said..." It's deliberate, and it's still a list of envelopes.
7. **Ch 5, the opening:** Kate's sheet-cake riff is charming but runs long, and Ellen's paragraph about holiday-shift bids is backstory I didn't need.

The three most boring passages in the book are items 1, 2 and 3 above.

## Where I got confused (across chapters)

- **Ch 1:** "he lost this place to you by eight days." Lost it how? A bid? An offer? The book never tells me.
- **Ch 1–2:** The deal structure. She owns it but owes Arlene $250K on March 1; Ray "signed behind" her and buys at appraised value if she misses; her bank lends only if the winter clears $30K and the money stays put. I had all of it by mid-Chapter 2, but only by working at it, and I never understood why a bank would set that condition.
- **Ch 2, the clock scene:** She lays a new key on his counter at 4:59 and they both watch the clock. I didn't know what calling would have done, since she hadn't agreed to sell, or what the key meant. Chapter 3 explains it ("It was a no to selling." / "The rest of it was a key."), a chapter late for the scene the book builds hardest.
- **Ch 2:** "The house." / "Not the rest." / "I swear." It stays cryptic until Chapter 5, and isn't fully clear until Chapter 9.
- **Ch 3:** "two women from Denise's team, the Lakeview Diner." I couldn't tell whether Lakeview Diner was Denise's team or the next item in a list. That mattered in Chapter 10.
- **Ch 4:** The new pool sheet: "Nothing on it said what it was for." I gathered it was a bet on the two of them, and Danny took Valentine's. I wasn't certain until Chapter 10 struck Valentine's through.
- **Ch 6–7, what Ray believed and when.** In the bob-house he asks "Did you know I didn't hear it?" and gets "I said it." At the store he says "You answered me." So he has concluded she knew. Then in Chapter 7 he publicly takes the fault, and after she confirms it he reacts as if it's news: "The sign, Mrs. Daly. You knew." I worked out that "the sign" means the kiss under the sign. It took a reread.
- **Ch 8, opening line:** "Ray had called the match to bring Ellen's two walkout teams home." Gil and Pine Ridge were one mention in Chapter 6, and the scene starts with a summary.
- **Ch 9:** "The D-side... I want you on it with me." Is this a proposal, a partnership, a job? Then she refuses his money *because* she asked him onto it, which seemed backwards on first read.
- **Ch 10:** Kate's "Like this." It took a second to read as "call me first, like this."
- **Ch 10, the Memorial:** Denise bowls on lane six, which was Ray's team's lane in Chapter 1. "The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins." Denise "sat down on the settee and folded her hands in her lap." Won or lost? I only knew when she took the cup out of the case.
- **Ch 10:** "That goes back to the credit union. Hold that for me. I'll walk it back myself." Is Danny holding it or taking it back?
- **Ch 10:** "You're in the other chair," Ellen said. It took a moment to work out that the phone was on the chair and Ray was "in" it.

## Where I stopped believing

- **The misheard clause (Ch 3).** Fifteen inches apart, and a strike drowns exactly five words. The book knows it's a device ("On the first word of it Denise's ball came down") and the timing announces it. I accepted it the way you accept a sitcom door. I didn't believe it.
- **Ellen's silence at the vote (Ch 5).** Her whole identity is "I wrote it up myself before anyone asked." She lets Ray put her half-sentence into the minutes before twenty captains, and lets Dale's crew pay on it. The page says why ("priced in him"), and I followed the reasoning without believing she'd do it in that room.
- **Ch 2, the key at 4:59.** A woman who demands readbacks answers a yes/no business question with an unexplained key and silence. It's lovely as an image and out of character as a communication.
- **Ch 8, Ray puts up Ellen's third Tuesday team without asking her.** Hollis names the risk ("She know that?"). The safer option, asking her, is never closed, and nobody ever mentions it again.
- **Ch 8, Ellen refuses the notary line because Main Street would watch her go into Granite Lakes.** This is the least believable choice in the book. She is a procedures person, she has just said Arlene's bed is the stake, and she skips the one formality on a release of a recorded contract for privacy. Arlene also ordered that line and then cut the lawyer out ("It won't come back through your office"). Two careful people skip the same step, and the plot needs that step skipped.
- **Ch 10, everything lands at four o'clock.** The Pines holds the room "through today and no longer," Arlene's van goes "at four," and the wire has to go "before four." It's three clocks set for one scene, and the crisis costs one signature and a walk up Main Street.
- **Kate dissolves the oath in one call ("Then do it.").** It's warm, but the obstacle that kept Ellen's savings out of the plot for nine chapters is gone in two lines.
- **Ch 6–7, Ray offers $48,000** to the woman he just found out let him vouch for a half-truth. In character for a man who wants in. But he offers it days after "Did you know I didn't hear it?", and the hurt seems to pause for it.

  - "Her voice was kind and a little bored, and under it she was still counting." (Ch 3)
  - "Her voice went out over the chairs courteous and clear, and under it she was counting Verna's second hand." (Ch 5)

  - "For the first time in her working life she let a rounded number stand." (Ch 9)
  - "She heard herself use the short words with him for the first time since the sign, and she did not take them back." (Ch 5)

- **Every Ray comparison is a machine:** "the way a motor runs fast before it's warmed through," "the way he'd seat a part he meant to keep," "the way he heard a pinsetter from the lot when a clutch was about to go." It's deliberate, and it's formula by the third.
- **Scene-ending buttons, nearly every scene:** "That's the bell," Normie said. / "Honey." / "Unable." / "I know what time it is." / "Then I keep my commitment." / "Where'll you be?" / "Her hand went out for it." / "Not yet." / "Ten." / "Both words." Individually good. Together, every scene closes on a clipped line and a door click.

## The ending

**Did it pay the promises?** Mostly:
- Locker fourteen: paid.
- The pool sheet: paid ("every square pencilled in but the last").
- The key: paid, nicely ("That's not asking." / "Will you take it?").
- The K: paid.
- The third item: paid.
- Denise and the cup: paid.
- Arlene's bed: paid.

**Scene or ceremony?**
- *Lane seven:* a scene.
- *The Memorial:* a scene with small stakes.
- *The bank:* ceremony dressed as crisis.
- *The sign:* ceremony, but a good one. "Both words" is a quiet, earned last line.

**The bank scene and the notary condition.** I half believed it. I could see why the bank cared: Ray's right to buy is recorded at the Laconia registry, an unnotarized release won't record, and so the title is clouded and Judy won't wire. The page says just enough for that ("the registry won't take it and I can't wire over it"). I didn't believe how we got there. Ellen skips the notary for privacy with Arlene's bed at stake, and Arlene, who ordered the line, cuts out the one person who'd chase it. And once it surfaces it costs nothing: one signature, one walk up Main Street. "I read that line in February and let it go. That one is mine." is a good echo of her character, and too tidy.

**Did the ending feel earned?** The romance ending, yes. The money ending, no. It's resolved by a daughter's phone call and Ellen's savings, which were in the drawer the whole book.

## Would I recommend it? Would I buy the next one?

**Recommend:** Narrowly, yes, to readers of quiet small-town literary fiction with older leads (the Strout / Russo / Haruf shelf) who enjoy procedure and dry humor and don't need much heat or plot. No to romance readers who came for banter density and momentum. They'll stall in Chapters 4 and 5.

**Buy the next book by this author:** No. Maybe, on a free sample.

**Star rating:** 3 of 5. This is my ceiling, not my floor. I run high, and I expect outside readers at 2 to 2.5.

**Character I'll remember:** Ellen at the gooseneck: "Lanes one through ten, cleared to bowl." Then Hollis, "which from Hollis was a measurement."

**The chapter where I'd most likely have put it down for good:** Chapter 4 (office scene), with Chapter 5 (the vote) a close second.

**The one change at the outline level that would have done the most for me:** cut the January vote and the bylaws subplot, and give the money one number the reader can see. Then the fight over Linda's name and the clause Ray never heard carries Chapters 4–6 directly, instead of waiting behind a rate increase, a meeting and a bank covenant.

## Twelve questions, from the page alone

1. **Why the bank cared about the notary, and why nobody caught it:** Ray's contract with Ellen is on record at the Laconia registry, and the registry won't take an unnotarized release, so Judy can't wire over Ray's recorded claim. Ellen saw the notary line and refused it so Main Street wouldn't watch her go into Granite Lakes, Ray signed above it without looking, and Arlene had told the lawyer the paper would not come back through his office. So it wasn't missed: it was skipped on purpose, and the page says so ("I read that line in February and let it go").
2. **The Saturday-night shortfall, Sunday, and the paydown:** On Saturday night she was $1,715 under $30,000. Sunday's snack bar brought her to $1,100 short (the page gives "twenty-eight thousand nine hundred" and says "Sunday's snack bar closed part of the gap," so about $615, by subtraction only). The $8,800 paydown (eight times $1,100) came out of Ellen's own savings, onto Arlene's note, with "none of it" Ray's.
3. **Why the winter's own cash can't go on Arlene's note:** The bank's condition from Chapter 2 is that the winter must clear $30,000 "with every dollar it made still sitting in the account," and Judy repeats it ("The winter's cash stays where it is. Anything else is new money"). The page states the rule, and only implies the reason: those dollars are the proof of the winter.
4. **Why Ellen doesn't take Ray's money in Ch 09:** He had offered once and won't ask twice, she won't ask him, and "she had asked him onto the position, and the position was hers to pay for." Behind that is her Chapter 7 line, "If I take it I'll never know which of us carried it."
5. **Why she refunds all ten evening teams instead of moving them to Sunday morning:** She offered the ten captains Sunday morning at noon and every one said no: work on Monday, and for most a long drive home to Massachusetts.
6. **What binds Ellen to the league's January vote:** At closing she inherited Arlene's September agreement with the league, which adopts the bylaws, and a bylaw Ray moved in January 2005 holds the September rate until the January meeting votes, and whatever it votes holds to the end of the season.
7. **Lakeview Diner, and who won the Memorial:** Lakeview Diner is Denise's team (inferred from Ch 3's "Denise's team, the Lakeview Diner" and Denise taking the cup from the case), and it beat Ray's Gagnon and Son by two pins. I had to work this out, because Denise bowls on lane six and the moment of victory is left ambiguous.
8. **What Ray put at risk at Pine Ridge, and why Gil took it:** If Ray lost, his own team, Gagnon and Son, would leave Kettle Lanes for Pine Ridge ("her third Tuesday team you're putting up," Hollis says), and Ray would pay the two walkout teams' lineage there for the rest of the season. Gil took the match because he had his night either way, with half the Tuesday division eating pizza in his lounge, and because Ray's name on his Tuesday sheet "is worth two more teams to me by April."
9. **How Ray giving Linda's name differs from Ellen taking it:** Ray is "the man it belongs to," and he gives it publicly, freely and from next year on, because Linda would have entered. Ellen took it without asking, to make her margin, after giving her word that the Memorial would stay, and while hiding the condition.
10. **The K:** Ray fits the glass at the top of the ladder. That's allowed because the Laconia shop's man set the new transformer in the cabinet, the disconnect on the pole is open, and Ray has the shop's sheet, which Ellen read twice. So the qualified work was hired out, as she insisted in Chapter 4. Only the K was dark because it ran on a transformer of its own that failed, so the rest of the sign burned on.
11. **The $48,000:** Ray's savings, as a cashier's check from the credit union. Ellen refused it, he carried it unwritten in his jacket all month, and on bank day he handed the envelope to Danny to go back to the credit union, though the page's "Hold that for me. I'll walk it back myself" leaves it unclear who returns it.
12. **Is 187 the house record?** It is the Women's House High Single, per the card in the case. The page doesn't say whether it's the overall house record: cannot tell.
