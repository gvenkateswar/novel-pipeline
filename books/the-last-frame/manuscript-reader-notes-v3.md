# Manuscript reader notes: The Last Frame, v3

**Draft read:** `books/the-last-frame/manuscript.md`, the v3 assembly of Oct 30, 2026, from Chapter 1 through the Epilogue. The orchestrator gives it as 32,495 words; I did not count. I opened no other file in the book folder.
**Positions** are given as chapter plus manuscript line, with a rough percentage of the book.

---

## Read this before the scores

- **I'd keep reading, but.** Chapters 4 to 6 and the back half of 8 are about paper: a backup contract, a release, a loan threshold, a hold and a wire. In those stretches the only question pulling me forward was "will she clear thirty thousand," and I couldn't feel it, because I never understood the rule.
- **This is where I would have quit for good:** Chapter 6, at Danny's "Get all the way out of her paper and I'll sign this one." (line 1124, about 46%). That made three scenes in a row of people passing a document across a table.
- **The surprises are in the texture and never in the plot.** I saw every big turn coming at least a chapter early, and most of them by Chapter 3 (table below).
- On the 29% mark where the author quit v1: in this draft 29% falls in Chapter 4, in the "Kettle Maintenance" radio scene and the staff party. I would have got past it. The radio banter and Normie's "Then he can go stand behind hers" carried me there. The draft loses me later, in Chapter 6.

## Confusion

**The money, which was asked about specifically:**
- **What she owed: clear.** A quarter of a million to Arlene on March first (Ch 1).
- **The rule for getting the loan: not clear.** "Clears thirty thousand, with every dollar it made still sitting in the account" (Ch 2, line 232). Is thirty thousand revenue, profit or deposits? I never found out. Ch 10, line 2202, says Arlene's best year was "twenty-one thousand, near enough" and "Thirty is the line," so I guessed revenue. That was a guess.
- **What she was short: half clear.** The figure goes from +$600 if every lane fills (Ch 8) to $1,400 under after the storm (Ch 9) to $900 under after Sunday (Ch 10). I could follow those numbers only because each one is announced. I couldn't do the arithmetic myself, and a listener won't keep three dollar figures in her head.
- **What fixed it: not clear.** The daughter's savings. But Ch 5 says "my own money lowers the line, slowly, and all of it wouldn't buy back half of January," and Ch 10 says "It takes a good many of your dollars to buy down one of the winter's." There is a ratio, and it is never given. So when Kate asks "How much is more than half?" and Ellen answers "Fourteen hundred dollars, and whatever Judy's committee puts on top of it," the answer doesn't connect to the question. Then the money is wired to *Arlene's note*, not into the account, and that "puts you at the line, with nothing to spare." I had to build my own theory (paying down principal lowers the threshold) to follow it. And after all that, the committee still has to vote, and it decides on character.
- **Why Kate's permission:** "the house my daughter let me spend." Why was the house Kate's to allow? Was there a husband, an inheritance? Nothing on the page says, and I wondered every time it came up.
- **The backup contract and the release:** I followed it in outline. He buys at appraisal if she misses; the release removes that, so a miss becomes a bank sale. But the reasons each person wants it signed or unsigned change scene to scene, and in the end the decision turns on "beside" versus "behind."

**Other confusion:**
- **Ch 2, line 370:** she lays her front key on his counter at 4:59. In the moment I couldn't tell whether it was a yes, a no, a dare or a bribe. Ch 3 clears it up ("It was a no to selling." "The rest of it was a key.").
- **Ch 3, line 413:** "The captain turned from the counter…" Who is the captain? Two lines later Ray says "She's right, Dale," and I worked out that the captain is Dale. The book calls him "the captain" twice more before it uses his name again.
- **Ch 6, line 1082:** "a man who had kept the store a museum six years and another in a padlocked locker down at the alley." I read it twice and still had to guess what "another" referred to.
- **Ch 6, line 1210:** "I had my blade up, Ray." It took a beat to understand that Hollis saw the kiss from his plow.
- **Ch 8, line 1690, against Ch 6, line 1118 and Ch 2:** In Ch 2 Ray does not make the call. In Ch 6 Danny takes "the credit-union form from December" out of the drawer, unsent. In Ch 8 Ray says "I sent it through the Thursday after you put that key on my counter." So which is it? I stopped and flipped back.
- **Ch 8, lines 1688–1690:** two Ray paragraphs in a row, and the second opens with a new "he said." For a moment I thought Ellen was speaking.
- **Ch 9, line 1835:** "Road agent, Kettle Lanes." She is calling him, but aloud it sounds as if she is announcing herself as the road agent.
- **Ch 10, line 2320:** "The shop's man had also taken out the jumper Arlene's sign man had put across the K… The disconnect on the pole was open." I don't know what a jumper is, and the moment depends on it.
- **Hollis's jobs.** He is the league's lane-sheet man, a plow driver, twenty-eight years with Fish and Game, chair of the derby, and in Ch 8 the new coach with the clipboard. I kept rebuilding him.

## Where I stopped believing

- **Ch 9, lines 1951–1953, and the Kate call:** Ray offers his name behind hers at the bank. She refuses, because "beside me, I said," and drains her daughter's safety money instead. That was the obviously safer option, and the page offered it and closed it on a preposition. Kate says it for me: "Then he's got more sense than you." Saying it out loud doesn't make me believe it.
- **Ch 8, lines 1696–1728:** to make a romantic point, Ellen pushes an 84-year-old woman to give up her guaranteed bed: "Today, Arlene. I know what I'm asking." Arlene's "I'll take the condo man's money before I'll take yours" is too convenient as permission. I liked Ellen less here, and I don't think the book meant me to.
- **Ch 10, line 2312:** the committee approves because Judy's nephew told her about the refunds at supper. That's convenient. The loan the whole book was built around is decided by an anecdote, off the page.
- **Ch 10, line 2288:** her savings put her at the line "with nothing to spare," to the dollar. Too neat.
- **Ch 6, lines 1254–1258:** Ellen walks onto the ice to pitch the derby, then hauls up Hollis's fish with her bare gloves in the water. Why? It reads as staged to get her hands cold for the glove scene.
- **Ch 7, line 1369:** Dale wanders up at the exact moment and invites himself into a private meeting about Linda's name. He's there to be apologized to.
- **Ch 1:** a careful, numbered, risk-averse former air traffic controller buys a business whose financing depends on a winter nobody has managed since Normand. The book says so itself (Ray: "Nobody's cleared thirty in this building since Arlene's Normand"), but it never shows me the moment she decided that risk was worth it. The vestibule memory in Ch 3 explains why she wanted the place, not why she bet the house on one winter.
- **The ink trail:** Ray's credit-union form (sent or not sent, above).

## What I think is going on (as a reader who has finished)

I knew how this ended by Chapter 3. Ellen keeps the alley. The dead K gets lit. Ray gets her key back, but only once she asks. The third item is "I love you." Linda's locker gets opened. Denise wins the cup she's wanted. The money gets solved by the daughter's savings and a bank that likes her. The book's secret is the airplane night and the car in the driveway, and I had it from Ch 3 and Ch 5. The Epilogue's new details (the bus, the two knocks on the glass) are the only new information about it, and they're the best part of the Epilogue. Normie's new betting sheet, with "R. Gagnon" on June 30, reads as a wedding or proposal bet, and that is the book's last wink. Nothing on the page is withheld that I wanted. Nothing was left open when I closed it.

## The listener pass (whole book, heard through a TTS voice)

**What a voice will trip on:**
- **Scene breaks** are escaped asterisks (`\* \* \*`). A voice that doesn't strip them will say "asterisk asterisk asterisk" about forty times.
- **Ms. and Mrs.** Ch 1 and Ch 4 hang jokes on "Mrs. Daly" / "Ms." and "Ms. Daly. Net thirty." If the voice reads "Ms." as "M S," both jokes die.
- **Too many clock times.** "six minutes past six," "twenty past seven," "a quarter to ten," "ten to nine," "five to nine," "a quarter past eleven," "Just before half past eleven," "a quarter past six," "ten past four," "a quarter past four," "twenty to two," "a quarter to four." Aloud they blur into one long timetable.
- **Dollar figures in a row.** A quarter of a million, thirty thousand, twenty-one thousand, six hundred, fourteen hundred, nine hundred. A listener can't hold them, and the plot depends on them.
- **Years.** "'seventy-four," "'ninety-six," "'oh-eight," "'ninety-eight," "'ninety-four," "'eighty-four," "nineteen seventy-one," "nineteen seventy-nine." The listener has to work out which winter is which, and "'oh-eight" may come out oddly.
- **Candlepin jargon with no gloss:** "lineage," "strings," "boxes," "wood," "kickback," "deck," "four horsemen." Eyes skim past them; ears stop.
- **Italic thoughts** (*No readback.*, *Take it.*, *Now you'll clear.*) are invisible aloud and come out as narration.
- **Long sentences with "and… and… and":**
  - Ch 1, line 5 (the opening)
  - Ch 1, line 35 ("She stood in the doorway with her heat going out…")
  - Ch 1, line 99 (the numbered items)
  - Ch 3, line 575 ("and his laugh… and the frost… and Hollis at seven… and a sweep motor…")
  - Ch 5, line 921 (the New Year's kiss is one paragraph of about 300 words)

  Some are deliberate. Aloud, all of them are hard to follow.
- **Unclear speakers:**
  - Ch 3, line 413 ("The captain")
  - Ch 6, lines 1114–1124: Danny speaks four times in a row and Ray says nothing, so a listener assumes the lines alternate.
  - Ch 8, lines 1688–1690: two Ray paragraphs in a row.
  - Ch 9, line 1835 ("Road agent, Kettle Lanes")
  - Ch 10, lines 2116–2142: Judy, Kate and Judy on the phone, all on speaker.
- **Readbacks.** The Ch 9 readback repeats a paragraph of logistics almost word for word. Read aloud, that is the same information twice in a row.
- **Sentences built the same way:** "X did not move, and neither did Y" recurs across the book; aloud it becomes a rhythm.

**Best passages aloud:**

## The ending

**Does it pay what Chapter 1 promised?** Yes, item by item: the lanes, the K, the key, locker fourteen, "Ray won't" (he buys his first square on Normie's new sheet), and Ellen finally asking. It's a closed ledger. That's the problem. Everything Chapter 1 planted comes back in order, so the last third reads like a checklist.

**Did it feel earned?**
- The emotional side, mostly yes. The Ch 7 cruelty and the Ch 8 apology are paid for, and "Third. I love you." works because the book waited eight chapters for it.
- The plot side, no. The money resolves on savings that land exactly at the line and a committee swayed by a supper-table story.

**Did the last line land?** Quietly. "Tell me what you'd miss" answers Kate's "You could ask me something sometime" from Ch 5. A reader who remembers that line gets a small click. A listener who doesn't remember it hears the book end on a mother asking her daughter a question, two scenes after the couple's story finished. The bigger last line is "Both words" at the end of Ch 10, and it's a good one.

## Would I buy the author's next book?

**No.** The writing is good enough that I'd open the sample. But this book spent its middle third making me watch people negotiate a contract I couldn't follow, and I'd expect the next one to make me sit through its paperwork too.
