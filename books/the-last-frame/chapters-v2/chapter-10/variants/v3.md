# Chapter 10: Ice-Out

The pocketknife took a curl of cedar off the pencil and laid it on the counter with the others, and then another, and the point that had been done two strokes back went on getting finer. Ten lanes of score sheets waited by his elbow. This was the last pencil in her cigar box.

Over the fryer the radio had the Laconia station on, and the Laconia station had Hollis.

"Four inches of slush on top at the public access, and water up through every hole I cut. Derby's off for Sunday."

Every bob-house on the lake had a free Sunday it hadn't asked for.

She had let him in at half past six. The whole time she turned the bolt his key was in her vest pocket, and he heard it tick against her pen.

Her phone went off in the same vest. She took it out and looked at the name, and then she laid it face up on the counter between them, beside the cigar box, and touched the speaker.

A car door shut at the other end, and an engine ran under it.

"Am I on speaker?"

"You are. Mr. Gagnon is here."

"Mr. Gagnon."

"Ray."

"Ray." A turn signal started ticking in a parking lot in Manchester. She sounded like her mother, run faster. "Okay. I said I'd sleep on it, and I slept on it, most of it, which for me is a— It doesn't matter." The ticking stopped. "Then do it."

Ellen's hands lay flat on the counter on either side of the phone.

"And call me first next time."

The engine idled. Behind it a door hissed open on a hospital lobby and shut, and the line went quiet long enough to take in the room: the fryer coming up to heat, the radio, the knife, which had stopped.

"Like this."

He laid the knife down by the pencil. "I'd have said no."

Kate's engine cut out. "You weren't asked."

A car door opened, and somewhere behind her a page went out over a speaker, a name he didn't catch.

"Is he the one who followed you out as far as the plow had been?"

Then the line was dead, and the screen went dark between them.

\* \* \*

The small brass key came off his ring hard. He stood at the counter on the customer side and worked it round the split steel with his thumbnail while the house filled up behind him. The Highway crew had come straight in off the night's plowing with their eyes red to the rims, Dale Hutchins among them with his hood still up. Gil Arsenault stood at the rail in his good overcoat, over from Halsey Falls for Linda's day. Judy from the bank had a place in the standing room by the shoe cubbies, her pocketbook held in front of her in both hands. The thaw came in on everybody's boots.

The key came free. He put the ring back in his pocket.

"Kettle Lanes," he said, "this is Gagnon. Request."

Ellen set down her pen. "Gagnon, say request."

He laid the brass key on the wood between them. "That's locker fourteen's. It's on your wall. I'd like it open, and you there when it is."

She looked at the key, and then at him. Then she picked it up and came round the end of the counter.

Denise was at the case with a cloth on the Memorial cup, because it was Sunday, going over a cup she had already done. Her cloth stopped. At the scorer's table on lane one Verna had her binder open and her pen lifted off the page.

The padlock was stiff from six winters with no hand on it. Ellen put the key in. It went to the shoulder, and she turned it, and it would not turn for her. Her knuckles went white across the brass. A foot from his ear the case glass ticked as the heat came up in it. He put his hand over hers on the key, and the lock sat a second against both of them and gave, with a small dry snap he could have named from the lot.

She took the padlock off and hung it on the hasp and opened the door.

Linda's bag sat on the floor of the locker with her shoes beside it, the laces tucked in, and her ball on the shelf above. In the side pocket of the bag was a sheet of paper folded in four. Ellen read any paper in her building. She took it out and unfolded it and read it, and handed it to him without a word.

It was a league sheet from a Tuesday, the last one Linda bowled, three strings in her own hand with the sevens crossed, and there was nothing on it anybody would frame. In the second string she had missed an easy spare and put a small pencil tick beside it, which she did when she was cross with herself, at the alley or over the checkbook. He folded it on its creases and put it in his shirt pocket.

Then he lifted the ball off the shelf, cold from the wall.

Denise had laid the cloth down on the case. "I lost to that ball twice, Ray, and the second time was worse, because she came over after and told me my third string was the best she ever saw me bowl, which it was, near enough, and I still lost, so don't you go bowling it sentimental today, because I'll beat you anyway and then I'll have to feel bad about it all spring."

"I'll bowl it about as well as I bowl my own," he said, "and you've watched me bowl my own thirty years."

He came back past the counter with the ball against his chest. The gooseneck was already bent across to the customer side at his height, and Ellen was taking her hand off it. He hadn't asked. He bent to it.

"From next year Linda's name goes on the Open, and anybody that pays the entry can win it, because Linda would've entered on day one."

Hollis, from the settee by lane six: "Committee never voted on a name." Then he opened his waterproof notebook and wrote it down.

Hollis's golf pencil had him back in at anchor on the Gagnon and Son sheet. His first box with her ball was on lane five. His thumb went looking for the place hers had worn into it, a dull patch about the size of a dime, and his thumb was too big for it and rode half off it all afternoon. His strings were ordinary. Each time he looked up, with all ten lanes going at once, Ellen was behind the counter with her eyes shut.

By the end it was lanes five and six, Lakeview Diner against Gagnon and Son, the other eight lanes finished and their bowlers standing on the approaches to watch. Verna ran her pen down both columns and looked up from the binder.

"The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins."

Denise sat down on the settee and said nothing at all.

The room came up onto its feet: the Highway crew off their settee with their red eyes, the Legion, Judy with her pocketbook clamped under one arm so she could clap. At the counter Ellen's hand was on the gooseneck, and she didn't key it. Denise got up and went to the case she had dusted every Sunday for six years and opened it, and took the cup out with both hands. There was no ring on the glass where it had stood.

Gil leaned on the rail. "Mrs. Lavoie." He held out a folded flyer for the spring doubles.

Denise took it and folded it once more and put it in her pocket, in front of everybody. Then she looked at Ray over the cup.

"Gil's short a partner for the spring doubles. I'm thinking about it."

Ray didn't answer it.

He lifted Linda's ball off the return on lane five and carried it past the open locker, through a room still on its feet, to the house rack between lanes four and five. He set it on the shelf among the house balls, by Ellen's tape on the steel upright, and with Ellen watching from the counter he gave it a quarter-turn, and then another, his hand still on it.

\* \* \*

His fingers worked the double knot loose on his bowling shoe. He sat on the end stool with the house lights down to the one over the counter, and across the dark room locker fourteen stood open with its padlock hung on the hasp, and Linda's ball sat on the house rack among the others, and you'd have had to know which.

Ellen came out of the office with her glasses on their cord against her vest.

"I'm going to look at seven."

"Seven's fine."

She went through the steel door by lane ten anyway. He got the other shoe off and his boots on and laced, and sat a minute with his elbows on his knees in the dark room, and then went after her.

The machines were off. She was on the iron step behind lane seven, where she had sat at New Year's with a cup of cider she didn't care for, and she had nothing in her hands now. He sat down beside her on the cold iron. The work light over his bench at the far end put their two shadows up the masking, and the pit smelled of grease and of belts going cold. Down the catwalk the motors ticked as they gave up the day's heat, one behind lane nine, then one behind lane four, then the one at his back, each in its own time, while the warmth went out of the kickback steel between his shoulders and she breathed beside him, slow, through her nose. He kept his hands on his knees. Linda's sheet was in his shirt pocket under his jacket, folded on its creases, and he left it there. Ellen's hands lay in her lap, bare, one over the other, and every so often the top one moved as though it had a pen in it, and then lay still again.

"You asked me whether New Year's too," she said. "It was. I knew, and I came down here at midnight so you'd find me."

"I'd about worked that out, in the truck."

Behind lane nine a motor gave one more tick and was done.

"Kate asked me something this morning, with you standing there."

"I heard her."

"I watched your lights turn round at the end of the plowing. I watched them all the way back to town."

She turned her hands over in her lap, palms up, and looked at them, and then at him, the cord of her glasses moving on her vest with her breathing.

"The night of the storm I told you two things and kept the third, because it wasn't in order. It is now. Third. I love you."

He took her hands in both of his. The curtain behind lane seven hung still, and the motor at his back had gone quiet.

"Third item, you love me." He took a breath. "And one you left off. I've loved you since you kept me out in the snow knocking while you finished your tape."

Her hands turned in his and held on.

Ellen moved first. She came up off the iron step to reach him, and her reading glasses swung out on their black cord and caught on the pull of his jacket zipper. The cord jerked her up short an inch from him.

He laughed.

It went down the dark catwalk and came back off the far wall. Ellen looked down at the cord and up at him, and left it where it was. Her hand went to his collar, and then to his jaw, where the day's gray was, and stayed there. He put his hand to the back of her head, into the short hair, and closed the inch himself. He kissed her with the laugh still in him. Her mouth was cold from the pit, and warmed. He heard the cord creak against the zipper pull. His other arm went round her inside his open jacket, glasses and cord and all. Ellen made a small sound in her throat and kissed him back, slowly. Her thumb went along his jaw once and came back. Somewhere down the catwalk the last motor ticked and quit, and neither of them hurried. When Ellen drew back it was only far enough to breathe, her forehead against his, the cord still caught between them.

She unhooked the glasses from his zipper with two fingers, one turn of the cord and then the other. "Twice behind lane seven and once under the sign. I've kept count."

She sat back down on the iron step beside him. Her hand went to her vest pocket, where his key was, and lay flat on it, and came away with nothing in it.

\* \* \*

"You're in the other chair."

Ellen's voice came from a little way off the phone. Behind it heels crossed marble, and the drive-up tube landed with a thunk. His phone lay face up by the register, and at the other end hers lay face up on the chair beside her at Judy's desk. The committee had sat at two, and Judy was in it.

The Bench sat on their stools with their cups halfway up. Danny was at the register. Behind him, on its nail, hung the Bench's old gray sheet, every square pencilled in but the last. He had kept to his own counter all day so that nobody on Main Street could say whose money it was.

On the line a door opened. Heels came across the marble, and Judy's chair creaked round.

"Committee approved it."

His hand went flat on the counter. Nobody on the Bench drank.

Down the line, cellophane.

"She's got the butterscotch out," he said to the Bench, low.

The cellophane stopped. "I can hear you, Ray."

The chair creaked again. "Approved subject to one thing. Your contract with Arlene is on record at the county." A drawer opened and shut. "The release her lawyer drew in February was never signed in front of anybody, and title won't insure over it." Paper slid on the desk. "I like you, Ray. The underwriter has never met you. I need you at this desk, signing it again in front of me, before the wire room shuts at four, or Arlene's money sits till—"

The Pines held her room through the first.

He was moving before Judy got to the end of it. The envelope came out of his jacket, where it had ridden a month, and he put it in Danny's hand. Danny didn't open it.

"Go on. I've got the counter."

He went out under the bell and up Main Street with his jacket flat against his ribs where the envelope had been, and the cold found the place. The barber had nobody in his chair. At the pharmacy's side door a van stood with its back open, and boxes were going up into it marked THE PINES in Arlene's green-ledger script, and Arlene stood on the sidewalk in her good coat with her pocketbook on her arm and watched each one go.

"Raymond. I'll have my key back while you're passing."

He worked the old key off his ring, orange tag and all, and put it in her hand. She licked her thumb and counted it.

"One."

"That tag's about worn through."

"So am I." She shut her hand on it. "The van goes at four, and I'm going in it."

At the top of the bank steps he looked back, and she was still on the sidewalk with her hand shut, watching the door.

The drive-up tube thunked again as he came in, and his own boots went across the marble this time. Judy's glass office had the lake trout on the wall and the bowl on the desk, and on her blotter, in her hand, a slush depth a quarter inch shy of Hollis's. Ellen sat in one chair. On the other her phone lay face up and lit, and down it came his own store: the key cutter's whine, Danny cutting somebody a key.

He picked it up and ended the call and handed it to her, and sat down in the chair.

Judy turned the release round to face him with one finger. "Sign it again. Here, where I can watch you do it."

He signed it, his name in his own scrawl a second time. Judy brought her seal down on it, and he felt the crimp through the desk under his wrists.

Judy slid a second page across to Ellen. "Eighty-eight hundred, out of your savings and onto Arlene's note. New money." Her pen touched the line. "Any of it his?"

"None of it is his," Ellen said.

She signed it in one pass and capped her pen. Then, low, to him, with nobody asking: "What's left heats the camp through one bad winter."

His hand stayed on his knee.

Judy picked up her phone and called the wire room and gave it to them, and waited, and put the phone down.

"It's gone."

He looked past her, through her glass and the lobby's front window. Down Main Street the van pulled out from beside the pharmacy with Arlene in its passenger seat, and turned toward The Pines.

\* \* \*

The new K was cold in his hands at the top of the ladder, glass the length of his forearm, bent in Laconia to the old script's curl, and he seated its electrode in the housing and felt it go home. The disconnect on the pole stood open, and the shop's sheet was folded in his shirt pocket. The old K hung by its wire from the top rung. It had been dark since the winter Danny got his license.

Below him Ellen held the foot of the ladder in both hands. It went steady under his boots, and he felt her through the rails.

The plow banks along Kettle Road had gone gray in the thaw. Across the road water stood in long sheets on the lake around the bob-houses on their skids. Nothing was falling, and nobody came down the road.

He came down with the old K and laid it on the plow bank at the foot of the pole.

His ring had a gap in it where Arlene's key had hung. Ellen took his key out of her vest pocket and laid it on her open palm between them.

He looked at it. "That's not asking."

She closed her fingers on it and opened them again.

"Ray." The light was going off the lake. "Will you take it?"

He took it off her palm, warm from her vest. He opened the split end of the ring with his thumbnail and worked the key on a quarter turn at a time, slowly, into the place Arlene's had hung thirty years, and she watched every turn.

The light went the rest of the way. He stood at the foot of the pole under the sign's cabinet, and Ellen went to the disconnect and put her hand on the handle.

"Throw her, Mrs. Daly."

He waited to be corrected. She threw it.

The transformer took hold with a buzz he felt in the pole under his hand. Red came down over the plow bank and out across the wet road, and lay on the old K in the bank, lit by the letters it used to sit with and dark itself. From under the cabinet he couldn't see the letters.

"How's she read?"

Ellen walked out into the middle of Kettle Road and looked up.

"Both words."
