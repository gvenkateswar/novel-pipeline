# Chapter 10: Ice-Out

The pocketknife took the cedar off in long curls that dropped onto her counter, and he turned each pencil between strokes and laid it back in her cigar box point first, ten lanes' worth, for a Memorial that was hours off yet. Over the fryer the radio had the Laconia station on, and the Laconia station had Hollis.

"Four inches of slush on top at the public access, and water up through every hole I cut. Derby's off for Sunday."

She had let him in at half past six, and the whole time she turned the bolt his key was in her vest pocket. He'd heard it tick against her pen.

The pencil in his hand was sharp already. He went on taking it down.

Her phone rang in her vest. She took it out and looked at the name, and then she laid it face up on the counter between them, beside the cigar box, and touched the speaker.

An engine was running at the other end, and a turn signal ticked.

"Am I on speaker?"

"You are. Mr. Gagnon is here."

"Mr. Gagnon."

"Ray."

"Ray." She sounded like her mother, run faster. "Okay. I said I'd sleep on it. I slept on it. I mean I lay down on it a while. Then do it."

Ellen's hand lay flat on the counter beside the phone.

The signal quit, and the engine went round a corner. "And call me first next time." Nothing came down the line for a second but road, and on his end the fryer ticked and Hollis went on about the ice, and she let it all go down the line to her. "Like this."

The pencil had gone past sharp to nothing much. He closed the knife.

"I'd have said no."

"You weren't asked." The engine quit. A car door shut, and over a speaker somewhere a page went out for somebody, and her shoes went quick across pavement. "Mom. Is he the one who followed you out as far as the plow had been?"

The line went dead, and the screen went dark between them.

\* \* \*

The small brass key came off his ring hard. It had ridden there six winters between the truck key and the store's back door, and the ring had closed down on it, and he stood at her counter on the customer side and worked it round with his thumbnail while the house filled behind him. The Highway crew had come in off the plows with their eyes red and sat in a row on the settees by lane nine, Dale at the end of them in his town jacket. The thaw came in on their boots.

The key came free in his hand.

"Kettle Lanes," he said, "this is Gagnon. Request."

Ellen set down her pen. "Gagnon, say request."

He laid the key on the counter between them. "That's locker fourteen's. It's on your wall. I'd like it open, and you there when it is."

She picked it up and came round the end of the counter with it.

At the case Denise had a cloth on the Memorial cup, because it was Sunday, and she was pretending to dust it. He stood with his ear a foot from the glass. The heat was coming up in the building, and the case glass ticked with it, once and then again, small and dry.

Ellen put the key in the padlock. It went in to the shoulder, and she turned it, and nothing turned. Six winters of lake damp had got into the works. She set her bare fingers harder on the bow and tried again, and the brass held, and her hand stayed on it. His hand came over hers. Her knuckles were under his palm, cold, and he could feel the small bones in them shift as she took a better grip, and he turned with her, steady, both of them on the one key, until something inside the lock let go with a click he felt in his wrist and the shackle came up.

She hung the padlock on the hasp and opened the door.

Linda's bag was on the floor of the locker, the blue one with the strap she'd mended with fishing line, and her shoes beside it with the laces tucked in, and her ball on the shelf above, where she had set it on a Tuesday. In the side pocket of the bag was a paper folded in four.

Ellen read any paper that came into her building. She drew it out and unfolded it and read it standing there, and then she folded it along its own creases and handed it to him without a word.

It was a league sheet: Linda's last Tuesday, three strings in her own hand, the pencil pressed hard into every box, and nothing on it anybody would have put in a frame. In the margin by her second string she'd drawn a short flat line. He folded the sheet into his shirt pocket and buttoned the flap down over it.

Then he lifted the ball off the shelf. It came away cold from the wall, and heavier in his hand than a thing that size had any right to be, and he held it against his belt.

Denise had put the cloth down.

"Now I've lost to that ball twice, Ray, and the second time was worse because Linda was nice about it, she bought me a coffee after, can you imagine, so don't you go bowling it sentimental, because I'll beat you anyway and then I'll have to feel bad about it."

"I'll bowl it bad and ordinary, Denise, same as always, and you can feel how you like."

He came back past the counter with the ball, and the gooseneck was already bent across to his side. He leaned down to it.

"From next year Linda's name goes on the Open, and anybody that pays the entry can win it, because Linda would've entered on day one."

From the settee Hollis said, "Committee never voted on a name." Then he opened his waterproof notebook on his knee and wrote it down.

His first box with her ball was on lane five. His thumb went looking for the place hers had worn into it, a dull patch the size of a dime, and his was too big for it and sat half off the edge. His strings were ordinary. Each time he looked up, with all ten lanes going at once, Ellen was behind the counter with her eyes shut.

By the end it was lanes five and six, Lakeview Diner against Gagnon and Son, and the other eight lanes finished and standing at the rail.

Verna looked up from the binder. "The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins."

Denise sat down on the settee and said nothing at all.

For a second nobody else did either. Then the Highway crew got up off their settees, and the Legion along the rail, and Judy from the bank by the door with her pocketbook on her arm, and the room came up onto its feet a row at a time and stayed there. At the counter Ellen's hand was on the gooseneck, and she didn't key it. Denise got up and went to the case she had dusted every Sunday for six years and took the cup out with both hands.

Gil Arsenault was at the rail in his good overcoat, come over from Halsey Falls for Linda's day.

"Mrs. Lavoie." He held out the spring-doubles flyer.

Denise took it and folded it small and put it in her pocket with everybody watching. Then she came over to Ray with the cup against her chest.

"Gil's short a partner for the spring doubles. I'm thinking about it."

He didn't answer it. He lifted Linda's ball off the return on lane five and carried it through a room still on its feet to the house rack between lanes four and five. It was heavy all the way. He set it on the shelf among the house balls, by Ellen's tape on the steel upright, and with Ellen watching from the counter he gave it a quarter-turn until it sat, his hand still on it.

\* \* \*

He sat on the end stool with the room dark past him and undid the double knot on his bowling shoe. Across the dark the locker stood open with its padlock hung on the hasp, and Linda's ball sat on the house rack among the others, where anybody could take it down. Her sheet was still in his shirt pocket, folded in four. The house had gone home, and Ellen had closed out the drawer, and the ten sheets lay on the counter under her stapler with his pencils worn back down on top of them.

Ellen came out of the office in her vest and put the light out behind her.

"I'm going to look at seven."

"Seven's fine."

She went through the steel door by lane ten anyway. He heard it shut behind her, and her boots go along the catwalk and stop.

His feet ached from three strings in shoes he'd had resoled the winter nine's belt let go the first time. He got the other shoe off and his boots on and laced, and gave it a minute, and zipped his jacket against the building going cold, and went after her.

She was on the iron step behind seven, where she had sat at New Year's, with nothing in her hands. He sat down beside her. The iron was cold through his trousers. Down the catwalk the motors were giving up the day's heat one at a time, a tick from behind lane four and a while later one from behind lane nine, while the warmth went out of the kickback steel at their backs. Seven ticked once, close by. Her hands lay on her knees, palms down and bare, and the reading glasses hung on their cord against her vest, and she had the bad knee out straight along the catwalk. There was a smudge of pin grease across the back of one hand that she hadn't found yet. Through the gap under the masking the pin deck lay pale and empty, with its ten marked spots. She breathed slower than he did. He could hear it under the motors, and he listened to that a while, and to the building settling round them in the cold.

"You asked me whether New Year's too. It was. I knew, and I came down here at midnight so you'd find me."

"I'd about worked that out, in the truck."

"Kate asked me something this morning, with you standing there."

"I heard her."

His boots stood beside hers on the catwalk grating, and her left heel was worn down on the inside.

"I watched your lights turn round at the end of the plowing." She was looking at the pin deck. "I watched them all the way back to town."

Behind lane two a motor ticked and was done.

"The night of the storm I told you two things and kept the third, because it wasn't in order. It is now." She turned on the iron to face him. "Third. I love you."

He took her hands off her knees and held them in both of his. There was pencil on the side of her right forefinger from ten lanes of sheets, and he rubbed at it once with his thumb, and it stayed.

"Third item, you love me." He let a breath go. "And one you left off. I've loved you since you made me knock three times on your door so you could finish a strip of tape."

Ellen came up off the iron to reach him. Her reading glasses swung out on their black cord and hooked the pull of his jacket zipper. They jerked her up short an inch from him.

Ray laughed.

Ellen closed the inch anyway. He was still laughing against her mouth when she kissed him, and she kissed him through it until he quit. Her hand went to his collar and took hold of it, and then it came up to his jaw, cold on the stubble there. He put his hand to the back of her head, into the short hair, and kissed her back slowly. She smelled of fryer oil and the cold off the lot. The cord ran tight between them from her neck to his zipper, and neither of them did a thing about it. Down the catwalk another motor gave up a tick. Ellen drew back far enough to look at him, and then came in again, and he let her set the pace of it. He got his other arm round her on the cold iron and held on, and her forehead came down against his jaw. He could hear her breathing, and his own, and nothing else in the whole building.

Ellen unhooked the glasses from his zipper with two fingers and let them fall on their cord.

"Twice behind lane seven and once under the sign. I've kept count."

He still had her left hand. Her other hand went to her vest pocket, where his key was, and lay flat on it, and came away with nothing in it.

\* \* \*

"You're in the other chair."

Ellen's voice came from a little way off the phone. Behind it heels went across marble, and the drive-up tube landed in its slot with a thunk. Her phone lay face up on the chair beside her at Judy's desk; his lay face up on his own counter by the register. The committee had sat at two, and Judy was in it.

The Bench had their cups halfway up. Behind the register, on its nail, hung the Bench's old gray sheet, every square pencilled in but the last. He had kept to his own counter all day, so that nobody on Main Street could say whose money it was.

A door, then heels, then Judy's chair creaking round.

"Committee approved it."

His hand went flat on the counter. Nobody on the Bench drank.

Down the line, cellophane.

"She's got the butterscotch out," Ray said, low, and the Bench leaned in.

"I can hear you, Ray." A drawer shut. "It's approved subject to one thing. Your contract with Arlene is on record at the county, and the release her lawyer drew in February was never signed in front of anybody. Title won't insure over it."

Judy's pen clicked. "I like you, Ray. The underwriter has never met you. I need you at this desk, signing it again in front of me, before the wire room shuts at four, or Arlene's money sits till tomorrow."

The Pines had held her room through the first.

He was moving before Judy finished. He took the envelope out of his jacket, where it had ridden against his ribs for a month, and put it in Danny's hand. Danny didn't open it.

"Go on. I've got the counter."

He went out under the bell and up Main Street with the wind off the lake at his back. His jacket hung flat on that side now, and the cold got in where the envelope had ridden, and he went uphill past the barber's window. At the side door of the pharmacy a van stood with its back open, and boxes were going up into it, marked for The Pines in Arlene's green-ledger script. Arlene stood on the sidewalk in her good coat with her pocketbook on her arm and watched every one of them go.

"Raymond. I'll have my key back while you're passing."

He stopped and got his ring out. Her key was stiff on it, and he worked it round with his thumbnail, the orange tag dragging through last, gone soft as flannel in his pocket, and he put the key in her glove.

She licked her thumb and counted it. "One."

"That tag's about worn through."

"So am I. The van goes at four, and I'm going in it."

His ring sat light in his pocket the rest of the way up the hill.

The tube went thunk again as he came in, and his own boots went across the marble now. Judy's glass office had the lake trout on the wall and the bowl on the desk. Ellen sat in one chair. On the other her phone lay face up and still lit, and down it came his own store, the key cutter's whine with Danny at it, cutting somebody a key. He picked the phone up and ended the call and handed it to her, and sat down in the chair.

Judy turned the release round to face him. "Sign it again, here, in front of me. Your own signature, of your own free will."

He signed it. Her seal came down on the page and crimped it, and he felt the bite of it come through the desk into his wrist.

She drew the next page across to Ellen. "Eighty-eight hundred, out of your savings and onto Arlene's note. New money." Her chair creaked. "Any of it his?"

"None of it is his."

Ellen signed it in one pass and capped her pen. Then, low, with her eyes on Judy's trout: "What's left heats the camp through one bad winter."

His hand stayed on his knee.

Judy called the wire room and read it in, every number twice. She listened. She put the phone down.

"It's gone."

He looked past her, through the glass and the lobby's front window. Down Main Street the van pulled out from beside the pharmacy, with Arlene in its passenger seat, and turned toward The Pines.

\* \* \*

The new K was cold glass the length of his forearm, bent in Laconia to the old script's curl, and at the top of the ladder he seated its electrode in the housing and felt it go home. The disconnect on the pole below him was open, and the shop's sheet was folded in his jacket pocket. The old K had been dark since the winter Danny got his license, and half the town had called the place Ettle's since.

Ellen held the foot of the ladder in both hands. A gust came across the road off the lake and the ladder moved a little under his boots, and then it didn't move at all. He could feel her through the rails.

He came down with the old K under his arm, dark glass in the old curl, and laid it on the plow bank at the foot of the pole. The banks were going gray in the thaw. Across Kettle Road water stood in long sheets on the lake around the bob-houses on their skids, and the sun was low over the west shore.

His ring had a gap on it where Arlene's key had hung.

Ellen took his key out of her vest pocket and laid it on her open palm between them.

"That's not asking."

She closed her fingers on it and opened them again.

"Ray." She let a second go by. "Will you take it?"

He took it off her palm. It was warm from her vest. He opened the split end of the ring with his thumbnail and worked the key on a quarter turn at a time, slow, into the place Arlene's had hung thirty years, and she watched every turn of it.

The light was going off the lake. He stood at the foot of the pole under the sign's cabinet, and Ellen went to the disconnect and put her hand on the handle.

"Throw her, Mrs. Daly."

He waited to be corrected. She threw it.

The transformer took hold with a buzz he felt in the pole under his palm. Red came down over the plow bank and out across the wet road, and it fell on the old K lying in the bank, lit by the letters it used to sit with, and dark itself. From under the cabinet he couldn't see the letters.

"How's she read?"

Ellen walked out into the middle of Kettle Road and looked up.

"Both words."
