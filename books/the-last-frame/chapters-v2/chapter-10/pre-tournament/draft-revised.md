# Chapter 10: Ice-Out

Ray had Ellen's cigar box of pencils open on the counter and was taking every one in it to a point for the ten lanes' sheets, and the cedar came off his pocketknife in curls and lay where it fell. The pencil in his hand had been sharp a while, and he kept at it. He had asked her once, at this counter, what she'd do if her daughter said no, and he didn't ask twice.

Over the fryer the radio had Hollis on, flat as a tide table.

"Four inches of slush on top at the public access, and water up through every hole I cut. Derby's off for Sunday."

Ellen came out of the office to his elbow.

"Kate said, 'Then do it. And call me first next time. Like this.'"

The knife stopped halfway down the pencil. She laid her hand in the shavings beside his wrist, and her little finger touched his cuff and stayed there.

Behind them the radio went on to a man in Gilford with a snowblower to sell, cheap, after the winter he'd had.

She had let him in at half past six. He'd stood outside her door glass with the lot going gray behind him, and she'd come down the length of the building and turned the bolt for him, and the whole time she turned it his key was in her vest pocket, where he heard it tick against her pen when she leaned on the door. She'd said good morning and gone back to the office with her coat still on. Out on the lake every bob-house in Ambrose had a free Sunday it hadn't asked for, and none of that was coming up her drive.

He said it to the pencil. "I'd have said no."

He would have, too. It was the rest, the part she'd sworn to Kate she'd keep out of it, and he'd have kept it for her and never once asked her leave.

Ellen took the pencil out of his fingers, looked at the point he'd put on it, and stood it in the box with the others.

"You weren't asked."

\* \* \*

The brass key to locker fourteen hung on his ring between the truck key and the store's back door, the smallest thing on it, and he hadn't turned it in six years. The padlock was stiff from six winters in a cold corner, and he leaned on the key, and leaned again, and the first quarter-turn gave all at once, and he felt it go in his back teeth.

With his ear a foot from the case he could hear the glass tick as the heat came up. Beside him Denise had the case door open and a cloth going round the Memorial cup, because it was Sunday, and if anybody asked she was dusting.

The locker door came open on Linda's bag and shoes and her ball on the shelf above them, and he lifted it out. It was cold from the wall, and the last hand on it before his had been hers.

"I've lost to that ball twice," Denise said, and shut the case on the cup, "and the second time was worse, and do you know why? Because she was nice about it. So don't you go bowling it sentimental on me, Ray, because I'll beat you anyway and then I'll have to feel bad about it."

The front door let in the Highway crew off their night's plowing, hoods still up and eyes red to the rims, and the thaw came in on their boots. Dale Hutchins laid bills on the counter for the crew's food.

"Charge me for the snowstorm."

Ellen rang it and counted his change into his hand.

Judy Ostrowski from the bank came in behind her nephew off the Legion team, stopped at the counter beside Hollis, and looked down at his waterproof notebook.

"You said four inches on the radio. I had three and three-quarters at my holes."

Hollis kept his pencil on the page. "Where were your holes?"

"Where I put them."

Hollis wrote his own number down a second time.

Ray had set Linda's ball on the house-ball rack by lane five, where it looked like any of them, and from there he heard Ellen lean across the counter to Judy.

"Before the first ball, Judy. Tomorrow I will bring a paydown to the closing, from my own savings."

Judy took that in with both hands on her pocketbook. "The winter's cash stays where it is, every dollar it made still sitting in the account. Anything you put on the note is new money, and I'll want to see where it came from."

"You will."

"Bring it. I'll circle it."

Where it came from. Ray heard it with the envelope zipped inside his jacket, where it had been four weeks.

He left Linda's ball on the rack and went round to the customer side of the counter and stayed there.

"Kettle Lanes, Gagnon. Request."

Ellen looked at him over the register for a second. "Gagnon, say request."

"I'd like the microphone a minute, if it's all the same."

She bent the gooseneck across the counter to him.

Ray leaned to it. "From next year Linda's name goes on the Open, and anybody that pays the entry can win it, because Linda would've entered on day one."

Hollis had gone to the settee by lane five. "Committee never voted on a name."

The derby treasurer stood up in his Sportsmen's Club jacket. "So moved."

Dale put his burger down. "Second."

Verna wrote it into the binder at lane one, league business or not. "The secretary votes yes. She voted yes on the owner's rate in January, and it has been folded in this binder since."

Ray was looking at Ellen when Verna said it. Her face went still, and she looked across the room at Verna a long time and said nothing at all.

Hollis had written him back in at anchor on the Gagnon and Son sheet with his golf pencil, and nobody said a word about it.

Ray's first box with Linda's ball was on lane five, where she'd thrown her hundred and eighty-seven one February while he watched from the pit. His thumb went looking for the place hers had worn into it, a dull patch the size of a dime, and found it, and his was too big for it. He bowled three ordinary strings with it and was glad of every one, and each time he looked up, with all ten lanes going at once, Ellen was behind the counter with her eyes shut.

It came down to the five-and-six pair, Lakeview Diner against Gagnon and Son, with the other eight lanes finished and standing behind the settees, and Denise in the tenth box needing everything on the deck. Her first ball took the front out of the rack. Her second took two more and left a four-horse standing at the back, and somebody on the Legion settee said "Oh, no," out loud, and his wife elbowed him.

Denise wiped her palm down her slacks and picked up her own ball, the one with the chip in it, and stood at the line a long time. Her third ball went in light and took all four at once.

At the rail Gil Arsenault, come over from Halsey Falls in his good overcoat for Linda's day, said it across to Denise. "That's twice."

The room came up onto its feet, the Highway crew in their hoods and the Legion and the old men from the Bench and Judy with her pocketbook, and the noise went up into the roof steel and stayed there. At the counter Ellen had her hand on the gooseneck and kept it there, and let the room have it. Verna wrote the result into the binder. Denise went the length of the room to the case she'd dusted every Sunday for six years, and opened it, and took the cup out herself with both hands.

Ray picked Linda's ball up off the return on lane five and started back with it toward the open locker, down the length of the settees, through a room that was still on its feet, and Dale Hutchins moved his boots to let him by.

\* \* \*

Ray squared the day's bills into stacks on the counter and tapped each stack on its edge until it sat. Ellen had the legal pad. Denise had gone home with the cup on the passenger seat with the belt across it, and over by the case, locker fourteen stood open with its padlock hung on the hasp, and Linda's ball was in his bag by the vestibule door.

Ellen drew a line under the last column with her pen. "Twenty-eight thousand nine hundred."

Ray's hands stopped on a stack. Down cellar the boiler lit, and he felt it come up through the floor.

She capped her pen. "Tomorrow I sign eighty-eight hundred of mine onto Arlene's note, and when the committee says yes, the bank's number comes down to meet it."

"Okay."

His hands stayed on the stacks. The envelope stayed inside his jacket, and he could feel the corner of it against his ribs every time he breathed.

"Judy's desk," she said. "Monday morning."

Ray squared the stack under his hand again, though it was square. "Judy wants to see where it came from. If I'm sitting in her office when it goes on that note, half of Main Street'll tell her, and they'll have it wrong, and by Wednesday you'll be wondering yourself."

She set the pad down. "You read it back to me behind lane three. Monday was in it."

"I'll be on it. I won't be in the chair."

She looked at him a while across the money. "Then be at your counter at ten. Under the clock."

The last time she'd said that to him she'd come down his aisle the next afternoon and laid a key on his counter and stood there with him while the minute hand went round. He didn't know what she meant by it this time.

She stood the pad up against the register. "I'm going to look at lane seven."

"Seven's fine."

She went through the steel door by lane ten anyway. He gave it a minute and went after her.

She was where she'd sat at New Year's, on the iron step behind lane seven, with nothing in her hands, and she didn't look round when the door went.

The machines were off. Ray sat down beside her on the cold iron and listened, because it was what he had: the motors down the catwalk ticking as they gave up their heat, one and then the next one over and then one away down by lane two, and the warmth going out of the kickback steel behind them a little at a time, and her breathing next to him, slower than his, and under all of it lane seven's machine, sitting quiet on the clutch he'd put in her the first week Ellen owned the place, which hadn't missed a set since. He had no idea what she'd come back here to say.

Ellen folded her bare hands together and looked down the catwalk. "You asked me whether New Year's too. It was. I knew, and I came down here so you'd find me."

Ray looked at her hands. "I'd about worked that out, in the truck."

He got up off the iron and bent his head under the beam, because he wanted to be standing for the rest of it, and took both her hands in his. They were bare and cold, as they always were, and he looked at them while he said it.

"Thirty years I've kept everything just how it was," he said, to her hands, "and I'd let go of the lot to be the one you ask." He looked at her then. "I love you, Ellen."

Ellen's hands turned over in his and took hold. "The night of the storm I told you two things and kept the third, because it wasn't in order. It is. I love you."

She came up off the step to reach him, and he came down to her under the beam, and she put one hand to his collar and the other to the side of his face, her fingers cold along his jaw, and kissed him. Something caught. Her reading glasses had swung out on their black cord and hooked the pull of his jacket zipper, and when she came up the last inch they jerked her short, and Ray laughed against her mouth. It came up out of him all at once, the same laugh she'd pulled out of him in the pit behind lane five in December, the first one in six years, and he felt her start to smile, and she kept hold of his face and kissed him through it. He covered her hand on his face with his own, and his other arm came round her and held on. He kissed her back slowly. Down the catwalk the last motor ticked and went still, and the building was quiet all round them, and he took his time, and so did she.

When they came apart she unhooked her glasses from his zipper with two fingers and let them fall back on their cord.

"Twice behind lane seven and once under the sign," she said. "I've kept count."

Her hand went to her vest pocket, where his key was, and lay flat on it for a second, and came away with nothing in it. Ray saw it. He didn't ask.

\* \* \*

The store phone rang under the clock, and Ray had his hand on it before the second ring. Down the counter the Bench stopped with their cups halfway up.

"Gagnon and Son."

"You're in the other chair."

Her voice came from a little way off, and behind it heels went across marble and the drive-up tube landed in its slot with a thunk. She'd laid her phone face up on the chair beside her at Judy's desk, and he could hear the whole room it was lying in.

A desk drawer opened and shut. "Is that Ray Gagnon on my chair?"

"On the phone. None of it is his."

"Then he can sit there."

Paper slid across a desk. Nobody on the Bench drank. A pen went round once on paper, slow, and came off it.

"She has circled it," Ellen said.

"Sign there," Judy said, "and it goes on the note when the committee says yes."

Ray heard her pen go, a long stroke and a short one, and stop.

Danny came up the aisle with the deposit bag for the credit union under his arm. Ray took the envelope out of his jacket one-handed, the blank one that had ridden in there since the first of February, and laid it on the counter under the clock.

Danny had been in line behind him the day it was bought, and he read it without opening it. "Neither invoice, then."

Ray put his hand over the mouthpiece. "I'll walk it back down to them at noon."

From the end stool Normie said, in the voice of the Saturday show, "And the veteran turns out his pockets."

Down the line cellophane crackled. Judy only offered from that bowl before bad news, and half of Main Street had a butterscotch to show for it.

Ray took his hand off the phone. "She's got the butterscotch out."

"I can hear you, Ray."

A wrapper went into a wastebasket, and Judy's chair creaked round.

"Ellen. I like you. The underwriter has never met you. Committee sits at two."

\* \* \*

Ray had the new K up against the sign in his bare hands, cold glass the length of his forearm bent in Laconia into the curl of the old script, and he seated the second electrode in its housing and felt it go home. The old one had been dark since the winter Danny got his license, and Arlene had always said the sign still said what it meant. He'd opened the disconnect on the pole before he climbed, and done what the shop's sheet in his shirt pocket said, in order.

The committee had sat. Nobody had called the store since, or come down Kettle Road.

Past the town beach the lake lay white with water standing on it in long gray sheets, and every bob-house in Ambrose was still out on its skids. He had fixed every machine up that drive twice over, and he couldn't fix a committee on Main Street, so he'd come down here and put up a K.

Her car came up Kettle Road and pulled in at the foot of the drive, and she came to the ladder and took the foot of it in both hands, and it went steady under his boots. He felt her come into the rails. From up there all he could see of her was the top of her head, no hat on it, short and silver.

"Well?"

"You're standing at the bottom of my drive on the first of March."

"Said I'd sooner it was me."

Her hands tightened on the rails. "It funded."

His hands stayed where they were on the glass.

A car stopped on Kettle Road beside the pole with its back seat full of boxes for The Pines, and the window came down. Arlene had one sheet in her hand, her lawyer's wire confirmation, and she licked her thumb and counted it.

"One."

She looked from Ray on the ladder to Ellen at the foot of it. "Out of the cars, I see. You beat the best winter I had on my own, dear, and I thought I'd done something."

Then up at him. "I'll have my key back, Raymond."

Ray came down the ladder and worked the old key off his ring, its orange tag gone soft as cloth, and handed it in at her window.

"Voyons donc," Arlene said, to the tag, and put it in her pocketbook, and went on to The Pines.

Denise came down the drive with the Bench's two sheets from Gagnon's, the old gray pool sheet with its March-first square still blank and the newer one, and the money in an envelope.

"She didn't quit and she didn't lose it, so nobody wins, and the pot goes to the derby kids' division, and that's the last pot Ettle's pays out, with the K back."

Ellen reached for the other sheet. "I'd like a square."

Denise took it back before her finger landed. "Honey, you don't get to bet on your own flight."

Gil's spring-doubles flyer showed folded in her coat pocket, and she saw Ray see it. "Gil's short a partner for the spring doubles. I'm thinking about it." She went back up the drive.

His key ring had a gap in it now. Ellen took his key out of her vest pocket and laid it on her open palm between them, as she had at her counter in February.

Ray looked at it. "That's not asking."

She closed her fingers on it, and opened them again. "Ray." She let a second go by. "Will you take it?"

He took it off her palm, and held the ring in his left hand, and opened the split end with his thumbnail and fed the key on a quarter turn at a time, slow, into the place Arlene's had hung for thirty years, until it came round the last of the coil and sat among the others. She watched every turn of it. The door it opened was hers now, every hinge of it.

The light was going off the lake. Ray stood at the foot of the pole under the sign's cabinet, and Ellen stood at the disconnect with her hand on the handle.

"Throw her, Mrs. Daly."

He waited for the Ms. She threw it.

Over his head the transformer took hold with a buzz he felt in the pole, and red came down over the plow bank and out across the wet road. From under the cabinet he could see nothing of the letters.

"How's she read?"

Ellen walked out into the middle of Kettle Road and looked up.

"Both words."
