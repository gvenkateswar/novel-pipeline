# Chapter 4: Say Intentions

Her kitchen light was on at twenty to seven.

Ray could see it from the road, a mile out Kettle Road past the alley, where she rented a camp: somebody's summer cottage, winterized for people who stayed, set right down on the water. Her car was in the dooryard with frost on the glass. He drove on to the end of the plowing, turned around against the bank, and went back.

At the alley he let himself in at the front with her key. It was the first time he had put it in the lock, and the cut he'd made on his own wheel went over smooth.

He threw all ten toggles under her tape at the counter, and went down the line rolling one house ball down every lane, so each machine would take its pins and cycle and set. On a party day you ran them all before anybody paid for anything. He stood at the head of each lane while it ran. Two and three set clean. Six took her time at the top, a half-beat slow, the way she had since the winter the roof let go over the five-and-six pair, and he wrote a six on the back of his hand with his pencil. By the time he was back at lane one, all ten were set and running, ten lanes lit end to end with nobody on them.

Headlights came up the drive.

The vestibule door opened, and she stopped in it with her hand on the bar and the cold coming in past her. She didn't say good morning. For a while she only stood in the doorway and listened to ten machines, and from lane one Ray watched her listen. When six came round late, her head turned a degree toward it.

She let the door close behind her.

"You started them."

"Party Saturday," he said. "Wanted to hear all ten before your parties did."

On the counter she laid down her clipboard, turned so he could read it. The whole day was there in her printing: three parties, every lane from ten in the morning until close, the hours ruled off with a straightedge. At the bottom was a line that said Maintenance, ten to close. His name was already on it, in the same pen as the rest, and she didn't mention it.

"I need a pinsetter man in the pit from ten until close," she said. "At the Worcester man's rate. Today, and every call after this one."

"Worcester's two hours off in this weather, and the fella'll want his mileage."

"Then I'll call Worcester."

Ray looked at his name on her line. It had been written before he got there, in ink, by a woman who did not cross things out.

"I'll bring my pad," he said.

She turned the clipboard back around to face her.

"Long as I'm on your clock," Ray said, "I could get that K lit for you. Be a nice thing to have lit for your parties."

"No. I hire out what I am not qualified for, and so should you. You are a pinsetter man. That is a neon tube."

"There's a shop in Laconia bends them, and the man there—"

"That is a sign shop's job, and it is not on my list this winter."

He let it go. "Fifteen minutes on lane one first, then. And those I don't bill."

"Fifteen," she said, after a look at her watch.

Before he could pick a ball out for her she took one off the return. It sat in her bare palm like something she'd been handed to hold and hadn't made up her mind about.

"Turn your wrist under," he said. "Let it go about six inches lower than you think you ought to. Then let the lane have it."

Her practice swing came through with the wrist flat, the way it always did, as if she were passing somebody a plate.

"Here."

He stepped in behind her right shoulder and put his hand over her wrist, over the steel band of her watch, and turned it under. The band was still cold from her car, and the clasp sat right on the bone. Under his thumb her pulse ran quick and even, like a motor come up to speed that meant to stay there. He held it a beat longer than the lesson needed, and then another.

"It is nine minutes past seven," she said.

She didn't move. He let go first.

The ball went low and on line, the best he had seen from her, into the pocket, and it took five. Two of those lay crossways on the deck with the rest standing up behind them.

"Reset it."

"She won't. Not in the middle of a box." He nodded down the lane. "The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."

"Who made that rule?"

"Nobody you can take it up with."

She took the second ball off the return. She turned her own wrist under, with his hand nowhere near it, and let it go low, and it went away from her down lane one toward the wood.

"And here he comes now, in off the Kettle Road end," Normie said, before the bell over the door had quit, "the veteran himself, a couple of hours late to his own store, and the boy's had her open since six, and he doesn't look one bit sorry about it, folks."

Ray went past the three stools without slowing down.

"No comment from the veteran at this time," Normie said.

Tacked up by the register was a new sheet in Normie's hand, ruled with a yardstick, dates down the left side and a square beside each, and a dollar a square printed across the top. Nobody said what it was for.

"Sheet's open," Normie said. "Valentine's is gone."

Ray found Valentine's Day. There were two initials in the square in blue ballpoint, and they were Danny's. He looked down the counter. Danny was at the key cutter with a blank in the vise and the wheel whining under his hands.

"That's your writing," Ray said.

Danny kept cutting. "It was a dollar."

Ray went on through to the back for his invoice pad. It was on the shelf over the desk with its carbons, Gagnon & Son across the top of every sheet, and he had it in his hand when Danny came in behind him with a letter.

"You've been in my mail."

"It came to the store. I open what comes to the store." Danny unfolded it. "Bank in Concord will lend you what the appraiser says the alley's worth, to buy it March first. That holds through New Year's Eve, and then it's gone. Keeping it to March first costs you a fee, and you pay it whether you buy or not."

He read his father the fee off the bottom of the page, in the voice he used for a back order.

Ray opened the desk drawer and put his hand on the store's long checkbook, the one with the stubs. He stopped there. Then he shut the drawer, took his own checkbook out of the inside pocket of his jacket, and wrote the check against the wall, standing up.

Danny watched him tear it out.

"If she makes it, Dad," Danny said, "what've you bought?"

Beyond the door the Bench had gone quiet, and somebody was pouring coffee with great care. Ray folded the check into the bank's return envelope and sealed it. He picked the invoice pad up off the desk, which was what he had come for, and put it under his arm.

Out front he dropped the envelope in the tray of outgoing mail at the end of the counter, address up, the way every piece of mail had gone out of that store since before he could see over it. The bell went over his head. Through the glass of the door, as it swung to behind him, Normie was leaning over the tray and reading the address with his lips moving.

\* \* \*

"Pit, Counter," said Ellen's voice at his hip. "Nine is short a pin."

She had bought two radios for the day and named them, Counter and Pit, and clipped Pit to his belt herself before the first party came through the door. Ray was on an upturned pin crate by the workbench under the caged bulb. He unclipped the radio and keyed it.

"Roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out."

"One of those will do," she said. "Ten-four is the state police. Over and out asks me to answer you and tells me not to."

"Wilco, then," Ray said.

"Wilco means you will comply. I have not asked you for anything yet."

Nine had been sulking since the second party came in. From the catwalk he could hear her elevator coming round with a hitch in it, a catch and a drag at the same place every turn, and when he put his light in, there was a pin lying crossways in the cups, riding up and riding back down and never getting delivered. He worked it loose with two fingers. Nine set clean.

Through the gap under the curtains the lanes ran bright and backward, lit maple end to end, and rented shoes went by at the far end, red and green and too big on the children.

All afternoon her voice had come off his belt like that, low and level over ten machines. He'd have known it in a full house with his back to the door. Twice already he'd caught himself on the catwalk with the radio in his hand and nothing to say into it, and clipped it back on his belt.

The radio came on again. "Pit, Counter. Two, ball return slow. Birthday party on the pair. Cake on the hour. Read it back."

Ray keyed it and took his time.

"Counter, this is Pit, and what I've got is the ball return on two is dragging its feet like a man on his way to the dentist, and there's a birthday party on that pair that won't wait on anybody, and they'll be wanting their cake on the hour, and the counter would like the pinsetter man up front where she can keep an eye on him. Over. Or whichever one of those I'm allowed."

"Negative on the last item."

"Which item was that, Counter?"

The channel stayed open a second. A register drawer banged shut behind her, and a child wanted ketchup, and nobody keyed anything.

"Two," she said.

He went to two, and he whistled most of the way down the catwalk, which nobody heard over ten machines.

The next time her mic opened, what came through first was Denise at the register, who had a twelve-year-old in front of her and a good deal to tell him. "They're sprayed toe and heel, every pair, every single time, and your father has bowled in those shoes with the worst average in the Tuesday division for as long as I've known him and never once complained, and if you'd like shoes that smell like you, you can bowl in the ones you came in."

The click cut her off.

The parties turned over at the far end of the lanes. No lane went down for longer than it took him to walk to it.

At five past five she came on again. "Pit, say intentions."

His watch said ten minutes to supper. He had sat down to it at the same minute every night since before Danny could reach the table.

He keyed the radio. "Intentions are to quit."

The channel stayed open, with the counter's noise behind it, a drawer and a fryer basket and somebody's whole family singing.

"Unable."

\* \* \*

Ray stood in the office doorway with his first invoice, torn off the Gagnon & Son pad. Ellen was at the adding machine with her back to him, and the day's tape had run off the edge of the desk and curled on the floor at her boots.

"Shut the door," she said to the adding machine. "The heater cannot keep up with the hall."

He shut it. That left one stride of floor between her desk and Arlene's shelves, and he was standing in it, near enough to read her tape over her shoulder. Denise had gone home. The space heater ticked. He kept his jacket zipped.

He laid the invoice on the desk by her elbow. She read it once, top to bottom, wrote a check on the alley's book, tore it out, and laid it on his side of the desk.

"You did not bill lane one," she said.

"Lane one was before ten."

He folded the check into his shirt pocket, behind the Worcester page.

She tore the tape off the machine. "That is the best Saturday in Arlene's ledgers."

"All of them?"

"I have read all of them."

One of the green ledgers stood a finger's width proud of the others on the second shelf. Ray pushed it back in line, and then the stapler, which was sitting crooked to the blotter.

"Normie'll have this at the post office by Monday," he said, "so you'd better have it from me tonight. I paid the bank in Concord this morning to keep my loan good to March first."

She turned around. "Tell me why."

"Because if I let it lapse and you came up short, there'd be nobody to buy this place March first, and Arlene would sit waiting on lawyers for her money and lose her place at The Pines, and the condo fella from Concord would get the lot for what the dirt's worth."

"That is Arlene's reason."

"If somebody's going to be standing at the bottom of your drive on March first, I'd sooner it was me."

"Then you will want to know what your league costs me," she said. "Seven hundred dollars every week it bowls."

"Always has. Arlene carried it."

"Arlene had you for nothing."

She took a sheet off the blotter and laid it on his side of the desk, where the check had been. It was one page in her printing: the new lineage, from Tuesday on.

"It goes on the vestibule board tomorrow. I wanted you to hear it from me first."

He knew the shape of it by the second line.

"Arlene put this same notice up once."

"Three things," Ellen said. "First. December of two thousand four. Posted on the vestibule board and charged from the next league night, in Arlene's hand. That is where I took the form. Second. Paragraph four says lineage stands as set in September, 'as the league's bylaws provide.' Third. I asked Verna for the bylaws the day after I closed."

"And Verna said."

"The secretary reads the bylaws aloud at the January meeting, in full. The binder does not leave the secretary."

"There's a line in them says the September rate holds till the January meeting votes," Ray said. "It went in the January after Arlene's notice."

"Who put it there?"

His hand was still flat on the ledger he'd squared. "I did."

She stayed on her feet. She drew the notice back across the desk and laid it face down on the day's tape, and put her palm flat on the back of it, and kept it there.

"At the new rate I make my number," she said. "At the old one I do not. So I will put it to the January meeting."

"And I'll stand up against it." He said it to her face. "Highway crew's on twelve-hour shifts from now to mud season, and the Legion's living on what the government sends them. They can't pay it, and I won't ask them to."

"Will they vote the way you stand?"

"Mostly."

She took that the way she had taken the invoice, once, top to bottom.

He took his hand off the ledger. Nothing on the shelves was out of line now, and he let his hands be. Her reading glasses hung on their cord at her collar, and there was a smear of pencil along the edge of her right hand from a day of ruling things off.

"I eat supper at a quarter past five, have for thirty years, and you've made me late twice this week, and I can't tell if you're doing it on purpose or you're just no good with a clock."

"I'm very good with a clock."

She said it level and exact, the way a good motor sounds under load, every part doing its share and not one of them complaining, and her palm stayed on the notice, and he didn't take a step.

"I'll be on my feet in January, Ellen."

"Good. I'd hate to beat somebody who wasn't ready."

He reached behind him for the doorknob.

She sat down. Then she hooked the second chair out from under the desk with the toe of her boot and left it there, facing her.
