# Chapter 4: Say Intentions

Ray's truck went past the foot of the alley's drive in the dark and kept on out Kettle Road, a mile farther, to where the plowing gave out. She rented the last place before it, a summer cottage somebody had winterized, low and shingled and set right down on the water. Her kitchen light was on at twenty to seven, and her car sat in the dooryard with the night's frost still on the glass. He turned around in the circle the plow had left and drove back.

He let himself in with the key she'd given him, and the cut he'd made on his own wheel went over smooth the first time.

At the counter he flipped the ten toggles along her strips of tape, and the lanes came up out of the gray in pairs. At each lane he threw one ball off the return, pressed the reset, and stood to listen while the machine at the far end swept and set. Running every one before a party day was how you checked them, and he could hear which was slow. Five and six came round together like two old sisters on the church steps. Eight was a half beat late setting, and had been since the winter the cellar pump quit. By the time he was back at lane one, all ten were set and running, ten tables humming under their bulbs and nobody on any of them.

Her car came up the drive. The vestibule door opened, and she stopped in it.

She stood with the cold coming in past her and her keys in her bare hand. Her head went a little to one side. He knew that tilt, because he used it himself in parking lots when a building had something to tell him. From lane one he watched her take the room a machine at a time: the sweeps, the hum under the floor, five and six together, eight coming down late. She heard eight; he saw the small check in her face, and then she went on to nine and ten. It took her about as long as it would have taken him. Then she let the door close behind her.

"You started them," she said.

"Party Saturday." He set his last ball back on the return. "Wanted to hear all ten before your parties did."

She laid her clipboard on the counter facing him. The day was there in her printing: three parties, every lane from ten until close, every hour ruled off. At the bottom was a line for maintenance, ten to close, and his name was already on it. She didn't mention that.

"I need a pinsetter man in the pit from ten until close," she said. "At the Worcester man's rate. Today, and every call after."

"Worcester's two hours off in this, and the fella's going to want his mileage both ways."

"Then I'll call Worcester."

Ray looked at his name on her line.

"Ten to close." He tapped it. "And as long as I'm on your clock, I could get that K of yours lit."

"No. I hire out what I am not qualified for, and so should you. You are a pinsetter man. That is a neon tube."

"There's a shop up in Laconia bends them, and—"

"A sign shop does that. My list for this winter is on the clipboard."

He let it go. "Then one thing. Fifteen minutes on lane one first, and those I don't bill."

She looked at her watch. "Fifteen."

She took a ball off the return herself and stood on the approach with it in her right hand, her weight even on both feet. He'd seen her let go of a ball as if she were handing it across a desk to somebody.

"Turn your wrist under when you let go," he said, "and get down lower than you think, and then six inches more, and let the lane have it."

"Show me."

He stepped in behind her right shoulder and put his hand over her wrist, over the steel band of her watch. The band was cold from the car, colder than the skin it sat on, and under the links he could feel her pulse going along, even. He turned her wrist under, slow, and it went where he put it and stopped where he stopped, like a new shaft in a good bearing, with no play anywhere. That was the whole lesson, and he'd given it, and his hand stayed where it was.

"It is nine minutes past seven," she said.

Her watch was under his palm, where she couldn't have read it, and she didn't move.

He let go first.

She went lower than he had ever seen her go and turned her wrist under on the way through. The ball came off her hand clean and went in a hair right of the head pin. It was the best ball he had seen from her. Three pins lay crossways on the deck and four stood behind them.

"Reset it," she said.

"Not in the middle of a box. The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."

"Who made that rule?"

"Nobody you can take it up with."

She took her second ball off the return. She went down low again and turned her own wrist under, with his hand nowhere near it, and let it go, and the ball went off down lane one toward the wood.

\* \* \*

The bell over the door went, and under it came the key cutter's whine, high and steady, from the end of the counter.

"And here's the veteran, folks, in off the Kettle Road end," Normie said from the last stool by the pot, "a couple hours behind his own store, and the boy had her open at six."

Ray kept moving. He wanted the invoice pad off the back-room shelf and himself out the door again before any of the three of them asked him a thing, and he got as far as the register.

Tacked to the wall beside it was a sheet he hadn't seen before, in Normie's careful hand, ruled in pencil against something straight. Dates ran down the left side and squares ran across, and at the top it said a dollar a square. Nobody had written what it was for.

"Sheet's open," Normie said. "Valentine's is gone."

Ray found Valentine's Day. The initials in its square were Danny's.

He looked down the counter. Danny had a key out of the vise and was running his thumb down the cut for burrs, and the tips of his fingers were nicked the way a key cutter nicks anybody who runs it fast and won't wear the glove. He clamped the next blank and went on cutting.

"It was a dollar."

The pad was on the back-room shelf where it had always been, the store's name across the top of every sheet in his father's lettering, and Ray had it in his hand when Danny came in behind him with a letter.

"Came yesterday with the supplier bills," Danny said. "I open everything that comes to the store." He read it the way he read a freight bill. "Bank in Concord will lend you what the appraiser says the alley's worth, to buy it March first. That holds through New Year's Eve, and then it's gone. Keeping it to March first costs a fee, and you pay it whether you buy or not."

He read off the fee at the bottom, flat, and folded the letter on its creases.

Ray opened the desk drawer. The store's long checkbook lay in it, the one with the stubs. He had his hand on it. Then he shut the drawer, took his own checkbook from the inside pocket of his jacket, and wrote the check standing up, on the corner of the desk. Danny watched him write it.

"If she makes it, Dad," Danny said, "what've you bought?"

Ray tore the check out along the perforation and sealed it in the envelope the bank had sent. He didn't answer. He picked up the invoice pad, which was what he'd come in for.

Out front he laid the envelope in the outgoing tray on the counter, address up, the way all the store's mail went out, and went through the door with the pad under his arm. Through the glass, as the bell quit over his head, he saw Normie get down off his stool, lean over the tray, and read the address, moving his lips.

\* \* \*

By mid-afternoon the pit was loud and close, ten machines going and the smell of belts and grease filling the low room. Through the gap under the curtain the lanes showed bright and backward, rented shoes going by at the far ends, a girl's pink laces, a father in his socks. Ray sat on an upturned pin crate by the workbench with a radio clipped to his belt. He meant to be in his truck at five, as he was every day.

"Pit, Counter," Ellen said off his hip. "Lane six is short a pin."

She had bought two radios for the day and named them, Counter for hers and Pit for his.

He keyed it. "Counter, this is Pit, roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out."

The channel stayed open a second with the counter's noise behind it.

"One of those will do," she said. "Ten-four is the state police. Over and out asks me to answer you and tells me not to."

He heard six before he got there, the pin elevator coming round with a hitch in it, a catch and a lurch on every turn, which meant a pin gone in crossways. He found it jammed across the elevator's lip and worked it loose by the neck with two fingers while she was still turning, then fed it back in straight. Six took it up and set ten clean.

"Six is set," he said.

"Thank you."

All afternoon she was at his hip. Parties shrieked out front, the fryer hissed through her open mic, and ten machines ran at his back. Her voice came up through all of it low and level, never a hair louder than the job needed, every word set down where she meant it. He could pick it out from the far end of the catwalk with two sweeps coming down at once. He could tell when she was smiling at a customer and when she was only being civil to one. He listened for the click that came ahead of her, and twice he had his thumb on his own key before she'd finished the call.

"Pit, Counter. Lane nine, ball return slow. Birthday party on nine and ten. Cake after their second string. Read it back."

He keyed it and took his time.

"Counter, Pit. Nine's return is taking the scenic route bringing the balls home, and there's a birthday party on nine and ten that's going to want its cake when the second string's done and won't care to stand around waiting on a ball first, so I'm to have nine moving before then, and Counter would like me up front after, where she can keep an eye on me."

"Negative on the last item."

"Which item was that, Counter?"

The channel stayed open. Behind her the register drawer ran out, somebody down the line was starting in on happy birthday, and a man was asking Denise for more ketchup.

"Lane nine," she said, and the click.

He went to nine.

The next time the radio clicked, the first voice off his belt was Denise's, at the register beside Ellen's mic and going full tilt at a birthday boy who had told the whole counter he was twelve.

"Those shoes get sprayed toe and heel every time they come back across this counter, and your father's bowled a low average in shoes off that same shelf since before you were born and never once complained, and if you'd like a pair that smells like you, you can bowl in the ones you came in."

The click cut her off.

At five past five it came again, and this time there was only Ellen.

"Pit, say intentions."

His watch said ten minutes to supper. He keyed it.

"Intentions are to quit."

The channel stayed open, with the counter behind it, the register and the fryer and a lane of kids, and close under all of it the small hiss of her mic held down.

"Unable."

\* \* \*

The adding machine was going when he got to the office, a chatter and a ratchet and a chatter, and its tape had run off the edge of the desk and lay curled on the floor by her boot. The lanes were dark behind him; Denise had gone home at close. Ray stood in the office doorway with his first invoice, torn off the store's pad, and his jacket zipped to the collar.

Ellen stood at the desk with her back to him, running the day.

"Shut the door," she said. "The heater cannot keep up with the hall."

He shut it. With the door closed there was a foot and a half of floor between the ledger shelf and the corner of her desk, and he was standing on it.

He laid the invoice by her elbow. She read it once, top to bottom, took the alley's checkbook from the drawer, wrote a check, and laid it on his side of the desk. He put it in his shirt pocket, behind the Worcester page.

She tore the tape off the machine and ran her eye down it to the total.

"That is the best Saturday in every ledger on that shelf," she said.

"You've read them all."

"Every one."

One of Arlene's green ledgers stood a half inch proud of the rest, its cloth worn shiny along the spine where a thumb had been pulling it out. Ray pushed it back in line. It didn't need it, and he pushed it again.

"I paid the bank in Concord this morning," he said, "to keep my loan good to March first. Thought you'd want it from me before Normie has it all over the post office."

She set the tape down. "Tell me why."

"If I'd let it go and you came up short, there'd be nobody to buy it March first, and Arlene would sit up over the pharmacy waiting on lawyers for her money while her place at The Pines went to somebody else, and the condo fella from Concord would get the lot."

"That is Arlene's reason."

"If somebody's going to be standing at the bottom of your drive on March first, I'd sooner it was me."

"Then as my buyer you will want to know what your league costs me. Seven hundred dollars every week it bowls."

"Always has. Arlene carried it."

"Arlene had you for nothing."

She laid one sheet on his side of the desk. It was in her printing, the new lineage starting Tuesday.

"It goes on the vestibule board tomorrow. I wanted you to hear it from me first."

He read it through. "Arlene put this same notice up once."

"December of two thousand four," she said. "First, she posted it on that board and charged it from the next league night, in her own hand. That is where I took the form. Second, paragraph four says lineage stands as set in September, 'as the league's bylaws provide.' Third, I asked Verna for the bylaws the day after I closed."

"And Verna said."

"The secretary reads the bylaws aloud at the January meeting, in full, and the binder does not leave the secretary."

That was Verna to the comma.

"There's a line in them says the September rate holds till the January meeting votes on it," he said. "It went in the January after Arlene's notice."

"Who put it there?"

His hand had gone back to the green ledger he'd squared. "I did."

She didn't answer it. He watched her hands, because her face would give him nothing he could use. They came up to the shelf and stopped at the ledger under his palm, and waited there until he took his palm away. She drew it out. It was the one she'd had down already; it fell open flat on the desk at December, the spine broken to that page. She ran one finger down Arlene's column to the night the rate changed, and held it there, and with her other hand she turned her own notice face down on the tape.

"At the new rate I make my number," she said. "At the old one I do not."

"Then you'll want to tell the league that."

"I will put it to the January meeting."

"And I'll stand up against it. The Highway crew's on twelve-hour shifts from now till the frost goes out, and the Legion's living on what the government sends them, and they can't pay it."

"Will they vote the way you stand?"

"Mostly."

He took his hand off the shelf and left the ledgers alone. The heater ticked. Under the floor the boiler came on, and the building was quiet enough to hear it.

"I eat supper at a quarter past five, have for thirty years, and you've made me late twice this week, and I can't tell if you're doing it on purpose or you're just no good with a clock."

"I'm very good with a clock."

She said it level and held it there, the way a good motor holds its speed when the load comes on, and the corner of her mouth didn't hold at all.

"I'll be on my feet in January, Ellen."

"Good. I'd hate to beat somebody who wasn't ready."

She sat down. Then she hooked the second chair out from under the desk with the toe of her boot and left it there, facing her.
