# Chapter 4: Say Intentions

Ray drove past the alley in the dark and kept going out Kettle Road. A mile on, the plowing stopped at a summer cottage somebody had winterized, on the water, and that was where she rented. Her kitchen light was on at twenty to seven. Her car sat in the dooryard with two arcs scraped clear on the windshield and none anywhere else.

He turned around at the end of the plowing, backed once into the bank, and drove back the way he had come.

He let himself in with her front key, the first time he had used it, and the cut he'd made on his own wheel went over smooth. At the counter he threw the ten toggles under her tape, and the lanes came up a pair at a time down the length of the building.

He took a ball off the rack and walked the line, throwing one down every lane so each machine swept and set ten fresh. It was what you did before a party day: run every machine once, empty, and listen. Three came round half a second late at the top of her table, and he put that away for later. By the time he was back at lane one, all ten were lit and set and humming, and nobody on any of them.

Headlights swept the glass block. The vestibule door opened, and she stopped in it with the clipboard against her chest and the cold coming in past her, and listened.

Ray watched her do it from lane one. Her eyes went down the building to ten and came back along it, lane by lane, and stopped on him. The door glass ticked in its frame behind her, and the cold got as far as the counter. His hands had come out of his jacket pockets without his say-so. She let the door close.

She came down onto the approach and stopped an arm's length off his right side.

"Why are all ten running?"

"Party Saturday," Ray said. "Wanted to hear all ten before your parties did."

She turned the clipboard round and held it out.

The day was in her printing. Three parties, every lane from ten until close, every hour ruled off. At the bottom was a line for maintenance, ten to close, and on it, in the same printing, R. GAGNON. She'd written it at her kitchen table, he'd guess, under that light, before she ever asked him.

"I need a pinsetter man in the pit from ten until close," she said. "Today, and every call after today, at the Worcester man's rate."

"Worcester's two hours off in this," Ray said, "and the fella'll want his mileage both ways."

"Then I will call Worcester."

She stood there with the clipboard held out and her arm straight, and it didn't waver. Ray read his name again. He took longer over it than two words take.

"Ten to close," he said, and gave her the clipboard back.

"And as long as I'm on your clock, I could get that K of yours lit."

"No. I hire out what I am not qualified for, and so should you. You are a pinsetter man. That is a neon tube."

"Shop in Laconia bends them. I'd only have to run it up there."

"Then it is a sign shop's job, and it is not on my list this winter."

"Then here's my condition," Ray said. "Fifteen minutes on lane one first, and those I don't bill."

She looked at her watch. "Fifteen."

She laid the clipboard on the scorer's table, square to the edge, and took a ball off the return herself.

"Turn the wrist under," he said. "Let it go about six inches lower than you think you ought to, and let the lane have it."

She swung once, slow, without letting go. The wrist stayed on top.

He stepped in behind her right shoulder, close enough that the front of his jacket brushed her vest, and put his hand over her wrist. Her watch was under his fingers, the steel band still cold from the drive and the skin above it warm. He turned the wrist under for her, slowly, and held it there.

Under the band her pulse was going quick, the way a motor runs fast before it's warmed through. The last wrist he had turned like this was Danny's, nine years old and sticky with orange soda. He held on a beat longer than the lesson needed, and then another.

"It is nine minutes past seven," she said.

She didn't move. She kept her eyes on the head pin, sixty feet down the old wood, and let the time she'd told him sit in the empty building with ten machines humming under it.

He let go first and stepped back.

She threw. It was the best ball he had seen from her, low and turning, with no wobble in it, and it put five down and laid two of them across the deck with the rest standing behind.

"Reset it," she said.

"She won't, not in the middle of a box. The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."

"Who made that rule?"

"Nobody you can take it up with."

She took the second ball off the return and turned her own wrist under, with his hand nowhere near it, and let it go low, and it went off down lane one toward the wood.

\* \* \*

The bell went over the door at half past eight, and Normie had him before it stopped.

"And here comes the veteran in from the Kettle Road end, boys," Normie said from the end stool, "a couple of hours late to his own store, and the boy opened up at six without him."

Danny was at the key cutter at the end of the counter with a blank in the vise, and the wheel whined and stopped and whined. He didn't look round.

Ray meant to go straight through to the back for the invoice pad. He got as far as the register. Tacked up beside it was a new sheet in Normie's hand, ruled in pencil against a yardstick, with dates down the left side and a dollar a square written across the top. Nothing at the top said what it was for. Half the squares already had initials in them.

"Sheet's open, boys," Normie said. "Valentine's is gone."

He found Valentine's. The initials in the square were D.G., in a hand Ray had taught to hold a pencil. He looked down the counter.

Danny kept cutting. "It was a dollar."

Ray thought of her printing on the bottom line of the clipboard, his name in it before she'd asked, and went on into the back.

The invoice pads were on the shelf over the desk, a stack of them with GAGNON & SON across the top, and he had one down and under his arm when Danny came in behind him with a letter.

"Came yesterday with the supplier bills," Danny said. "I open everything that comes to the store." He unfolded it. "Bank in Concord'll lend you what the appraiser says the alley's worth, to buy it March first. That's good through New Year's Eve, and then it's gone. They'll hold it to March first for a fee, and you pay the fee whether you buy or not."

He read the fee off the bottom of the page in the same voice he used for a carton count.

The store's long checkbook was in the desk drawer, the one with the stubs, and Ray had it half out before he stopped. He put it back and shut the drawer. He took his own checkbook out of the inside of his jacket and wrote the fee on the desk blotter, standing, and tore it out along the perforation slowly so it wouldn't rag.

Danny watched him do all of it.

"If she makes it, Dad, what've you bought?"

Ray put the check in the bank's envelope and sealed it and ran his thumb along the flap, twice. He didn't answer. He picked up the invoice pad, which was what he had come for.

Out front he laid the envelope in the wire tray of outgoing mail on the counter, address up, the way all the store's mail went out, and went to the door with the pad under his arm. As the door swung shut behind him he could see through the glass Normie get down off the end stool and lean over the tray and read the address, moving his lips.

\* \* \*

Ray was on an upturned pin crate by the workbench, with the lanes bright and backward through the gap under the curtain and rented shoes going past it, when the radio on his belt said, in her voice, "Pit, Counter. Lane two is short a pin."

She had bought two of them for the day and named them both, and he was Pit.

He unclipped it and held the button down. "Roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out."

"One of those will do," she said. "Ten-four is the state police. Over and out asks me to answer you and tells me not to."

He went down the catwalk to two with the radio in his fist. He heard the trouble before he got there: the elevator coming round with a hitch in it, a catch at the top and a knock on the way down, the same every turn. A pin was jammed crossways in her elevator, and he worked it loose with his fingers and stood it in the rack where it belonged. She set clean on the next cycle.

"She was only sulking," he told the radio, without keying it.

All afternoon her voice came off his hip, low and level over ten machines, a lane and a trouble and nothing extra, and he got up off the crate for every one of them. By the middle of the afternoon he could tell from the way she said his call sign whether it was a pin or a party, and a while after that he stopped clipping the radio back on his belt and kept it on his knee.

Late in the afternoon the radio said, "Pit, Counter. Lane nine, ball return slow. Birthday party on nine. Cake at the half hour. I want the return running before the candles. Read it back."

He keyed it. "Counter, I've got it that nine's ball return is taking her sweet time bringing them home, and there's a birthday on nine that'll want its ball back before anybody lights anything, and cake's at the half hour, and I'm to have her running before the candles, and the counter would like the pit man up front after, where she can keep an eye on him."

"Negative on the last item," she said.

"Which item was that, Counter?"

The channel stayed open. He could hear the counter behind her, the register drawer going out and in, a kid asking where the cake was, the crash of a full rack on one of the near lanes, and no voice on it for a full second, which on that radio was a long time.

"Nine," she said, and let go of the button.

He went down the catwalk to nine grinning at the pinsetters like a fool.

The next time the radio opened, the first voice on it was Denise's at the register, close to the counter mic and going full tilt at a twelve-year-old who wanted to keep his own sneakers on: "Those shoes get sprayed toe and heel, every pair, every time, and your father's bowled an average I'm too kind to say out loud in those very shoes since before you had teeth and never complained once, so if you'd like shoes that smell like you, you can bowl in the ones you came in—"

The click cut her off. It opened again a few seconds later, and it was Ellen, level, with a lane and a trouble and nothing else.

At five past five the radio said, "Pit, say intentions."

Ray's watch said ten minutes to supper. If he left now, he would make it. He keyed it. "Intentions are to quit."

The channel stayed open, with the counter behind it, the drawer and somebody laughing and a full rack going down on one of the near lanes, and then she said, "Unable."

\* \* \*

Ray stood in the office doorway with his first invoice, torn off the Gagnon & Son pad. Ellen was at the adding machine with her back to him, standing, and the tape had run so long off the roller that it curled over the edge of the desk and lay in loops on the floor by her boots. Denise had gone home, and behind him the building was dark and quiet all the way to lane ten.

"Shut the door," she said, with her back to him. "The heater cannot keep up with the hall."

He shut it. With the door shut his shoulder was against the old coat hooks, and he was a foot from her, and there was no farther to go.

He held out the invoice. She took it without turning all the way round and read it once, top to bottom, and wrote a check on the alley's book standing up, and tore it out, and laid it on the desk on his side of the adding machine.

He pulled his jacket zipper down a hand's width, put the check in his shirt pocket behind the Worcester page, and zipped the jacket back to his chin.

She tore off the tape. "That is the best Saturday in Arlene's ledgers."

"You'd know."

"I read all of them."

One of the green ledgers on the shelf over the desk stood an inch proud of the others. Ray reached past her shoulder and pushed it back in line, and ran his thumb along the spines to be sure.

"I paid the bank in Concord this morning," he said, "to keep my loan good till March first. Normie read the envelope over the mail tray, so you'd better have it from me before the post office does."

She turned round. She stood with her back against the desk and the tape in her hand, close enough that he could see the adding machine's purple ink on two of her fingers.

"Tell me why."

"If I'd let it run out and you came up short, there'd be nobody to buy it March first, and Arlene would sit waiting on lawyers for her money and lose her place, and the condo fella from Concord would get the lot."

"That is Arlene's reason."

"If somebody's going to be standing at the bottom of your drive on March first, I'd sooner it was me."

"Then you will want to know what your league costs me," she said. "Seven hundred dollars every week it bowls."

"Always has," Ray said. "Arlene carried it."

"Arlene had you for nothing."

Under the zipped canvas the check in his shirt pocket gave a small crackle when he breathed in.

She took a sheet off the blotter and handed it to him. One page in her printing: the new lineage, from Tuesday, to go up on the vestibule board in the morning.

"I wanted you to hear it from me first."

"Arlene put this same notice up once," he said.

"First. December of two thousand four, posted on the board and charged from the next league night, in Arlene's hand. That is where I took the form." She tipped her head at the shelf, at the ledger he had just squared. "Second. Paragraph four says lineage stands as set in September, 'as the league's bylaws provide.' Third. I asked Verna for the bylaws the day after I closed."

"And Verna said."

"The secretary reads the bylaws aloud at the January meeting, in full, and the binder does not leave the secretary."

Ray had to look down at the notice for a second.

"There's a line in them says the September rate holds till the January meeting votes on it," he said. "Went in the January after Arlene's notice."

"Who put it there?"

His hand was still on the ledger he'd squared. "I did."

She looked at his hand on the ledger. Then she took the notice back from him and laid it face down on the tape, and smoothed it flat with the side of her hand, once. He took his hand off the ledger.

"At the new rate I make my number," she said. "At the old one I do not. Then I will put it to the January meeting."

"And I'll stand up against it," Ray said. "The Highway crew's on twelve-hour shifts all winter, and the Legion's living on what the government sends. They can't pay it."

"Will they vote the way you stand?"

"Mostly."

He had nothing left in reach to straighten, and his hands hung at his sides with nothing in them.

"I eat supper at a quarter past five, have for thirty years, and you've made me late twice this week, and I can't tell if you're doing it on purpose or you're just no good with a clock."

"I'm very good with a clock."

She said it to his face, a foot away, in the same voice she used for the time, and under it he heard the small change you hear in a motor the second before it catches. Her hand stayed on the edge of the desk, and his stayed at his side.

"I'll be on my feet in January, Ellen."

"Good. I'd hate to beat somebody who wasn't ready."

She sat down. Then she hooked the second chair out from under the desk with the toe of her boot, and left it there, facing her.
