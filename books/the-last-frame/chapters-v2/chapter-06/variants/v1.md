# Chapter 6: Walking Ice

"You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?" Hollis asked it into the thermos cup while he poured.

It was ten to seven on Saturday, the sun not up over the far shore, and through the bob-house's one window every flag on every tip-up lay flat.

Ray had his head in the heater's warm and his boots in the cold along the floor.

"I'm on call," Ray said. "It's printed on her sheet, and she pays by check inside the week, which is more than Arlene managed in twenty years."

Hollis drank and looked at him over the cup.

"You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?"

Same words, same flat voice. Out past the window something had come down off the public access and was crossing the ice toward them.

It was a navy vest over a parka, and it walked the way it stood at a counter, square, the weight even on both feet. She never watched her boots. First-timers watched theirs the whole three hundred yards, as though the lake might change its mind. She carried a five-gallon bucket in one gloved hand with the arm held out from her side, and it had slopped anyway, because the right glove was dark to the wrist. At the near tip-up she set the bucket down and went to one knee, the right one, easing the left. She hooked the smelt with her gloves on, slow and exact, and turned it once to look before she let it down the hole. Then she reset the flag and tried the spring with a finger. She had done it in her head the whole way out.

"Who told her it'd hold?" Ray said.

"Eleven inches of black ice at the access," Hollis said. "I drilled it Thursday. She asked was it black or white."

Ray watched her pick up the bucket.

"She asked what she could bring," Hollis said. "Told her the smelt bucket in my truck, and bait the near one coming in."

"You knew she was coming."

"Said seven. Said she'd like you here."

"You might've told me."

"You didn't ask."

She knocked on the orange door, on the stencil that said Hollis Pike with his address under it, at seven by Ray's watch. She came in stooping, set the bucket by the near hole, and peeled off her gloves, soaked dark to the wrist and stiffening, a good pair kept two winters by the look of the palms and ruined in three hundred yards. She hung them by the cuffs on the heater guard, and in a minute they were steaming and the shack smelled of smelt.

Hollis looked at the bucket, at the gloves on his heater, and at her.

"Got a license?"

"Yes."

Hollis waited. Ellen waited. The heater hissed between them.

"Let's see it," Hollis said.

She took it from inside the vest, folded behind three printed pages. Hollis read the front, turned it over, read the back, and read the front again.

"Says you bought this at ten past four this morning."

"I was up."

He handed it back and stood. "Going to check my flags."

"They're all down," Ray said.

"Then it won't take long."

He went out and shut the orange door on the cold.

That left four feet of plywood floor between them. She sat down on the upturned pail by the cooler before she had said a word to him, and Ray, who had watched her stand through a storm and a vote, knew it was settled.

She unfolded the three pages on the cooler lid and laid her bare hands on them.

"The last Saturday in February," she said. "An Open, on derby weekend. The whole Saturday, every lane, any team of four that pays the entry."

Ray said nothing.

"The Linda Gagnon Memorial becomes its final," Ellen said. "On all ten lanes. Open to anyone. Linda's trophy is the prize, and the league's teams bowl for it with everyone else. Page two is what happens if the ice goes. Without the Open, I do not make my number."

"Without Linda's name, you mean."

"The Open needs a final."

Ray stopped talking. He sat with his gloved hands on his knees and she sat with hers bare on her pages, and neither of them filled it. Ray let the quiet go on past where a man would say something to be polite, and then past that.

"You gave me your word," he said.

She had a finger on the corner of the top page. She kept it there.

"I told the whole league so. Verna wrote it down. Dale paid his lineage on it." He looked at her. "I stood up in that room, Mrs. Daly."

She lifted the corner to turn to the second page.

He was already pulling off his gloves, the right and then the left, heavy leather gone soft and dark at the palms. He leaned across the cooler, took her by the wrist and lifted her hand off the paper. Her fingers smelled of smelt. He opened the right glove with his thumb and started it over them.

"Verna asked would you answer," he said. "You said no."

He worked her first finger down to the web, then the second. The glove was a size and a half too long for her and still warm from his hand, and he pushed each finger home until it sat.

"You sat on that stool. You let it stand."

She let him do it. She held the hand out flat and still and watched his fingers on hers. He brought the cuff up over her wrist bone, let that hand go, and took the other.

"Dale's crew believed me. So did the Legion. Verna's got it in ink."

He did the left the same, the thumb last, and closed the cuff and let go.

She put her gloved hand to the corner of the top page to turn it. The leather would not part the sheets. She tried again with the thumb, and all three pages came up off the cooler together and fell back. Ray watched her look at that. He swallowed. Then she laid both his gloves flat on her pages and left them there.

"The house sets the lanes and the hours of championship weekend," she said. "Verna read it to the room."

"I heard her."

"I am inside the bylaws."

"The Memorial's the league's," Ray said. "Six years. Every one of them the league's."

"It is the house's weekend," Ellen said. "The league bowls its championship within it. I did not write it."

"Then you know what you said."

"I said, 'It stays. Unless the numbers say otherwise.'"

He had the pit behind five back in one piece: the flashlights down five, Denise's ball coming out of the dark, the whole house going up, and a voice a foot from his ear saying something he took for the building.

"I never heard that," he said.

She said nothing to it. The heater hissed. One of her wet gloves turned a little on the guard as it dried, and he let the quiet run as long as it ran, and she let it.

"Did you know I didn't hear it?"

Out toward the far shore the ice let go a long boom that came in under the shack and went away beneath their boots, one sound, the length of the lake. Her gloved hands closed on the pages she couldn't turn and creased the top one. She looked down at his gloves on her hands, and her eyes stayed there.

"I said it."

He had asked her once. He looked down at his own hands on his knees, bare, the knuckles nicked.

He got up, stooping under the roof, and opened the orange door, and the cold came in along the floor. He went out bare-handed with his gloves still on her, across the snow to Hollis, who was standing over a flag that was down.

"She wants an Open on derby Saturday," Ray said. "Any team of four, all day, and Linda's Memorial for the final."

Hollis looked down at the hole by the flag.

"Your people can come in off the ice and watch the Memorial for nothing, same as every year," Ray said. "Tell her that."

"Our people come in off the ice to bowl," Hollis said. He looked at Ray's bare hands. "They'll pay to bowl a final with Linda's name on it."

Ray put his hands in his jacket pockets.

"Plan if the ice goes?"

"Page two," Ray said.

The bell over the door at Gagnon & Son went at a minute past six on the Monday after next, nine days after the bob-house, with Main Street still dark. Ray was at the register recounting the drawer Danny had counted Saturday night, and the coffee maker by the Bench's stools was hissing through its last cup, and the plate under the pot had begun to burn.

Ellen came down the center aisle under the roof rakes and stopped on her side of the counter, three feet of worn oak from him. She laid his gloves on it, folded one into the other.

"Thank you for the loan."

He picked them up. They were warm all through, carried inside her vest, and he put them in his jacket pocket.

Neither of them said anything for a while. Ray's thumb found the seam of a glove in his pocket.

"You have been in my building four times since the bob-house," Ellen said. "Lane three, twice. Nine's ball return. Ten's sweep. You left the invoices with Denise and went out the back."

"Invoices get paid at the counter," Ray said.

"The chair is where you left it."

He did not answer it. She let it lie there, and then held up a printed page and a box of tacks.

"May I put this on your board?"

"Board's anybody's," Ray said.

She went to the corkboard over the Bench's stools, four yards off, and put the page up with a tack in each corner.

He read it from the register. She had printed it big and plain, for a man reading across a hardware store, and it said the Kettle Lanes Open, the last Saturday in February, with the Great Kettle Derby, any team of four. Under that it said the final would be the Linda Gagnon Memorial, on all ten lanes. Linda's name was there in somebody else's letters. He had never seen it on his own wall where somebody else had put it. The tack in the top corner had gone through the K of Kettle. The last line said cash refunds if weather stops play. Behind it the coffee maker hissed. It was a good notice.

She came back to the counter. "The derby committee voted it Saturday."

"Hollis said."

She stayed standing. There was nowhere on her side of the counter to sit, and on his side only Linda's chair, and she did not look at it.

"In the bob-house you asked me whether I knew—"

Ray lifted the stack of bills out of the drawer and counted them into his palm out loud, one, two, three, steady and no louder than every other morning. She stopped. She waited with her hands at her sides for him to finish, and did not fill it. He finished, wrote the count on the slip, and shut the drawer.

"You answered me."

"Mr. Gagnon. I would like—"

The bell went, and Normie came in stamping snow with the other two behind him. "And the owner of Kettle Lanes is in ahead of the Bench, boys," Normie said. "That's a first."

Ellen looked past Ray's shoulder at the door to the back room, the one door in the store that shut. Her eyes stayed on it while Normie got his coat off. She did not ask for it.

She turned to the Bench. "Good morning. There is a notice on your board. It concerns the last Saturday in February."

She went out, and the bell went once behind her.

Ray had his hand flat on the shut drawer. Normie went up off his stool to the board before his coffee was poured, reading it out in the Saturday-show voice. "Kettle Lanes Open, boys. Any team of four. And the final, on all ten lanes, the Linda Gagnon—"

On the last Tuesday before the half, Ray came in the front at ten past six with his team's lineage in an envelope, twenty minutes before league. The lanes were lit, the first practice boxes were crashing on two and three, and the Highway crew had the snack-bar stools with their burgers, the onion smoke off the flattop drifting out over the approaches.

At the register Denise had her head close to Ellen's and was saying something low and fast. A full rack went down on three and took every word of it. Ray stood six feet off with the envelope and got nothing, only Denise's mouth going and Ellen's eyes on the counter. Ellen shook her head once, and Ray's hand closed on the envelope. Denise dropped a fry basket into the oil harder than it needed and turned her back.

Ray laid the envelope by the register. Ellen was a yard from him across the counter, and over her shoulder, through the open office door, the second chair sat hooked out from the desk, facing hers, with nobody in it.

Dale Hutchins came off his stool and laid a yellow legal pad on the counter beside the envelope, squarely in front of her. In Dale's hand it said Highway Department, and under that, American Legion. Above both he had left one line empty.

"Gil Arsenault'll take us at Pine Ridge," Dale said. "Tuesdays."

"Go before the half and you forfeit," Ray said.

"Half's Saturday. We go the Tuesday after."

Ray looked at the pad. "Halsey Falls is forty minutes on the winter road."

"My crew eats at her counter Tuesdays," Dale said. "They can eat at Gil's."

Down at the far settees the Legion were lacing their shoes. Ray had known that team since the winter they broke the ball return on four, and he had their averages in his head, and their wives' names.

"Rest of them go where you go," Dale said, and held out the pen.

Ray took it. Ellen had the register drawer open and one hand on the till, and her eyes were on the pen.

"You put her word in Verna's book," Dale said.

"I know what I put in it."

The fryer hissed. Nobody at the counter moved, and Ray let it sit a breath longer than Dale liked.

He laid the pen across the foot of the pad, below the Legion. He took out his wallet and counted onto the counter beside the pad, in cash, Gagnon & Son's lineage for every Tuesday left before the Memorial. Ellen counted it once with her eyes and said nothing. She wrote a receipt on the alley's pad in her printing, tore it off, and held it across the counter, and for a second it was in both their hands. Her thumb had pressed a crease into the corner.

"Your receipt, Mr. Gagnon."

He took it and put it in his shirt pocket behind the Worcester page.

Dale looked at the cash, and at the pen lying below the Legion. "That's your answer."

"That's my team's."

Dale picked up the pad and the pen and walked down along the settees to the Legion. Their captain took the pad on his knee, signed it without reading it, and handed back the pen.

\* \* \*

On Monday, the first of February, the day before the second half started, Ray stood at the credit union's window at ten minutes to five and waited on a cashier's check. The teller slid it under the glass and kept two fingers on it, with Danny right behind Ray in line holding the store's deposit bag.

"Are you sure, Mr. Gagnon? It's nearly all of it."

"I'm sure," Ray said.

She took her fingers off it. He carried it to the stand-up writing counter by the door of the room where the loan committee sat on Thursdays, and took out the Gagnon & Son envelope he had brought from the store in his jacket. Danny made the deposit and came to stand beside him with the empty bag folded under his arm, and they kept their voices down, because the town was in line.

"That's your down payment on the alley," Danny said.

"It was."

"If she takes it, you can't buy it March first."

"That's right."

Danny looked at the check a while. The pen's bead chain rattled against the counter's edge when the woman in line leaned over for a deposit slip.

"What's it for, then?"

"So she can give the league its Saturday back."

"And still make her number."

"That's the idea."

"Store's got a line here," Danny said. "Take it out of that, and keep yours."

"I asked the store once."

Danny nodded and shifted the bag under his arm. "Is this you buying in, or getting out of the way? Because those are different invoices."

Ray didn't answer. Danny went out with the empty bag, and the door opened once on Main Street and shut behind him.

The check lay face down on the counter, and the teller's careful printing showed through the back of it, backward. Beside it lay his envelope, with Gagnon & Son in red in the corner and nothing else on it, and the pen on its bead chain. Kettle Lanes was a quarter mile out Kettle Road from where he stood. Her receipt was in his shirt pocket behind the Worcester page, in her printing, and he could feel the corner of it when he breathed. Down cellar at home there was a coffee can of old cotter pins that Linda used to call his retirement. He had never held the alley in one hand before.

He turned the check over and put it in the envelope, sealed it, and pressed the flap down once with the heel of his hand. He picked up the chained pen to write her name. The bead chain ran out and pulled short at the counter's edge. He held the pen over the blank place where her name would go, and then let it go, and it swung back and knocked against the counter. The envelope stayed blank. He put it inside his jacket, in the pocket where his own checkbook lived, and zipped the jacket to his chin.
