# Chapter 6: Walking Ice

Saturday morning, four days after the vote, the flags on Hollis's tip-ups were all down. Through the bob-house window Ray could see them lying flat on their springs across the snow.

The propane heater hissed on the wall, and at six foot one Ray had his head in the warm and his boots in the cold.

Hollis poured coffee from the thermos into its cup and asked his question into the steam.

"You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?"

"I'm on call, is all," Ray said. "It's printed on her sheet in her own hand, ten to close, and I send an invoice every time I go, and she pays by check."

Hollis drank, and asked it again in the same words and the same voice. "You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?"

Ray let that one go by. Something was coming out from the public access.

A navy vest over a parka was crossing three hundred yards of open ice at the pace of somebody who had worked out how long it would take. She walked the way she stood at her counter, square, with her weight even on both feet. First-timers out here watched their boots, every one of them, and stopped dead whenever the ice talked. This one kept her eyes on the bob-house. The bucket in her gloved hand rode out from her side on a straight arm so it wouldn't slop. At the near tip-up she set it down, went to one knee, and reached into the bucket for a smelt. She hooked it through the back, slow and exact, and let it down the hole on its line. The flag got folded down and set. She had never done any of it before. She did it right.

"Who told her it'd hold?" Ray said.

"Eleven inches of black ice at the access," Hollis said. "I drilled it Thursday. She asked was it black or white."

Ray watched her come on.

"She asked what she could bring," Hollis said. "Told her the smelt bucket in my truck, and bait the near one coming in."

"You knew she was coming."

"Said seven," Hollis said. "Said she'd like you here."

"You might've told me," Ray said.

"You didn't ask."

She knocked twice on the orange door, which had Hollis Pike stenciled on it in black, with his address under the name.

"It's open," Hollis said, and she came in stooping, with the bucket ahead of her. She set it down by the near hole and peeled off her gloves. They came away soaked dark to the wrist and stiffening at the fingers. She hung them on the heater guard, and inside a minute they were steaming and the whole shack smelled of smelt. Lined deerskin, Ray saw, two winters old, and the bait water had gone clean through them.

Hollis looked at the bucket, and at the gloves on the guard, and at her. "Got a license?"

"Yes," Ellen said.

Hollis waited. Ellen waited.

"Let's see it," Hollis said.

She had it inside the vest, folded behind three printed pages. He read the front and turned it over and read the back, and took longer over the back than there was on it to read.

"Says you bought this at ten past four this morning," Hollis said.

"I was up," Ellen said.

He handed it back. Then he stood, bent under the roof. "Going to check my flags."

"They're all down," Ray said.

"Then it won't take long."

Hollis ducked out and shut the door on the cold.

Ellen sat down on the upturned pail by the cooler before she had said one word to Ray, and he, who always noticed when she sat, knew that something had been decided.

She unfolded the three pages on the cooler lid and laid her bare hands flat on either side of them.

"The last Saturday in February," Ellen said. "An Open, on derby weekend. The whole Saturday. Any team of four that pays the entry may bowl."

Ray said nothing. The heater hissed.

"Linda's Memorial becomes its final," she said. "All ten lanes. Open to anyone. Her trophy is the prize, and the league's teams bowl for it with everyone else. Page two is what happens if the ice goes."

"Without the Open, I do not make my number," Ellen said.

"Without Linda's name, you mean," said Ray.

"The Open needs a final."

"You gave me your word," Ray said.

Her hand had gone to the corner of the top page, to turn it.

"In the pit behind five, the night of the storm. I told the whole league so."

"I was in the room," Ellen said.

"Verna wrote it in her book." He was pulling off his gloves, the right and then the left. "Dale's crew paid on it. They paid at your register that same night."

"I rang it," she said.

He took her hand off the page by the wrist and turned it palm down. His right glove went on over her fingers, and he worked the leather down the first one to the web and pressed it home with his thumb. Then the second.

"I stood up in front of twenty captains, Mrs. Daly." The third finger. "I put my name next to yours."

"You did," Ellen said.

"Dale said that'll do for my crew. He said it to your face."

"He said it across my counter," she said.

The fourth finger, then the thumb. She let him. When he reached for her other hand she gave it to him, and he began again at the first finger and did not hurry any of it.

"Linda's Memorial is the final," Ellen said. "It is bowled on the last Saturday in February, on all ten lanes, for her trophy. All of that stays."

"With anybody off the ice who's got the entry," Ray said.

"With anyone who pays," said Ellen.

She tried to turn the page then. The leather would not part the sheets, and the top one slid along with the one under it. She tried once more. Then she laid both gloved hands flat on the pages and left them there.

"The house sets the lanes and the hours of championship weekend," Ellen said. "Verna read it to the room."

"I heard her."

"I am inside the bylaws," she said.

"Then you know what you said," Ray said.

She kept her eyes on the pages. "I said, 'It stays. Unless the numbers say otherwise.'"

Ray was back in the pit behind five. Flashlights down the lane, Denise's ball out of the dark, the house going up all at once, and a voice a foot from his ear that he had taken for the building.

"I never heard that," he said.

She said nothing to it.

"Did you know I didn't hear it?" Ray said.

Out toward the far shore the ice let go a long boom that came across the lake and went under the shack and away, one sound. Her hands closed on the pages she could not turn and creased the top one. She looked down at his gloves on her hands, and her eyes stayed there.

"I said it," Ellen said.

He had asked her once. Ray looked at his own hands on his knees. They were bare, and the knuckles had gone raw from the cold along the floor.

He got up, stooped under the roof, and opened the orange door, and the cold came in at the ankles. He went out bare-handed, with his gloves still on her, and walked across the packed snow to Hollis, who was standing over a flag that was down.

"She wants an Open on derby Saturday," Ray said. "Anybody who pays can bowl, and Linda's Memorial is the final."

Hollis looked down at the flag. "League bowls in it?"

"With everybody else," Ray said. "Your people can come in off the ice and watch the Memorial for nothing, same as every year. Tell her that."

Hollis took his time over it.

"Our people come in off the ice to bowl," Hollis said. "They'll pay to enter a final. Nobody pays to watch one."

Ray put his bare hands under his arms.

"Plan if the ice goes?" Hollis said.

"Page two," Ray said.

The bell over the store door went at one minute past six on Monday morning, nine days after the bob-house. Ray was at the register with the drawer out, counting what Danny had already counted on Saturday night. By the Bench's empty stools the coffee maker was hissing through its last cup and burning it on the plate.

Ellen came down the aisle past the rock salt and laid his gloves on the counter, folded one into the other.

"Thank you for the loan," she said.

He picked them up. They were warm all through from riding inside her vest, and he put them in his jacket pocket.

"You have been in my building four times since the bob-house," Ellen said. "Lane three, twice. Nine's ball return. The sweep on eight. You left the invoices with Denise and went out the back."

"Invoices get paid at the counter," Ray said.

"The chair is where you left it."

He didn't answer that.

She had one printed page in her hand, and a small box of tacks. "May I put this on your board?" she said.

"Board's anybody's," Ray said.

She tacked it up over the Bench's stools, one tack in each corner, and he read it from the counter.

It was the Kettle Lanes Open, on the last Saturday in February, with the derby. Any team of four could enter. The final was the Linda Gagnon Memorial, on all ten lanes. Her name sat in the middle of the page in heavy black print, a plain square typeface off a printer, on the wall of his own store. The top tack had gone through the corner of the paper and into the cork on the first push. Behind him the coffee maker hissed and quit. The last line on the page was smaller than the rest. It said, Cash refunds if weather stops play. He read her name again. It was spelled right.

"The derby committee voted it Saturday," Ellen said.

"Hollis told me last night," Ray said.

She came back to the counter and stood at it. There was nowhere in the store to sit but Linda's chair, behind the counter at his elbow, and she did not look at it.

Ellen said, "In the bob-house you asked me whether I knew—"

Ray took the stack of bills out of the drawer and counted them into his palm aloud, steady, not loud. "One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine."

She stopped. She waited for him to finish, and did not fill it.

"Ten, eleven, twelve." He finished, and wrote the count on the slip, and shut the drawer. "You answered me."

"Mr. Gagnon," Ellen said. "I would like—"

The bell went over the door. Normie came in stamping snow off his boots, with the other two behind him, and stopped on the mat to call it.

"And would you look at that, boys," Normie said. "The owner beats the Bench to the coffee. That's a first in the books."

Ellen looked at the door to the back room, the only door in the store that shut. Ray watched her look at it. She did not ask for it.

The Bench had stopped on the mat, and she turned to them. "There is a notice on your board," she said. "It concerns the last Saturday in February."

She went out, and the bell went once behind her.

Ray's hand was still flat on the shut drawer. Normie went straight past the stools to the board before anybody had poured him a cup, reading it out in the voice he kept for the Saturday show.

"The Kettle Lanes Open. Any team of four. The final, on all ten lanes, the Linda Gagnon—"

On the last Tuesday before the half, Ray came into the alley by the front door at ten past six, with his team's lineage in an envelope. League was twenty minutes off. The first practice boxes were crashing on two and three, and onion smoke was coming off the flattop and drifting down the lanes. The Highway crew had the snack-bar stools and their burgers.

At the register Denise was saying something to Ellen, low and fast, with her head down close. A full rack went down on three and took every word of it. Ellen shook her head once. Denise dropped a fry basket into the oil harder than it needed and turned her back.

Ray laid the envelope on the counter. Through the open office door behind Ellen, the second chair stood hooked out from the desk and facing hers, with nobody in it.

Dale Hutchins came down the counter and laid a yellow legal pad beside the envelope, right in front of her. In Dale's square print the second line said Highway Department. The third said the Legion. The top line was empty.

"Gil Arsenault'll take us Tuesdays," Dale said.

"At Pine Ridge," Ray said.

"He's got the lanes."

"Go before the half and you forfeit," Ray said.

"Half's Saturday," Dale said. "We go the Tuesday after."

"Halsey Falls is forty minutes on the winter road," Ray said.

"My crew eats at her counter Tuesdays," Dale said. "They can eat at Gil's."

Down the settees the Legion team was lacing shoes. Ray had known them since the winter they broke the ball return on four. On six, Hollis sat with his own shoes half on and said nothing.

"Rest of them go where you go," Dale said, and held out a pen.

Ray took it. A yard off, Ellen stood with the register drawer open, and her eyes were on the pen.

"You put her word in Verna's book," Dale said.

"I know what I put in it."

Ray laid the pen down across the pad, below the empty line. Then he took out his wallet and counted cash onto the counter beside it, his own team's lineage for every Tuesday left before the Memorial, paid ahead. Ellen counted it once, without a word. On the stools the Highway crew had stopped eating. She wrote a receipt on the alley's pad in her printing, tore it off, and held it across the counter. It stayed there between them a second before he took it.

"Your receipt, Mr. Gagnon," Ellen said.

He unzipped his jacket a hand's width and put the receipt in his shirt pocket, behind the Worcester page.

Dale looked at the pen lying across the pad, and at the cash going into her drawer.

"That's your answer," Dale said.

"That's my team's," Ray said.

Dale picked up the pad and the pen and walked down along the settees to the Legion. Their captain took the pad on his knee, signed it without reading it, and handed back the pen.

On Monday, the first of February, the day before the second half started, Ray stood at the teller's window of the credit union at ten minutes to five. The teller slid a cashier's check across to him and kept two fingers on it.

"Are you sure, Mr. Gagnon?" she said. "It's nearly all of it."

Danny was right behind him in line with the store's deposit bag, the way he was every weekday before five.

"I'm sure," Ray said.

She took her fingers off it.

He carried the check to the writing counter by the window, where a ballpoint hung on a bead chain beside the deposit slips. Across the lobby was the door to the room where the loan committee sat on Thursdays. He had brought an envelope from the store in his jacket, one with Gagnon & Son printed in the corner, because he had planned this.

Danny made the store's deposit and came and stood at the counter beside him. A woman from the pharmacy was next at the window, and they kept their voices down.

"That's your down payment on the alley," Danny said.

"It was."

"If she takes it, you can't buy it March first," Danny said.

"That's right," Ray said.

Danny picked up the chained pen and put it down again, and the beads rattled against the counter's edge.

"What's it for, then?" he said.

"So she can give the league its Saturday back," Ray said.

"And still make her number."

"That's the idea."

"Store's got a line of credit here," Danny said. "Take it out of that, and keep yours."

Ray turned the check face down on the counter. "I asked the store once."

Danny looked at him a while. "Is this you buying in, or getting out of the way?" he said. "Because those are different invoices."

Ray didn't answer. Danny tucked the empty bag under his arm and went out, and the door shut behind him once.

The check lay face down by his hand, and the printing showed through the paper backward, his name and the rest of it. Beside it lay the envelope, with the store's name in the corner and nothing else on it. The pen hung off the counter's edge on its chain. He had put that money by one deposit at a time for thirty years, the way he kept the coffee can of old cotter pins down cellar that Linda used to call his retirement. The check was one sheet of paper. It fit in the envelope with room to spare.

He put the check in the envelope and sealed it, and pressed the flap down once with the heel of his hand. Then he picked up the pen to write Ellen's name. The bead chain rattled across the counter and pulled up short. He held the pen over the envelope. After a while he let it go, and it swung back on its chain and knocked against the counter's edge. Nothing was written on the envelope. He put it inside his jacket, in the pocket where his own checkbook lived, and zipped the jacket to his chin.
