### Ellen's true sentence (for the writer; never stated on the page in Ray's POV)

**What she could say:** *"Yes. I heard the strike take the second half. I saw you hadn't heard it, and I let it stand."*

**Why she doesn't, in s1:** she briefs and doesn't ask. "I said it" is a report, a closed fact with nothing in it for him to decide. The true sentence is an error report, and an error report hands the other person the decision. Here the decision is whether he keeps her. Bible rule 13: she lies only with a true sentence.

**Why she tries in s2, and why it fails:** nine days of him not coming as far as the office. The cost of the lie has arrived, and she does what she has done after every error of her career, which is go and report it. He won't let the subject open ("You answered me"), the town walks in, and she lets both stop her. That last part is hers. She could ask for the back room, and she looks at its door and doesn't. The reader should see the easier option close in action. Neither a sentence of hers nor of the narrator's closes it.

### Scene 1

**Setting:** Hollis's bob-house on Great Kettle Lake, about three hundred yards off the public access. Saturday, January 9, about ten to seven, hard clear cold, the sun not up. Six by eight, plywood on skids, the blaze-orange door with HOLLIS PIKE and his address stenciled on it, two holes in the floor skinning over, one small window onto the tip-ups. **Sensory anchor:** the propane heater's hiss, and later Ellen's soaked gloves steaming on its guard and smelling of smelt. Warm at head height, freezing at the ankles; Ray at six foot one has his head in the warm and his boots in the cold.

**Characters:** Ray, Hollis, Ellen.

**Want:**
- **Ray, at the open:** a quiet Saturday, and not to be asked what Hollis asks him.
- **Ray, once she's in:** her word to hold, meaning the Memorial as Linda bowled it. In the way is a plan printed on three pages.
- **Ray, then:** one plain answer. The reader learns this want at the line "Did you know I didn't hear it?"
- **Ray, at the end:** Hollis's no, so he doesn't have to ask her twice. The reader learns it at "Tell her that."
- **Ellen:** to deliver the plan on schedule, and to get through the morning without making the one report she has never made.

**Surface action, in order:**
1. **Open on the laugh, no setup.** Through the window every tip-up flag is down. Hollis pours thermos coffee into the cap and asks it into the cup: "You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?" **(keep)** Ray deflects with a mock-solemn line about his invoice, with **no fella story**. Model: "I'm on call. It's printed on her sheet. She pays by check." Hollis asks again, same words, same tone. Ray doesn't answer the second time, because something is coming out from the access.
2. **Interior passage 1: reading her by wear.** A navy vest over a parka, crossing the ice. She walks the way she stands, square, weight even, and never watches her boots, which first-timers do. She's carrying a bucket in one gloved hand, the arm out from her side so it won't slop. She stops at the near tip-up, sets the bucket down, and baits it with her gloves on, slow and exact. Ray watches her work a thing she has never done. End the passage on one short accurate sentence about her. Keep it physical, with no comparison to machines.
3. **Ice once, and the arrangement.** Ray: "Who told her it'd hold?" Hollis: "Eleven inches of black ice at the access. I drilled it Thursday. She asked was it black or white." **(keep)** Then: "She asked what she could bring. Told her the smelt bucket in my truck, and bait the near one coming in." Ray: "You knew she was coming." Hollis: "Said seven. Said she'd like you here." **No gloss on "like."** Ray: "You might've told me." Hollis: "You didn't ask." **(keep)**
4. She knocks on the orange door. She comes in, sets the smelt bucket by the hole, and peels off her gloves. They're soaked dark to the wrist and stiffening. She hangs them on the heater guard, where they start to steam. Ray notes what the gloves have been through, which is how he reads people, in one clause.
5. **The license check (the laugh, kept whole).** Hollis looks at the bucket, at the wet gloves, at her. "Got a license?" / "Yes." Hollis waits. Ellen waits. / "Let's see it." She has it inside the vest, folded behind three printed pages. He reads the front and turns it over and reads the back. "Says you bought this at ten past four this morning." / "I was up." He hands it back.
6. Hollis gets up "to check my flags." Ray: "They're all down." Hollis: "Then it won't take long." **(keep)** He ducks out and shuts the door on the cold. Plain and short. The laughs are spent from here on.
7. **She sits** on the upturned pail by the cooler before she says a word. Ray is the one who notices when she sits, and he knows it means something has been decided. One sentence.
8. **Her brief, no preamble.** She unfolds three printed pages on the cooler lid with her bare hands and starts, in full sentences with no contractions:
   - "The last Saturday in February." An Open on derby weekend, the whole Saturday, any team of four that pays the entry.
   - Linda's Memorial becomes its final, on all ten lanes, open to anyone, Linda's trophy the prize, the league's teams in it with everyone else.
   - "Page two is what happens if the ice goes." That's the only page number she says.
   - Optional, her true-sentence method again: "Without the Open, I do not make my number." **No narration translating it.** If Ray hears Linda in it, he says so out loud ("Without Linda's name, you mean."), and she answers with another true sentence ("The Open needs a final.").
9. **Ray's anger register.** He stops talking. When he starts again it's short and formal: she gave him her word. He told the whole league so, and Verna wrote it down. Dale's crew paid on it. "Mrs. Daly," once. He doesn't ask her to keep the Memorial. He asked once, on Dec 17, and he argues the word, not the want.
10. **The longing, inside the argument.** She reaches to turn to page two. He's already pulling off his gloves, right then left. He lifts her hand off the page by the wrist and starts the glove over her fingers, working each finger down to the web the way he'd seat a part, and **he doesn't stop arguing while he does it**. His sentences stay hard and his hands stay careful. She lets him. Other hand. **No sentence over 40 words in the fitting, no "and… and… and" run.** Then she tries to turn the page in his gloves. The leather won't separate the sheets. She tries once more, then lays both gloved hands flat on the pages and leaves them there. From here she has no brief to read from.
11. She argues the bylaw with the pages under her hands: "The house sets the lanes and the hours of championship weekend. Verna read it to the room." / "I heard her." / "I am inside the bylaws." The anybody/anyone duel from the earlier draft may stay as **two exchanges at most, with a speaker tag on every other line** so a listener can follow it.
12. Ray: "Then you know what you said." She, exact: **"I said, 'It stays. Unless the numbers say otherwise.'"** Quote it exactly.
13. **The pit, in one clause of memory:** the flashlights down five, Denise's ball out of the dark, the whole house going up, and a voice a foot from his ear saying something he took for the building. **No clutch, no motor, no "he could hear a … from the lot."** Then: "I never heard that."
14. She says nothing to it.
15. **The question, straight:** "Did you know I didn't hear it?"
16. The pause. The ice lets go a long boom out toward the far shore that goes under the shack and away, one sound. Her gloved hands close on the pages she can't turn and crease the top one. She looks down at his gloves on her hands, and her eyes stay there. **"I said it."** **(keep)**
17. **Ray takes it.** A plain answer, taken at its plain meaning. He asked once. He looks at his own bare hands on his knees (body only, no interior gloss on what he believes).
18. He gets up, stoops under the roof, opens the orange door, and the cold comes in at the ankles. He goes out bare-handed with his gloves still on her **(keep)**, across the snow to Hollis, who is standing over a flag that's down.
19. **The coda: the reversal, about 150 words.**
    - Ray tells Hollis her plan in two sentences: derby Saturday, an Open, Linda's Memorial for its final.
    - Then his alternative: "Your people can come in off the ice and watch the Memorial for nothing, same as every year. Tell her that."
    - Hollis: **"Our people come in off the ice to bowl. Not to watch your league."** The money fact, in his mouth, flat. Hollis may add one plain clause so a listener can repeat it ("They'll pay to bowl a final. They won't pay to watch one.").
    - Then Hollis's one question, the safety man's: **"Plan if the ice goes?"**
    - Ray, who read the pages and can't help knowing: **"Page two."** Last line.

**Rules for steps 9–17:** no joke, no fella, no motor comparison, no "she" for a machine, no cutaway. From "You gave me your word" to "I said it" runs **at least 400 words** on the page, in real time.

**Subtext:** She came prepared for everything on the ice. What she isn't prepared for is answering without her pages, and he takes them from her with the same hands that are looking after her. He walks out to get his oldest friend to say no for him, and ends up answering for her plan out loud.

**Texture budget:** Two.
- The ice thickness and the bait errand through Hollis. These show her precision ("black or white?") and that Hollis arranged the morning.
- The license, which shows she saw Hollis's test coming at ten past four in the morning.

**Point of view:** Ray. Plain physical interior. Anger register in the argument. Her lie is visible only from outside.

**Ends on:** **Reversal.** "Page two." Ray's line, last. Nothing after it, and no look back at the shack.

**Want:**

- **Ray** wants his gloves back and her out of his store before she says it again, because hearing "I said it" twice would mean the missing half is his twice. The reader learns it at "You answered me."

**Surface action, in order:**
1. **The date in the first two sentences, plainly.** The bell over the door at one minute past six, nine days after the bob-house.
2. Ellen at the counter. She lays his gloves on it, folded one into the other. "Thank you for the loan." **(keep)** He picks them up. They're warm, carried inside her vest. That's one clause, and it's the scene's longing. He puts them in his jacket pocket.
3. **The three weeks, priced in her voice, as a report.** "You have been in my building four times since the bob-house. Lane three, twice. Nine's ball return. You left the invoices with Denise and went out the back." Ray: "Invoices get paid at the counter." Ellen, a true sentence that is as near as she can come to asking: **"The chair is where you left it."** He doesn't answer it.
4. **The notice.** She has one printed page and a box of tacks. She asks if she may put it on the Bench's board. Asking is new for her, and it's one line. Ray: "Board's anybody's." She tacks it up. He reads it from the counter.
5. **Interior passage 2: Linda's name in print.** The Kettle Lanes Open, the last Saturday in February, with the Great Kettle Derby, any team of four. Its final is the Linda Gagnon Memorial, on all ten lanes. The last line on the page is **cash refunds if weather stops play.** Ray reads Linda's name in somebody else's typeface on his own wall. Physical, in front of him: the font, the tack through the corner, the coffee's hiss. End on one short sentence. **No reasoning.**
6. **The committee's yes, one exchange.** Ellen: "The derby committee voted it Saturday." Ray: "Hollis said." (He heard it over cribbage Sunday. One clause, or nothing.)
7. **The try.** She comes back to the counter and **stays standing.** There's nowhere to sit but Linda's chair behind his counter, and she doesn't look at it. "In the bob-house you asked me whether I knew—"
   - **Ray counts the drawer aloud over her**, twenties into his palm, steady, not loud. In words, never totalled, never a money fact.
   - She stops. She waits for him to finish, and doesn't fill it.
   - He finishes, writes the count on the slip, and shuts the drawer. "You answered me."
   - Ellen: "Mr. Gagnon. I would like—"
8. **The room stops her.** The bell. Normie and the Bench, stamping snow, calling it like the Saturday show: "And the owner's in ahead of the Bench. That's a first." (Or similar; their line is the scene's laugh.) Ellen looks at the door to the back room, the one place in the store with a door that shuts. She doesn't ask for it. **That look and the not-asking are the beat.** Ray sees both, and the page says only what he sees.
9. She turns to the Bench and briefs them, polite and exact, which is her fear register: "There is a notice on your board. It concerns the last Saturday in February." **Never "before you hear it."** She goes out, and the bell goes once behind her.
10. **Cut into motion.** Normie is up off his stool to the board before his coffee is poured, reading it aloud in the Saturday-show voice: "Kettle Lanes Open… any team of four… final, on all ten lanes, the Linda Gagnon—". Cut there. Ray's hand is still on the shut drawer.

**Subtext:** She came at the one minute of the day the store would be empty, to make the report she has made after every error of her life. He wouldn't take it, because taking it means hearing the missing half was his. The town came in, and she let all of it stop her.

**Texture budget:** One. The notice, and its last line (cash refunds). It shows she planned for weather, which is the thing Hollis voted for, and it plants Ch 09.

**Point of view:** Ray. Short, formal and no names in his lines. The Bench talks in play-by-play. Ellen has no contractions.

**Ends on:** **Mid-action.** Normie reading aloud, cut at Linda's name. No reaction beat.

**Word target:** 600

### Scene 3

**Setting:** Kettle Lanes, **the last Tuesday before the half**, January 26, about ten past six, twenty minutes before league. The lanes are lit and the first practice boxes are crashing on two and three. The Highway crew are on the snack-bar stools with their burgers. **Sensory anchor:** onion smoke off the flattop drifting down the lanes. Don't add "same as every Tuesday." The office door behind the counter is open; the second chair is hooked out, facing hers, empty.

**Characters:** Ray, Ellen (at the register), Dale Hutchins, Denise, the Legion's captain (walk-on, signs). Hollis may be on six's settee, silent.

**Want:**
- **Ray** wants to keep his own team in this building without telling Dale, in front of her, that he didn't hear her. The reader learns it at "I know what I put in it," and then at the money.
- **Dale** wants Ray's name on the top line, because the rest go where Ray goes.
- **Ellen** wants to know what Ray will do with the pen. She can't ask, so she watches the pen and not the money.

**Surface action, in order:**
1. **The date in the first two sentences, plainly:** the last Tuesday before the half. Ray comes in by the front with his team's lineage in an envelope. He collects it from his three and pays it at the counter every week, which is the captain's job.
2. **Denise, heard by nobody.** At the register Denise is saying something low and fast to Ellen. A box goes down on three, and Ray can't hear a word of it. Ellen shakes her head once. Denise drops a fry basket into the oil harder than it needs and turns her back. **No gloss, no "honey" on the page.** The man who can name a machine by its knock from the lot watches two women talk three feet away and gets nothing. The page does not point this out.
3. Ray lays the team's lineage on the counter. Over Ellen's shoulder, through the open office door, he sees the second chair, hooked out and facing hers, with nobody in it. One clause.
4. **Dale lays the legal pad on the counter**, beside Ray's envelope and in front of Ellen. On it in Dale's square print: the Highway Department, and the Legion under it. The top line is empty. Spread the names across lines:
   - Dale: "Gil Arsenault'll take us at Pine Ridge. Tuesdays."
   - Ray: "Go before the half and you forfeit."
   - Dale: "Half's Saturday. We go the Tuesday after."
   - Ray, or Dale: "Halsey Falls is forty minutes on the winter road."
   - Dale: "My crew eats at her counter Tuesdays. They can eat at Gil's." This plants the margin that walks in Ch 07.
5. "What broke," once: Ray has known that Legion team since the winter they broke the ball return on four.
6. Dale: "Rest of them go where you go." He holds out the pen. Ray takes it. Ellen is a yard away with the register open, and her eyes are on the pen.
7. Dale: **"You put her word in Verna's book."** Ray: **"I know what I put in it."** That's all the talk about the minutes. **No interior about the minutes.**
8. **Ray's answer, in action.** He lays the pen down on the pad, not on the top line. He takes out his wallet and counts onto the counter, beside the pad, Gagnon & Son's lineage for every Tuesday left to the Memorial, in cash. No figure and no number of weeks. Ellen counts it once, without speaking. She writes a receipt on the alley's pad in her printing, tears it off, and holds it across the counter. It stays between them a second before he takes it. That's the scene's longing. Ellen: "Your receipt, Mr. Gagnon." He puts it in his shirt pocket behind the Worcester page.
9. Dale reads it for what it is. Ray has answered for his own team and nobody else's. Dale: "That's your answer." Ray: "That's my team's." This is new for a man who speaks for everybody, and the page doesn't say so.
10. **Consequence.** Dale picks up the pad and the pen and walks down the settees to the Legion. Their captain takes the pad on his knee, signs it without reading, and hands back the pen. End there.

**Subtext:** He won't sign against her and he won't speak for her. So he pays her, in front of the man he vouched to, for his own four bowlers and no one else, and lets the league go where it's going. She takes his money across the counter, and it's the only thing they've handed each other since the gloves.

**Texture budget:** Two.
- The half rule (leave without forfeit only at the half). It shows why the walkout waits and plants Ch 07.
- Lineage paid at the counter. It turns Ray's answer into an action.

**Point of view:** Ray. Short and level with Dale. Dale is laconic and makes no speech. He does **not** use his Ch 07 line ("Ray said it. It's in Verna's book. I'm only reading it back."); save it.

**Ends on:** **Consequence.** The Legion captain signs and hands back the pen. No line after it, no interior, and no look at Ellen.

**Word target:** 550

### Scene 4

**Setting:** The credit union lobby on Main Street, **Monday, the first of February, the day before the second half starts**, about ten minutes to five. Two teller windows, a stand-up writing counter with deposit slips and a ballpoint on a bead chain, and the door to the room where the loan committee sits on Thursdays. **Sensory anchor:** the bead chain rattling against the counter's edge whenever anyone picks up the pen. One other customer in line, unnamed.

**Characters:** Ray, Danny, the teller (unnamed walk-on, one line).

**Want:**
- **Ray** wants it drawn and sealed before the window shuts and before he can talk himself out of it. The reader learns what it's **for** at his answer to Danny ("So she can give the league its Saturday back."). The reader learns what it **costs** at Danny's line about the alley and Ray's "That's right."
- **Danny**, behind him in line with the store's deposit bag as he is every weekday before five, wants his father to say out loud what he's buying.

**Surface action, in order:**
1. **The date in the first two sentences, plainly.** Ray at the window. The teller hands a cashier's check across, and asks it with Danny standing right behind him: **"Are you sure, Mr. Gagnon? It's nearly all of it."** Ray: "I'm sure." **No figure, anywhere, ever.**
2. Ray takes it to the writing counter. He has brought a Gagnon & Son envelope from the store in his jacket, because he planned this. Danny makes the store's deposit and comes to stand at the counter beside him. They keep their voices down because the town is in line.
3. **What it costs.** Danny, in inventory: "That's your down payment on the alley." Ray: "It was." Danny: "If she takes it, you can't buy it March first." Ray: **"That's right."** Nobody explains Concord, the loan or how a bank counts. The reader has Concord from Ch 04 and Ch 05, and that's enough.
4. **What it's for.** Danny: "What's it for, then?" Ray: "So she can give the league its Saturday back." Danny: "And still make her number." Ray: "That's the idea." That's all the reader learns about the money.
5. **The store, refused.** Danny: "Store's got a line here. Take it out of that, and keep yours." He means it kindly and practically, as a man protecting his father's alley money. Ray: **"I asked the store once."** Lucien and 'seventy-four get one clause at most, or nothing; the reader has it.
6. Danny's last line, in inventory, in the model of the bible's: **"Is this you buying in, or getting out of the way? Because those are different invoices."** Ray doesn't answer. Danny goes out with the empty deposit bag. The door, once.
7. **Interior passage 3: what's in front of him.** The check face down on the counter, the teller's careful printing showing through, his own envelope, the pen on its chain. Physical and patient. One clause of memory at most (the coffee can of old pins down cellar that Linda called his retirement is the right size of thing). End on one short accurate sentence. **No reasoning about banks, Ellen's numbers or what tomorrow holds.**
8. **The outline's ending.** He puts the check in the envelope, seals it, and presses the flap once with the heel of his hand (Ch 04 ran a thumb along a flap; don't copy it). He picks up the chained pen to write her name. The bead chain rattles and pulls short. He holds the pen over the envelope, then lets it go, and it swings back against the counter. Nothing is written. He puts the envelope inside his coat, in the pocket where his own checkbook lives, and zips the coat to his chin.

**Subtext:** He can't ask her twice to keep Linda's night, so he's paying to give it back to her, with the alley he has wanted for thirty years. The envelope is blank because he doesn't know whether he's writing to the owner or to Ellen. His son has just asked him the same thing in inventory terms.

**Texture budget:** Two.
- The cashier's check at the window: "nearly all of it," said in front of his son.
- The store's line, offered at the same credit union, and refused. This pays Lucien's 1974 and keeps the money his own.

**Point of view:** Ray. His interior is patient and physical. The tender register goes to what his hands do with the pen and the flap, never to her. Danny talks in inventory.

**Ends on:** **Question** (the outline's ending). The envelope inside his coat, nothing written on it, and the zipper up. Last beat. No interior about tomorrow, no plan stated, no clock, no shade pulled down, no snow in the window. The reader should be asking what he'll do with it, and whether he'll tell her what it cost.

**Word target:** 550

## Tournament lead instructions

All three variants write the same four scenes with the same beats, endings, word targets and floors. Only the lead changes.

**V1: Lead with the couple in the room, with the rift as distance.** *(From reader notes §8: "After the bob-house, the two of them share no scene for the rest of the chapter… Half the chapter is the romance offstage." And §4: "The fight has no aftermath… Either the rift is real or it isn't.")* Play the rift as manners between two people who are excellent at manners.
- In every scene, track the distance between Ray and Ellen in feet, and what passes between their hands instead of words: the gloves going on in s1; the gloves coming back and the notice and the tack in s2; the cash, the receipt and the pen she watches in s3.
- In s4 she isn't there. Keep her present in the blank place on the envelope where her name would go, and in the receipt already in his shirt pocket.
- Let every silence between them sit one beat longer than is comfortable before anyone fills it.
- Ray reads her only by what he can see and hear. Each reading registers on him in one plain physical clause and is never explained.
- The longing is in what the hands do while the mouths behave.

**V2: Lead with pressure and pace.** *(From reader notes §2, the boredom ledger, where the chapter "loses steam three times in a row: a committee meeting, a petition, and a page of arithmetic," and §1, "The page hasn't made me afraid of anything.")* Every scene has a clock or a third party bearing down that the reader can see, and the scene is played against it:
- s1: Hollis out on the ice, liable to open the door; the bait water freezing in her gloves on the heater.
- s2: the Bench due through the door any second; she knows it, and so does he.
- s3: league at half past six, Dale's crew watching from the stools, and the half four days off.
- s4: the window shutting at five, a stranger in line, and Danny's deposit bag.

Enter each scene late and leave the instant its ending beat lands. No transit. Procedure in one clause or not at all. Play the laughs at full strength with no reaction beat. Aim at the bottom of each scene's range, never under s1's floor or its 400-word exchange.

**V3: Lead for the ear.** *(From reader notes §7, the listener pass ("Dates the ear can't hold," the page numbers, the untagged glove exchange, the 70-word run, three proper nouns in one breath), and §5 (the narrator explains subtext; engine similes; template sentences). The author's human gate is a text-to-speech listen.)* Write every scene to be heard, not seen:
- A speaker cue at least every third line in any exchange.
- No sentence over 45 words.
- One new proper noun per sentence, at most.
- Dates said as a weekday plus an event.
- One page number in the whole chapter (page two), said twice.
- Nothing that relies on italics or capitals for meaning.
- No narration that translates a line someone just said.
- Zero engine or motor comparisons.
- Never three sentences in a row opening on the same subject.

The voices carry who's speaking: Ellen's full stops and exact numbers, Ray's *and*-chains with the weight on the last word (his anger drops the *and*), Hollis's questions and measurements, Danny's inventory, the Bench's play-by-play. Read each scene aloud in your head before you finish it.
