# Chapter 9: The D-side

Her hands were empty on his back step, and she had no use for them. They went into the pockets of her vest under the open coat, where the right one closed on his front key, which had ridden there since the night he left it lying on her palm, and they came out again with nothing in them. The left corner of the step had iced under the drip from his gutter, so she had come up on her right leg and kept the left one back, and she was standing on his boards one minute before she was due.

The invitation had come pencilled under the total on his invoice for the belt on six, and she had paid the invoice to the cent and torn the bottom off and put it in her wallet: *Supper. Wednesday. A quarter past five. No charge.* It was the only line on any bill of his she had let stand without a correction.

Through the storm door a radio was talking to his kitchen. At the half hour it would get to the weather. She had heard it at noon in her office and at three in her car, and the Saturday storm had come farther inland both times, and she had carried none of it up School Street.

Her hand was up to knock when the inner door opened, and the storm door after it, and the heat of the kitchen came out over her face with hamburg and green pepper and tomatoes cooked down in it.

Ray filled the doorway. "Heard you come up. You're early."

She lowered the hand. "By one minute."

"I'll allow it." He held the storm door open against its spring with one long arm, and she went in under it.

The kitchen was small and yellow and too warm. He ducked the lamp over the table out of long habit, and she watched him do it from below and liked it; she so seldom got to look up at anybody. Two bowls that did not match sat on the oilcloth, and on the stove a dented pot.

He filled her bowl first and set it down in front of her, and did not let go of the rim until she had hold of the other side. "American chop suey, every night for thirty years, and it hasn't let me down yet," he said. "Doctor in Laconia told me to vary it, and I told him I'd think it over, and they've buried him since, and I'm still thinking."

She picked up her spoon. "What do you make the second night?"

He sat down across from her, ducking the lamp again. "That one."

He reached behind him and turned the pot on the burner so its handle faced the wall. "Pot's cooked for one six years now, and she's never had a repeat customer."

She looked from the pot to him. "Then I'll be a minute early for supper."

Ray's spoon stopped over his bowl, and he looked at her across the oilcloth. "Supper," he said, and went back to it.

The first spoonful was too hot, and she ate it anyway, and the second. It was salted right, the macaroni soft and the hamburg browned before it went in, with the green pepper still holding its shape. The bowl was hot through to the oilcloth under her wrists, and she left her wrists there.

Her eyes went round his kitchen and would not stay anywhere: the window over the sink gone dark blue above his yard, the furnace coming on under the floor, the radio on top of the refrigerator going through the news from Concord, each of them held a moment and let go, until they came back again to his hands, which were too big for the spoon and held it at the very end of the handle, with a white seam of old scar across the back of the left one and grease in the creases of the fingers where no soap had reached in forty years. She had stopped eating.

So had he. He was looking under the edge of the table at her boots. "What'd you do to the left one?" Ray said. "You came up my step on the right and favored the left, and that boot's worn down further on the inside of the heel."

She folded her hands on the oilcloth and told it to him in order. "Two thousand nine. Kate was fourteen, and she picked the trail because it had a black diamond and was called the Chute. She went down first and waited for me at the bottom. I met a tree on the third turn."

"You finish the run?" He leaned back until the chair creaked.

"All of it. Some of it standing." She picked her spoon up again.

"How much of it standing?" He was watching her mouth.

"To the third turn." She held her face exactly as it was.

Ray set his spoon down in his bowl and laughed with his head back under the lamp. "That'd be the winter the ice got under the town dock," he said, and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist.

The radio finished the oil prices and started on the weather. *"…a storm coming up the coast, heavy snow Saturday, beginning before noon, accumulations of—"*

Ray reached up and turned it off.

She stood up from his table with her napkin in her hand, and said nothing.

Ray looked up at her standing there. Then he reached up again and turned the dial back past where it had been, and the man's voice came into the hot kitchen louder than before, and Ray's hand stayed on the dial, and she stood with the napkin in her hand while the man began to read the inches.

\* \* \*

Ray's hands were inside three's table to the wrists, bringing it down off the top of its stroke a notch at a time. Under the day's load it had hung at the top with all ten pins held over the deck, and out past the masking a family had stood at the line, waiting for pins that did not come down. She had come along the catwalk from the steel door and set her radio on his bench, and she stood behind him and waited for it to set.

Warmth came off the motors all along the catwalk, with grease and hot belt in it. Through the masking came the crash of a full house, heard from behind, and the curtain on four bellied each time a ball came through it.

"She's been half a second late up top since December," Ray said, without turning round, "and I let her ride."

A ball came down three, and the sweep came across and went back, and the table dropped and set.

Ellen stood over his shoulder. "I would like to give you a relief briefing."

Ray stayed on one knee with the wrench in the table. "Go on, then."

Ellen said, "Weather. Hollis called at ten past four this morning. The ice is closed for the day, and he says the snow gets worse after two."

Ray's hands did not leave the wrench.

"Equipment. Three was late at the top of its table. You have just put it right."

Behind the curtain three took a ball and swept and set again.

"Traffic. A hundred and fifty in the building, forty-one of them children. Both morning squads are done. The Memorial at two, on all ten lanes. The Open's evening at six: ten teams, and most of them drive home to Massachusetts tonight."

He took the wrench out of the table and laid it across his knee.

"Special conditions." She stopped. She had said that heading at the start of every handover she had ever given, and whatever came under it had always come with a form to write it on. The curtain on four bellied and fell back. Ray waited on his knee, with the wrench across it, and looked up at her.

"At the Center they call it the D-side. Two people on one sector. One works the frequency. The other one sees what she can't while she's talking."

Ray's eyes were on her face, and she kept hers on his. "I keep the position. I want you on it with me. Today, and after Monday, whatever the bank decides."

Ray laid the wrench down on the catwalk and stood. He was taller than the pit had been built for, and he bent his head under the beam, which brought it down nearer hers, with a foot of catwalk between them and grease on his hands to the second knuckle.

"Read it back to me. I want to hear it in your voice."

He looked at his hands, and wiped one of them down the front of his jacket, and looked at her. "Snow's worse after two. Evening squad's driving to Massachusetts tonight whatever we do." He put the clean hand flat on the beam over her head. "And I'm on it with you. Today, and Monday, and after."

His eyes went down to her boots and came back. "And you haven't sat down since Hollis called."

He was right. In her whole working life she had never passed an item that came back rounded.

"Readback correct."

She sat down on the ledge behind three, with her back to the kickback, and the warmth off the motor came through her vest and into her shoulders. It was the first time she had been off her feet since four that morning. Ray watched her sit, and stayed where he was, with his hand on the beam. From the ledge she could see the underside of his jaw, shaved that morning and missed in one place.

"Ellen?" Denise's voice came off the bench, plain and loud over the lanes. "Hollis is at the counter with Dale on his phone. You back there?"

She was on her feet and walking the catwalk before Denise let go of the key. Ray picked up the radio she had left on his bench, and the wrench off the catwalk, and came after her.

\* \* \*

Hollis held his phone out from his ear in a glove gone white, snow on his blaze-orange hat and both shoulders. "Dale says the town wants everybody off the roads by four this afternoon. His crew's out plowing till morning."

Snow off every boot in the building lay gray under the settees, and the glass block had gone gray since noon.

"Three strings a bowler on all ten lanes is two hours and some," Hollis said. "Start at two, you're done past four. Who drives home after?"

Denise said, with the fryer basket in her hand, "Keep them, like December, and they're still telling that one at the diner, so bowl Linda's at two and the evening after, and I'll run this fryer till morning, and who pays for coffee? Nobody."

Ellen said, "Thirty-three in December. Today, this room, and a boiler that quits when the power does."

Ray had come up from the pit behind her with grease still on his hands, and he stood at her shoulder with his sleeve against her arm. "D-side says send them home."

Denise looked past the two of them at the trophy case and put the basket down.

Ellen pulled the gooseneck down. "The Linda Gagnon Memorial moves to Sunday at one, on all ten lanes, for the league's teams. The Open's evening is cancelled. Evening captains, please come to the counter for your entry in cash. Everyone else, finish the box you are on and drive home slowly."

Forty cars, one plowed lane, Hollis's plow ahead of them: the vans with car seats first, Massachusetts plates last, since they had the farthest to go and would most want to hurry.

A Legion boy about twenty, bag on his shoulder, leaned in. "My aunt Judy from the bank was coming at two to watch me. She'll come Sunday at one." Ellen nodded once.

The derby treasurer was next, in his Sportsmen's Club jacket. "Half my tickets went for a six o'clock draw. Send them home at three and you've killed it. Hold it till six. They'll come back."

Ellen's hand lay flat on the counter by Ray's. "Nobody comes back up that drive tonight. The raffle is the derby's. Draw it now."

He drew at the ball return with half the ticket holders out in the lot scraping windshields, and said the committee would hear about this. She had no answer for him. Denise read the number twice over the PA to a room putting its coats on, and nobody came up. The prize, an ice auger with a red bow, leaned on the shoe cubbies.

Ten envelopes stood on end in the cash box, the team names in Denise's round hand. Ray's forearm lay on the counter by the box, and each captain came to her past his arm. To each she said the same thing. "Keep to the plowed side of Kettle Road. Drive slowly."

The first went to a Massachusetts captain with two car seats in his van. The evening went down the drive in license plates.

One read HUTCHINS. Dale came in off the plow with snow to the knee, took his wife's envelope without a word, put it inside his coat, and went back out to his truck. Ray watched him go, his arm still against hers.

Thirty thousand stood where Judy had set it, every envelope went the wrong way from it, and Monday was two days off.

The tenth, and the box was light in her hands.

By a quarter to four the lot was empty, and Denise had gone with the last cars to beat the town's four o'clock. Ten lane lights burned over nobody.

The vestibule door opened on a man with snow up both legs and his hat gone white, breathing hard from the drive. "Which one of you's got my auger?"

Ellen said, "Neither of us. It is by the shoe cubbies, with the bow on." Ray's shoulder shook once against hers.

He put it over his shoulder and went back out into the storm with it, toward a lake that was closed.

Ellen turned the empty cash box toward him. "That was sixteen hundred dollars. I'm seventeen hundred and fifteen under thirty thousand, and Sunday can't bring that much."

Ray put his hand flat on the counter beside hers.

"Arlene's room is on it."

Ray's hand went to the zipper of his canvas jacket. She saw it go there. He pulled the zipper to his chin, over the inside pocket. "I asked you once, on the hood of my truck. I don't ask twice."

She let out a breath. "In January I was four thousand short, and four thousand at eight to one was more than I have. Seventeen hundred and fifteen at eight to one is nearly all of it."

He leaned on the counter beside her. He had heard her half of that call through her front door in December. "That's the rest. You swore Kate you'd keep the rest out."

"I did." She kept her eyes on the box.

He turned his head to her. "What'll you do?"

"Ask her."

"And if she says no?" He said it to the side of her face.

She reached past his arm to the lane-light panel and put the toggles down, one to ten, and the building went dark a lane at a time.

\* \* \*

Kate's name was lit on the phone in her hand, ringing, and she stood at the dark window over the lake with it. Out on Kettle Road, the headlights that had followed her car from the alley reached the end of the plowing, turned in somebody's drive, and went back toward town. Under the new snow the ice let go with a boom near the camp, and the sound ran away out across the lake until she could not hear where it ended. Behind her on the table lay the legal pad with this call written out on it in three items, the pen laid across them, and she had left it there.

Kate picked up with water running and a pan going down on a burner. "It's Saturday."

"It is. I checked."

"You call Sundays at seven—" The water went off. "Are you hurt?"

"No. I'm calling to ask you something before I decide it."

The burner clicked off at Kate's end, and nothing else came down the line.

"On the second of December I swore to you. The house, and not the rest. I want to break it."

"How much of the rest?" Kate said. "No, wait. It's the alley. Don't give me dollars, Mom, or I'll be doing subtraction at this stove all night."

"Nearly all of it, if Sunday brings nothing."

A pan lid went down on the counter. "Is anybody there with you?"

"Not in the camp. Ray Gagnon followed my car out from the alley as far as the plow went, and turned around there."

"And if I say no?" Kate said.

"Then I keep my word. Arlene Fortier loses her place at The Pines, and it'll be my name that put her there."

Kate was quiet long enough that her refrigerator in Manchester came down the line. Out past the point the ice went again, farther off. Then Kate said, "Scale of one to ten."

"Ten."
