# Chapter 9: The D-side

Her hands were empty on his back step. They went into her vest pockets, and the right one found the key he had worked off his ring at her counter, and they came out again. The left corner of the step had glazed where the eaves dripped, so she took it on her right leg and kept the left back, and stood at his storm door a minute before she was due.

The invitation had come penciled under the total on his invoice for the belt on six, in the square hand he labeled parts with: *Supper. Wednesday. A quarter past five. No charge.* She had paid the invoice the same afternoon and kept the bottom of it. It was the first thing he had offered her free that she had taken, and he had known to put it on an invoice.

The radio was talking behind the storm door. At the half hour it would come to the weather, and she knew what the weather was. She had heard it at noon and again at three, and both times the storm coming up the coast had moved farther north, with Saturday in it.

The door opened before she could knock.

"Heard you come up," Ray said. "You're early."

"By one minute."

"I'll allow it."

The kitchen's heat came onto her face after the cold of the step, and with it hamburg and green pepper and tomatoes cooked down a long way. He ducked the lamp over the table out of thirty years' habit, and she watched him do it. She liked looking up at someone, and seldom got to. On the oilcloth were two bowls that did not match, on the stove the dented pot, and on top of the refrigerator a radio giving out the price of heating oil in Portsmouth. The clock on his stove said fourteen past. That left sixteen minutes to the weather.

"American chop suey," Ray said, and filled her bowl from the pot. "Thirty years, every night, and it's never let me down once. Doctor in Laconia told me twenty years back I ought to vary it, so I went out and bought a second bowl."

She looked at the second bowl.

"What do you make the second night?"

"That one." He sat down across from her. "Six years that pot's cooked for one, and it's never had what you'd call a repeat customer."

"Then I'll be a minute early for supper."

"Supper," Ray said, and bent to his bowl.

The bowl was too hot, and she ate from it too soon and burned the roof of her mouth and went on eating. He had salted it right. The heat of it came through the oilcloth into her wrists where they lay along the edge of the table, and she left them there.

Across the table his hands were too big for the spoon. He held it at the far end of the handle, as he held a part he had not yet decided to trust, with the white seam of an old scar across the back of the left one, which she had seen a dozen times by flashlight in the pit and was seeing now for the first time under a lamp, and in the creases of every finger the grease no soap had ever got out of him. She kept looking. The stove clock said twenty past, and there were ten minutes yet.

When she lifted her eyes he had stopped eating, and he was looking at her boots.

"What'd you do to the left one?"

"You were looking at my boots."

"You took my step on the right and kept the left out of it, and that boot's gone down on the inside of the heel some farther than the other one."

She told it short and in order, since she had no other way to tell it. "Two thousand nine. Kate was fourteen, and she picked the trail for its black diamond and for its name, which was the Plunge. She went down first and waited for me. I met a tree on the third turn."

"You finish the run?"

"All of it. Some of it standing."

"How much of it standing?"

"To the third turn."

Ray put his spoon down to laugh, and wiped one eye with the back of his wrist. "That'd be the winter the ice got under the town dock."

On the refrigerator the oil prices ran out, and a man's voice came on that she knew from noon and from three. "Now the forecast. A storm coming up the coast, with heavy snow Saturday, beginning before noon. Accumulations of—"

Ray reached up from his chair and turned it off.

The furnace came on under the floor. He picked his spoon back up.

Ellen stood up from his table with her napkin still in her hand. She said nothing.

Ray looked at her standing. He reached up again and turned the radio back on, louder than before, and kept his hand on the dial, and the voice came back into the hot kitchen over him with his arm up and her on her feet, and began on the inches.

\* \* \*

Ray's hands were inside three's table, bringing it down off the top of its stroke a notch at a time. Under the day's load it had hung there with ten pins held over the deck while a family stood at the line waiting for them. She had come along the catwalk from the steel door, set her radio on his bench, and waited behind him.

Past the masking all ten lanes were going at once, a full house heard from behind, and at the end of every lane the curtain jumped when a ball came through. The motors along the catwalk threw off heat with grease and hot belt in it. On the bench his watch said half past one.

"She's been half a second late at the top since December," Ray said to the table. "I let her ride."

A ball came down three, and the machine swept and set.

He stayed on one knee with the wrench, and she stood.

"I would like to give you a relief briefing," Ellen said.

"Go on, then."

"Weather. Hollis called at ten past four this morning. The ice is closed for the day, and he says the snow gets worse after two."

Ray's hands stayed on the wrench.

"Equipment. Three was late at the top of its table. You have just put it right."

Three set again, on time.

"Traffic. A hundred and fifty in the building, forty-one of them children. Both morning squads are done. The Memorial at two, on all ten lanes. The Open's evening at six: ten teams, and most of them drive home to Massachusetts tonight."

He looked up at her from the knee.

"Special conditions."

The heading was there, and nothing she had a form for was under it. A ball came through on four, and the curtain lifted and fell.

"At the Center they call it the D-side. Two people on one sector. One works the frequency. The other one sees what she can't while she's talking. I keep the position. I want you on it with me. Today, and after Monday, whatever the bank decides."

Ray laid the wrench down on the catwalk and stood. He was taller than the pit allowed, and he bent his head under the beam, which brought it nearer hers, with a foot of catwalk between them and grease on his hands to the second knuckle.

"Read it back to me. I want to hear it in your voice."

"Snow's worse after two. Evening squad's driving to Massachusetts tonight whatever we do." He wiped one hand down his pant leg. "And I'm on it with you. Today, and Monday, and after."

He looked down at her boots, and back up. "And you haven't sat down since Hollis called."

She had never in her working life accepted a rounded number back from anyone.

"Readback correct."

She sat down on the ledge behind three with her back to the kickback, in the motor's heat, for the first time since before four that morning. Ray saw her sit. He stayed where he was.

The radio on his bench clicked, and Denise said, "Ellen, Hollis is at the counter with Dale on his phone. Are you back there?"

She was on her feet and walking the catwalk before Denise let go of the key. Behind her Ray picked up the radio she had left on his bench, and the wrench, and came after her.

\* \* \*

Hollis held his phone out from his ear at the counter, in a glove gone stiff with ice, and snow lay on his blaze-orange hat and across both shoulders of his coat. At the snack bar the fryer was going. Under the settees the boots had left gray puddles on the tile.

"Dale says the town wants everybody off the roads by four this afternoon. His crew's out plowing till morning."

He put the phone in his pocket. "Memorial's three strings a bowler on all ten lanes. Two hours and some, with Dale's team out on the roads. Start at two, you're done past four. Who drives home after?"

The glass block across the front of the building had been white at noon. It had gone the gray of dishwater.

Denise said, with the fryer basket in her hand, "Keep them, like December, thirty-three of them slept on those settees and paid for strings at midnight and they're still telling it at the diner, so bowl the Memorial tonight and bowl the evening after it, and who runs the fryer till morning? I do, and nobody pays for coffee."

"Thirty-three in December," Ellen said. "Today, this room, and a boiler that quits when the power does."

Ray had followed her up from the pit with the grease still on his hands. He put her radio on the counter by her hand and stood at her shoulder.

"D-side says send them home."

Denise looked past them both at the trophy case. Then she put the fryer basket down. Nobody said Linda's name.

Ellen pulled the gooseneck down and keyed it.

"The Linda Gagnon Memorial moves to Sunday at one, on all ten lanes, for the league's teams. The Open's evening is canceled. Evening entrants, please come to the counter for your entry, in cash. Everyone else, finish the box you are on, and drive home slowly."

Vans with car seats first, down the one plowed lane behind Hollis's blade. Then the pickups. The Massachusetts plates last, because they had the farthest to go and would most want to hurry.

A boy of about twenty with a Legion bag on his shoulder was at the counter before she let go of the gooseneck. "My aunt Judy from the bank was coming at two to watch me. She'll come Sunday at one, then."

Ellen nodded once.

Behind him the derby's treasurer, in his Sportsmen's Club jacket, set the raffle drum on the counter. "Half these tickets went for a six o'clock draw. Send them home at three and you've killed it. Hold it till six. They'll come back."

"Nobody comes back up that drive tonight. The raffle is the derby's. Draw it now."

He drew it on top of the ball return on one, with half the ticket holders out in the lot scraping windshields, and handed the stub to Denise at arm's length. "The committee will hear about this."

She had no answer for him.

Denise read the number over the PA, and read it again, to a room putting its coats on. Nobody came up. The ice auger with the red bow on it leaned at the end of the counter by the shoe cubbies.

The ten envelopes stood on end in the cash box with the team names on them in Denise's round hand. She gave them out one at a time as the captains came, and to every one of them she said the same sentence. "Keep to the plowed side of Kettle Road, and drive slowly."

The first went to a captain from Lowell whose van she could see through the door glass with two car seats in the back, and he thanked her for it as if she had given him something, and she said the sentence, and watched his taillights down the drive to the road before her hand went back into the box.

The evening was going down the drive one set of plates at a time.

Two, three, four. Through the door glass the lot was showing gaps, dark squares where cars had stood since morning, going white again while she watched.

The fifth said HUTCHINS. She was holding it when Dale came in off the plow in his town jacket with snow to the knee and held out his hand. She put his wife's envelope into it and said the sentence. He put it inside his coat without a word and went back out to his truck, and the yellow light swung once across the glass block and went away down the drive.

The bar sat where the bank had set it, and Monday was the bank.

Six, to a team of sisters from Nashua who argued over which of them would carry it. Seven, eight, nine.

The tenth went to a captain from Fitchburg with the longest drive of any of them, and when it was inside his coat the box was light in her hands.

The glass block had gone the color of slate. Denise put her coat on at twenty to four. She went out with the last two cars to beat the town's four o'clock. By a quarter to four the lot was empty.

The vestibule door opened on a man with snow to his knees and his hat gone white, breathing hard from walking back up the drive.

"Which one of you's got my auger?"

"Neither of us," Ellen said. "It is by the shoe cubbies, with the bow on."

He took it up onto his shoulder and went back out into the storm with it, toward a lake that was closed.

The cash box sat open on the counter between them with nothing in it.

"That was sixteen hundred dollars. I'm seventeen hundred and fifteen under thirty thousand, and Sunday can't bring that much."

The wind pushed at the door glass, and let go.

"Arlene's room is on it."

Ray's hand went to the zipper of his canvas jacket. She saw it go there. He pulled the zipper up to his chin, over the inside pocket.

"I asked you once, on the hood of my truck. I don't ask twice."

She let her breath go.

"In January I was four thousand short, and four thousand at eight to one was more than I have. Seventeen hundred and fifteen at eight to one is nearly all of it."

"That's the rest. You swore Kate you'd keep the rest out."

"I did."

"What'll you do?"

"Ask her."

"And if she says no?"

Ellen went to the panel and put the lane toggles down one to ten, and the building went dark a lane at a time from the counter out.

\* \* \*

The phone was in her hand with Kate's name lit on it, and she stood at the dark window over the lake. Ray's headlights had stayed in her mirror out Kettle Road as far as the plowing went, and at the end of it they had turned around and gone back toward town. Behind her on the table lay the legal pad with this call written out on it in three items. She had left it there.

Under the new snow the ice let go somewhere off the camp, a long boom that ran away down the lake until she could not hear it. She called Kate on Sundays and told her how the week had gone, and Kate told her what she had already decided, and neither of them had ever called the other on a Saturday night to ask anything. She pressed the name.

Kate picked up with water running behind her and a pan going down onto a burner. "It's Saturday."

"It is. I checked."

"You call Sundays at seven—" The water went off. "Are you hurt?"

"No. I'm calling to ask you something before I decide it."

The burner clicked off. "Okay."

"On the second of December I swore to you. The house, and not the rest. I want to break it."

"How much of the rest? No, wait, it's the alley. Don't give me dollars, Mom, or I'll be doing subtraction at this stove all night."

"Nearly all of it, if Sunday brings nothing."

A door shut somewhere in Kate's apartment. "Is anybody there with you?"

"Not in the camp. There's someone. He followed me home as far as the plowing goes."

"And if I say no?" Kate said.

"Then I keep it. Arlene Fortier loses her place at The Pines, and it'll be my name that took it from her."

Kate was quiet long enough that her refrigerator came down the line from Manchester. Out past the point the ice went again, farther off.

"Scale of one to ten," Kate said.

"Ten."
