# Chapter 9: The D-side

Her hands were empty, and she had no use for them on a man's back step. She put them in her vest pockets and took them out again. Every time she had gone to Ray Gagnon this winter she had carried something to hand him, a form or a page to sign, and tonight she had brought nothing up School Street but herself and the bad knee. The step had a skin of ice across its left corner, and she had taken it on the right leg and kept the left one back.

The invitation had come in pencil under the total on an invoice, the one paper of his she would take, and Denise had handed it over with her mouth pressed shut.

The kitchen light was on behind the storm door, and a radio was talking in there. She was one minute early, and she stood with her hand up and considered whether one minute broke thirty years of the man's evenings, and decided he would have something to say about it either way.

The inside door opened before her hand came down to the glass.

"Heard you come up," Ray said. He held the storm door wide with one long arm, and the heat of the kitchen came out past him onto her face. "You're early."

"By one minute."

"I'll allow it." He stood back to let her by and lifted his chin at the radio on top of the refrigerator, which had just given the hour. "There. A quarter past five. You're on time now, and we can both quit worrying about it."

Ray ducked the lamp over the table, a glass shade on a short chain hung for somebody shorter, out of long habit. She had spent most of her life looking across at people or down at a screen, and she liked having to look up at one.

Two bowls that did not match waited on the table. The pot stood at the back of the stove with a ladle in it, and the steam coming off it smelled of green pepper, with something sweet underneath that she could not name until she tasted it.

"American chop suey," Ray said, and took her bowl to the stove. "Doc Marchand told me fifteen years back a man ought to vary what he eats. Doc's in Florida now, and I've still got the pot."

She sat down in the chair he pointed to. He looked once at her sitting there, and brought the bowl back full and set it in front of her. It was too hot to eat. She held her face over it anyway and let the steam come up, then ate too soon, burned her tongue, and went on eating, macaroni and meat and tomato gone sweet from the long cooking, salted right, the bowl hot through to the table under her wrists. Neither of them said anything about it.

He ate across from her with his elbows off the table, and her eyes kept leaving the bowl for his hands, which were too big for the spoon and held it by the very end of the handle, with the old seam of a scar down one finger and a black crescent under one nail that no soap had got out, while the radio read the price of heating oil to the tenth of a cent. She had sat at a great many tables and could not remember looking at anyone's hands at one. She went on looking.

"What'd you do to the left one?"

She looked up from his hands.

"You came up my step on the right," Ray said. "And the inside of that left boot heel's worn down further than the other. Knee, I'd guess."

"You said you heard me come up."

"I heard you, and then I came to the glass to see what a minute early looks like."

She put down her spoon. "Skiing. Two thousand nine. Kate was fourteen, and at the top she picked the trail off the map because it had a black diamond and a name she liked. She went down it first and waited for me at the bottom. I went down it second and met a tree on the third turn."

"You finish the run?"

"All of it. Some of it standing."

Ray put his spoon down to laugh.

"How much of it standing?"

"To the third turn."

Nobody had heard it from her before. Kate had been there for it, and the surgeon in Concord had read the rest off a picture.

"Two thousand nine," Ray said, and ate a mouthful while he worked it out. "That's the winter the ice got under the end of the town dock and lifted the last section off its cribs and set it down a foot off true. Town's been meaning to get to it since."

She finished the bowl and set the spoon in it.

"What do you make the second night?"

Ray took his time with the question.

"That one."

He reached for her bowl. "I'll have to warn the pot. Six years she's cooked for one, and she's never had a repeat customer."

"Then I'll be a minute early for supper," she said, and heard whose word that was, and did not take it back.

The radio got through the oil and started in on the weather: a storm coming up the coast, heavy snow Saturday, beginning before noon, accumulations of— Ray had her bowl in one hand. He reached up with the other, and his fingers closed on the dial.

Ray's hands were inside three's table to the wrists, bringing it down off the top of its stroke a notch at a time. Under the day's load it had hung at the top with ten pins held over the deck, and the family on the other side of the masking had stood at the foul line and waited for them. She had come along the catwalk from the steel door and stood behind him until he was done.

The table came down and set ten. Ray sat back on his heel with the wrench across his knee.

"Three's been coming round slow since your party Saturday, and I let that ride."

A ball came down three, and the machine swept and set again. One caged bulb hung over the pair and threw his shadow up the block wall, and the warmth came off the motors along the catwalk with the smell of grease and hot belt in it. Through the gap under the masking the lanes ran backward and bright, rented shoes going by at the far end, and every one of the ten had something rolling on it, the crash of one running into the next until the building was full of it, all ten at once, a sound she could have stood in all afternoon if she had had the afternoon.

Ray stayed down on one knee by the machine, and she stood over him on the catwalk with her weight even on both feet.

"I would like to give you a relief briefing."

He looked up at her and did not move. "Go on, then."

"Weather. Hollis called at ten past four. The ice is closed for the day, and he says it gets worse after two.

"Equipment. Three was late at the top of its table. You have just put it right.

"Traffic. A hundred and fifty in the building, forty-one of them children. Both morning squads are done. The Memorial at two, on all ten lanes. The Open's evening at six: ten teams, and most of them drive home to Massachusetts tonight.

"Special conditions. Coolers on lane ten. The derby's raffle is in a ball return, and its treasurer counts it twice."

She stopped there. Ray waited for a fifth item with the wrench in his hand.

"At the Center they call it the D-side. Two people on one sector. One works the frequency. The other one sees what she can't while she's talking."

She took her hands out of her vest pockets.

"I keep the position. I want you on it with me. Today, and after today, whatever Monday says."

Ray laid the wrench on the catwalk and stood up. He was taller than the pit allowed, and he had to bend his head under the beam, which brought it down nearer hers, with a foot of catwalk left between them and grease to the second knuckle on both his hands.

"Read it back to me," she said. "I want to hear it in your voice."

Ray put one hand up on the beam and gave it to her. "Hollis at ten past four. Ice is closed, and worse after two. Three was late at the top of her table, and she's right now. A hundred and fifty in the building, forty-one of them kids. Mornings done. Memorial at two, all ten. Evening at six, ten teams, Massachusetts tonight. Coolers on ten. Raffle's in a ball return, and the treasurer counts it twice."

Her own words came back to her in his voice, low and even under the noise of ten lanes, the voice of a good pilot at three in the morning with a long night ahead of him and nothing in it he could not handle, her whole afternoon in the order she had put it, handed back by somebody else while she stood on the catwalk with one hand on the cold rail. She had given briefings all her working life, and at the treasurer she would have liked another item.

He had not let go of the beam.

"And I'm on it with you. Today, and after today, whatever Monday says."

Three set again behind him.

"And you haven't sat down since Hollis called."

"Readback correct."

\* \* \*

Hollis had his phone in his glove at the counter, and snow on his hat and across both shoulders of his coat, going to water on the wood.

"Dale says the town wants everybody off the roads by four. His crew's out plowing till morning."

He put the phone away inside his coat. "Memorial's three strings a bowler on all ten. Two hours and some, with Dale's team out on the roads. Start at two, done past four. Who drives home after?"

Denise came down the counter from the register with the fryer basket still in her hand. "Then keep them. Keep them like December, thirty-three of them slept on those settees and paid for strings at midnight and they're still telling it at the diner, and is this lot any different? It is not. Bowl the Memorial tonight, bowl the evening after it, and I'll run the fryer till morning and nobody pays for coffee."

Down the length of the building every lane was going, coolers stacked on the settees at ten, children three deep at the snack bar with their parents' money in their fists, the whole room smelling of wet wool and road salt over the fryer, and the glass block had gone from white to gray since noon.

"Thirty-three in December," Ellen said. "Today, this room, and a boiler that quits when the power does."

Ray had come up from the pit with grease still on his hands, and he stood at her shoulder.

"D-side says send them home."

Denise looked past them at the trophy case, and put the fryer basket down on the counter.

Ellen bent the gooseneck to her and keyed it. "Good afternoon. The town asks everyone off the roads by four o'clock. The Linda Gagnon Memorial moves to tomorrow, Sunday, at one o'clock, on all ten lanes, for the league's teams. The Open's evening squad is cancelled. Evening entrants, come to the counter, and you will have your entry back in cash. Everyone else, finish the box you are on, change your shoes, and drive home slowly. Thank you for coming."

Her voice went out over the ten lanes level and courteous, and the lanes went quiet a pair at a time to hear it. Under it she was already out on the drive, putting forty cars down one plowed lane to Kettle Road behind Hollis's plow, vans with car seats ahead of pickups, the Massachusetts plates last of all because they had the farthest to go and would most want to hurry.

The first at the counter was a Legion boy of about twenty with his bag on his shoulder, wanting the Sunday time. "Aunt Judy from the bank was coming at two to watch me. She'll have to come Sunday at one, then."

Ellen nodded once.

The derby treasurer pushed in behind him in his Sportsmen's Club jacket, red in the face from the lot. "Half my tickets were sold for a six o'clock draw. You send them home at three, and you've killed it. Hold it till six. They'll come back for it."

"Nobody comes back up that drive tonight," Ellen said. "The raffle is the derby's. Draw it now."

He went down to the ball return on lane one, where the stubs had lain heaped in the rack all day, stirred them with his arm and drew one, while half the people who had bought them were out in the lot scraping their windshields. He laid it on the counter in front of her.

"The committee'll hear about this."

Ellen had no answer for him. She passed the stub to Denise.

Denise read the number out twice on the PA to a room putting its coats on, and nobody came up. The prize was an ice auger with a red bow taped to its handle, and it leaned against the end of the counter by the shoe cubbies.

The envelopes stood on end in the cash box, ten of them, with the team names on the fronts in Denise's big round hand. Ellen took them out as the captains came to the counter, said each team's name and the captain's, put the envelope into a glove or a bare hand, thanked them for coming, and told them to keep to the plowed side of Kettle Road, every word of it courteous and exact. Under it she was taking each one off a number she had carried all month, this one and this one, with Monday two days off and the bar sitting where the bank had set it, and she heard herself wish the fifth captain a safe drive in exactly the voice she had used on the first. Through the glass block she watched the plates go down the drive and take that much of her winter with them to Massachusetts. Her voice did not change once, and she listened the whole way for it to.

The tenth went to a woman from Lowell with two boys in snow pants, and the box was light.

By a quarter to four the lot was empty. The last taillights went down the drive to Kettle Road and were gone in the snow. Denise had gone with them to beat the town's four o'clock.

Then the vestibule door opened on a man with snow to his knees and his hat gone white, breathing hard from the drive he had walked back up.

"Which one of you's got my auger?"

"Neither of us," Ellen said. "It is by the shoe cubbies, with the bow on."

He took it off the end of the counter, bow and all, put it over his shoulder, and went back out into the storm with it, toward a lake that was closed.

Ray had not left the counter. The cash box sat open between them with nothing standing in it.

"Seventeen hundred and fifteen dollars under thirty thousand," she said. "Sunday can't bring that much."

He waited.

"Arlene's room is on it."

Ray put his hand on the lid of the cash box and closed it, and kept his hand there.

"What'll you do?"

"Ask."

"Ask who?"

\* \* \*

The phone was in her hand with Kate's name lit on the screen, and she stood at the dark window over the lake while it rang. The new snow had covered the ice to the far shore, and under it the lake boomed once, a long low sound that started near the camp and ran away out of hearing. She could have waited for Sunday at seven and told Kate in order, after. On the table behind her lay the legal pad with this call written out on it in three items, and she had left it there.

Kate picked up on the fourth ring, with water running behind her and a pan going onto a burner. "It's Saturday."

"It is. I checked."

"You call Sundays. At seven. You've called Sundays at seven since I was—" The water went off. "Are you hurt?"

"No."

"On the second of December I swore to you. The house, not the rest."

"Mom—"

"I'm calling to ask you something before I decide it. I want to break it."

Kate was quiet long enough that the refrigerator in Manchester came through the line.

"Okay. Okay, I'm sitting down. You're standing, aren't you."

"Yes."

"How much of the rest? Wait. It's the alley. How much? And don't give me dollars, or I'll be doing subtraction at the stove all night."

"Nearly all of it, if tomorrow brings nothing. I'll know tomorrow night."

"And if tomorrow brings something?"

"Then you'll get your call on Sunday at seven, and I'll tell you about the snow."

"Is anybody there with you?"

"No. I wanted to ask you first."

Out past the point the ice went again, farther off, and she stood and listened to it go. On the line Kate let out a breath Ellen had heard from her at the end of night shifts.

"Scale of one to ten."

"Ten."

"Then do it. And call me first next time. Like this."
