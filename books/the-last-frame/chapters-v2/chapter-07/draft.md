# Chapter 7: Loss of Separation

Ray got down out of his truck when she was halfway across the lot, and before she reached him he had the envelope out of his jacket and open and the check lying flat on the hood. It was a quarter to six, his engine had run in her lot for eleven minutes without his coming in, and the first league night of the second half began at half past six. The envelope lay on the hood beside the check, and there was no name on it.

She had come out in her vest and her new gloves and no coat. She had watched his parking lights from the vestibule for as long as she could stand to, and then come out to ask him what he wanted. The wind off the lake came across the lot from the ice and found the gap at her collar before she was ten steps from the door, and behind her the glass block threw its light over the plowed snow as far as his front bumper. His exhaust went sideways from under the tailgate and tore apart in the wind.

She pulled her right glove off with her teeth to pick up the check, and she saw him watch the bare hand.

"Don't count it," Ray said. "You'll lick your thumb like Arlene."

Her mouth wanted to go. She knew he was watching it, and she held it where it was and picked up the check.

It was a cashier's check drawn on his own savings at the credit union, her name and the sum set out in a teller's careful printing, and she read it once in the light off the glass block: forty-eight thousand dollars. She laid it back on the hood where he had put it and kept her bare hand flat on the paint beside it. The hood was warm. The heat came up through the steel into her palm while the wind went on at the back of her neck.

"This is your savings," she said.

"Near all of it," Ray said. "None of it's the store's, and Danny's seen it, before you ask."

"Mr. Gagnon—"

"I asked the Concord bank how a bank counts a winter like yours," he said. "Same people that wrote my commitment on this building, so they had the building's figures to hand and didn't mind saying. What the alley makes stays in the alley, and this goes on Arlene's note the day you close, and every eight dollars on the note takes a dollar off what they need you to make."

He looked at her hand on the hood. "You'll have that sum done before I finish saying it."

She had it before he finished. Six thousand.

"Let me get to the end of it," he said. "Write me down for whatever share that buys, and I'll sign what Arlene's lawyer puts in front of me. I've gone out your back door three weeks running, and I'm some tired of it. You give the league its Saturday back, Linda's name and all, and you've still got your room, and—"

Headlights came up off Kettle Road and climbed the drive. A town pickup with the seal on its door came over the rise and crossed the lot and stopped against the plow bank on the far side, nose toward them, lights on and engine running, and the beams lay across the hood and the check and both their hands. Ray put his palm down flat over the paper. Neither of them turned toward the truck. Through the windshield she could make out Dale Hutchins behind the wheel with his arms folded on top of it, and she knew from the set of his head that he could see everything at the hood and hear none of it.

Ray's hand lay over the check a finger's width from her thumb, and she wanted to fold the check once and put it inside her vest and close the vest over it, and she wanted the Saturday back for the league, and Linda's name off her notice, and him in her office after close with his jacket zipped to the chin while the tape ran, and his voice at a quarter past ten telling the clutch on seven it had kept them both late, and the finger's width gone, and she had wanted that one first and set it down last, and none of it would take a slot, and she let it come in whatever order it came, and he was saying—

"And Dale's crew comes home," Ray said, "on your word."

Her hand came off the hood.

Her eyes went past his shoulder to the foot of the sign pole, to the plow bank over the place where the two of them had dug down to frozen gravel in December, and stayed there. Above it the sign was on, red script over the white pin, its first letter dark. Then she put the glove back on and worked each finger down into it.

Ray said the rest to the check, with his hand still over it. "That's most of what I've got, under there. I'd sooner be in it with you than standing at the bottom of your drive."

Her voice, when she used it, came out level and courteous and pitched to carry to him over two engines and no farther.

"If I take it I'll never know which of us carried it."

Ray took his hand off the check. He did not argue it. He folded the check once, along the crease the envelope had given it, and slid it back into the blank envelope, and put the envelope inside his jacket and pulled the zipper up over it. Across the lot Dale's headlights were still on them.

"Then I keep my commitment."

\* \* \*

Dale Hutchins's hand flattened a folded sheet of legal paper on Verna's open binder, on top of the Legion captain's sheet, which was already there. He kept his palm on it.

All ten lanes were lit, and forty Tuesday bowlers sat down the length of the settees changing their shoes, and at the end of the snack bar the flattop stood scraped clean and cold, ticking now and then as the building's heat worked on it. Nobody had ordered a burger. The Highway crew stood at the end of the settees with their coats on and their bowling bags in their hands, and the Legion stood behind them, and the Legion's captain looked at the floor. Verna sat at the end of the counter with her pen uncapped and looked at the hand on her binder until Dale took it away.

"The secretary has received written notice from the Highway Department and from the American Legion," Verna said, in the voice she kept for the minutes, "given at the half, which is the one night in a season a team may leave this league without forfeit."

"Your notice is in order, Mr. Hutchins," Ellen said. "The secretary will note both."

"Gil's holding lanes for us in Halsey Falls at half past seven," Dale said, to Verna. "Read them January fifth first."

Verna turned back through the binder a page at a time and found it by the date in the margin. She read it the way she read everything, as though the room had asked.

"Mr. Gagnon states that the owner gave him her word." Her finger went down two lines. "Quote, it stays, unquote. Mr. Gagnon will hold her to it."

Dale looked at Ellen. He did not turn to the room, and he did not raise his voice.

"Ray said it. It's in Verna's book. I'm only reading it back." He put both hands in his coat pockets. "I called you at ten past three the day of the storm and told you it wasn't stopping."

She had the two teams' lineage in her head to the dollar before he finished, and the weeks left in the season, and what his crew spent at her counter on a Tuesday, and she put all of it in sequence and was done with it, and listened for lane six.

At the Legion end of the settees a man laughed once, short, and somebody zipped a bag shut, and somebody else said, "That's right, Dale," low, the way a man says it at a meeting when he means to vote.

She saw Ray get up from six.

She had known where he was since he came in, the way she knew where everyone in her building was and which way each of them was moving, and she watched him come the length of the settees as she would have watched any one thing moving through all the rest. He came the whole way at a walk, and nobody spoke while he did it. Hollis stayed on the settee at six with a ball in both hands. The Legion men turned their heads one after another as Ray went by them, and at the register Denise's hands went still on the keys. He came around the end of her counter, where nobody but staff went, and stood beside her, facing the room, with his shoulder an inch from hers.

The cold of the lot was still in his jacket, and she felt it across the inch. She did not move off it. On the boards an inch would have been a loss of separation, and she would have written it up before morning.

"She said, 'Unless the numbers say otherwise.'" Ray did not raise his voice either, and the room went quiet to hear him. "Said it to me in the pit behind five, the night of the storm, and Denise put a strike down over the top of it, and I never heard the end. That's mine. Put it in the book, Verna."

Verna's pen moved.

On the Highway settee a man said, "Well, if it's Ray's—" and two of the Legion nodded, and the bags came up off the floor, and Dale looked at her.

For one second nobody in the building needed anything from her. She could have stood in it all winter.

"It is not yours."

She said it to him, at an ordinary volume, and the room was quiet enough to take every word. He did not turn his head. Down on three a practice ball came up the return by itself, late, and the whole building heard it arrive.

"In the bob-house you asked me whether I knew you had not heard it. I knew. I watched you miss it."

Verna's pen stopped. At the register Denise's hands lay flat on the keys and stayed there.

"Where I worked, you hear every instruction come back before you let it go. I got no readback. I knew it, and I let it go. Then I let you stand up in this room in January and give them my word."

Ray took the inch back, and then a step, and turned his head to the register.

"Denise."

"I asked her who she said it to," Denise said. "In January. She didn't tell me either."

Ray went around the end of the counter past Denise, and gave her no look going by, and walked back down the settees to six. Hollis held up a ball, and Ray took it, and neither of them said anything.

Dale had picked up nothing. His notice lay in Verna's binder where his hand had left it.

"Then the book's wrong," he said. "We're still going."

The Highway crew went out through the vestibule with their bags, and the Legion went after them, and nobody on the settees said goodnight. The outer door opened on the lot and shut, and the cold came in along the floor behind them and lay around her ankles under the counter.

Denise kept her eyes on the register. "Their lanes stay lit either way, honey," she said, low. "You just lose what they paid."

Ellen did not answer it.

At half past six she pulled the gooseneck down and keyed the PA. The hum came first, the way it always came, and then her voice went out over the building level and warm, with every word in its slot.

"Good evening, Tuesday division. Second half. Bowl when you are ready."

Down at six Ray stood with his back to the counter and the ball in his hand, and he had heard it, as everyone had. On lane one the first ball of the second half left a hand and went rolling down the maple toward the pins.

\* \* \*

Ellen threw the lane toggles off from ten down, one at a time, under the tape where she had printed the numbers on the first of December, and the lanes went dark from the far end of the building toward the counter. Ten went, and nine. Ray stood on the customer side of the counter with his jacket zipped. He had come to it when his team went home, and stood there through the last boxes on two and three and the snack bar's close and the fryer basket hung up to drain, and he had not taken a stool or gone out the back.

The PA was still on from the last call, and the gooseneck's hum was the only sound in the building. Through the glass block a pair of headlights sat low and steady in the lot, where Denise's car was idling. Eight, seven, six. She threw the rest, down to one, and the last lane went out, and the light over the snack bar was what was left of it.

Ray reached across the counter and switched off the PA with one finger. The hum stopped. Behind the snack bar the fryer ticked as it cooled.

"The sign, Mrs. Daly. You knew."

"Yes."

The word went out into the dark lanes, and nothing came back off them. He waited. She took her hand off the panel and stood square at the counter across from him, with her weight even on both feet.

"You will want the rest," she said. "I will give it to you in order."

He gave her nothing to that, and she began.

"First. An Open without Linda's name would have cleared. I put her name on it for room."

The fryer ticked. Nothing moved in his face.

"Second. In the bob-house you asked me straight. I gave you a true sentence because a true sentence would hold."

Under the office the boiler came on, and a pipe along the cellar wall knocked once and was quiet.

"Third. Every error I have made in my life, I wrote up before the shift was over. This one would have cost me you."

Ray waited for a fourth item, as if there might be one. His hands lay flat on the worn front edge of the counter, both of them, still, and in two months she had never once seen them still.

"You sounded fine on the radio that night too, I bet."

Her forearms had come down onto the counter's edge at some point, and the edge was where it had always been, worn round by fifty years of elbows. Her own voice was still in the building, four hours old, Tuesday division, second half, level and warm. Under it was a headset on a midnight shift, and the same voice going out of her into it, steady. Under that was a pin in her two hands in the pit behind five, in the dark in December, cold and heavier than it looked, which she had held the whole time she was telling him. The fryer ticked again, and in the glass block Denise's headlights did not move.

"Understood."

She pulled the legal pad across the counter. The top page was tonight's close in her printing, nine lines, eight of them struck through. She tore it off along the gum and laid it face down, and at the top of the clean page under it she wrote the date, tomorrow's, the third of February, and underlined it once.

Ray put his hand into his jacket pocket and brought out his key ring. It came out heavy and loud in the empty building, every key he carried on it. He found her front key by feel and began to work it off with his thumbnail. The split ring bit, and he did not hurry it; he walked the key round the coil a quarter turn at a time, the big nicked fingers going careful the way they went with every small part in her building, and she watched them all the way round. Arlene's old key stayed on the ring under its orange tag with his initials and the year on it, and had opened nothing here since December. He laid her key on the counter beside the pad, with no tag on it, and said nothing.

He went out by the front. His boots crossed the vestibule, and the outer door opened on the lot and swung shut behind him. In the glass block Denise's headlights held where they were, and then a second pair came on beside them.

She picked up the key. It was warm from his pocket. She turned to the board behind the counter where Arlene had hung keys for fifty years and lifted it to an empty hook, and took it down again. She opened the cash drawer and laid it in the tray, and shut the drawer, and opened it, and took the key back out. She got her own keys out of her vest and opened the split ring with her thumbnail, and stopped with the key at the gap.

She put it in her own vest pocket.
