# Chapter 5: The Vote

"Mom. Scale of one to ten."

"Of what."

"You know of what."

"Six," Ellen said.

"So nine," said Kate.

The phone lay face up on the blotter with the speaker on, and her daughter's voice came out of it from a break room in Manchester with a hospital going on behind it: a cart on a hard floor, a door sighing shut, and now and then a woman overhead saying a name and a number in the flat voice that carried through anything. Through the shut office door five lanes were going under the black lights, half a house of open bowling for glow night, with the league dark for the holiday week, and the adding machine sat quiet at her elbow.

"I'm calling now because at midnight I'll be on the floor," Kate said. "Don't say it, I know it's Thursday. Somebody brought a sheet cake, somebody always brings a sheet cake to a holiday shift, it's yellow with the frosting that's mostly shortening, and somebody spelled Happy with one p. You call when you get your break. That's the rule. I grew up on it."

Ellen had the answer to that, and it was in order. First, holiday shifts went out on bids, and the bids went by seniority. Second, she had not had the seniority to bid her way off a holiday until Kate was in high school, and by then Kate had stopped asking which nights she would be home. Third, she had not made the schedule. She gave none of it.

"How long is your break?"

"Ten minutes. Eight, now."

She looked at her watch, the steel one Ray had covered with his palm on the nineteenth. It was eighteen minutes to twelve. In all her years on the boards she had never once seen a year in on the telephone with her daughter from anywhere but a break room, and tonight she had a quiet office and nowhere to be, and she had wanted Kate on the line when it turned. Kate had eight minutes.

"Did you eat?" Kate said. "Don't say popcorn."

"Fries."

"Fries are a side. You can't have a side of nothing." She was eating something herself; Ellen could hear it. "There's one sheet cake in New Hampshire, and it goes around. This is the one they gave you when they retired you. Same yellow roses."

"Mine were blue."

"Course you know that," Kate said. "You'd have written it up."

Kate went on about who had cut it and how badly, and about a resident who had eaten a corner piece off a tongue depressor because the forks were locked in a cabinet and nobody had the code.

The second chair stood where she had hooked it out from under the desk with her boot on the nineteenth, still facing hers. Ray had sat in it after close on six of the eleven nights since, with his canvas jacket zipped to the chin as though he might be called out any minute, while she ran the tape, and neither of them had said one word about the sign or about January. Her boot found the chair's lower rung and stayed there.

"Mom? You there?"

"Yes."

"Okay." Kate's voice had gone quick, the way it went when the minutes were running down and there was one thing left she meant to get in before they did. "Is anybody there with you tonight?"

Ellen drew a breath. Overhead in Manchester the woman said a number and a word, and Kate said, "That's me," and the line went dead.

\* \* \*

At two minutes to twelve Ellen went through the steel door by lane ten with the paper cup of sparkling cider Denise had put in her hand at ten o'clock, along the catwalk in the dim, and sat down on the iron step behind seven.

Out front Denise had the microphone, and in a minute every face in the building would be lit green and pink by a glow necklace and turned toward the owner, to see where Ray Gagnon was standing when the year went over. She had told no one where she was going, him least of all, and she had come to the one place in the building where he would look first.

Through the gap under the masking the lanes lay bright and backward, the pins standing violet under the black lights, and one caged bulb burned over seven and eight. Seven took its wood in front of her, and the sweep came down and went up again, and she could hear now, after a month of listening, that it had come on time.

"Ten," said Denise on the PA, and the curtain brought it to her half a beat behind the room, so that every number came twice. The house took it up at seven and was shouting by four, and at the last one the whole building went off together, a roar and paper horns and somebody's whistle and, on one of the near lanes, a full rack going down that nobody could have been aiming at.

The steel door opened at the end of the catwalk and shut again on the noise.

She knew the boots on the grating. He was on call tonight, ten to close, his name on her clipboard in her own printing, and he came along the catwalk with one hand on the rail and lowered himself onto the step beside her, and both his knees reported on the way down.

For a whole minute he did not say Happy New Year. She always knew the time, and she counted the minute off second by second while the house roared on through the curtain and his sleeve lay along hers from shoulder to elbow. Happy New Year was what you said to anyone, at a door or across a register, and he was keeping it back, and by the fortieth second she knew he was keeping it on purpose. He looked at the cup in her hands.

"You've carried that since ten o'clock," he said, "round the counter and down the lanes and out to the lot to look at that Subaru with the dead battery, and back in, and you haven't drunk a drop of it."

"I don't like it. It's cider pretending."

She heard *don't* and *it's* go out to him, the short words she had kept from him since the morning under the sign, and let them go, as she had let the first one go at the desk on the nineteenth, and he had heard that one too.

"Denise says it's festive," Ray said.

"Denise said the black lights were festive. Half the town looks like an X-ray."

"Denise asked me where the owner got to," he said, "right before she started counting. I told her the owner was seeing to a machine."

"That's true, as far as it goes."

"Seven's right there," Ray said, "and you're seeing to her."

He took the cup out of her fingers and stood it on the grating by his boot, and gave it a quarter turn so it sat level.

Then he turned toward her, and she felt his fingers close in the short hair at the back of her head, and he waited like that long enough for her to say any of the things she had in order. She said none of them. She met him.

Her hands were bare, and she put them inside his open canvas jacket where it was warm, and under her right palm, through the flannel of his shirt pocket, was folded paper, and she knew by the shape of it that it was the Worcester page. His mouth was cold from the lot and tasted of the urn coffee, and he kissed her as if he had spent six nights in her second chair working out how and had it settled. She leaned into the arm that had gone round her over the vest, and gave it her weight.

She meant to keep the order, and she thought of the cup standing level by his boot, and Tuesday, five days off, and his knees on the way down, and the five words he had never heard, and the house still roaring through the curtain as though somebody out there had won something, and how far she had to look up at him even sitting down, and Tuesday again, and she heard that Tuesday had come round twice and nothing was in its place, and she did not go back for any of it.

He drew back an inch and no farther, with his hand still in her hair.

"Happy New Year, Ellen," he said, low.

"It's four minutes past."

"I know what time it is."

\* \* \*

The folding chairs had come up from the cellar that afternoon and still smelled of it, and by six they stood in rows on the dark approaches facing the counter, every coat in the building steaming dry on its owner. Twenty captains sat in front of their teams, and Verna Tibbetts sat under the gooseneck lamp at the lane-one scorer's table with the binder and the Grange gavel.

In the front row sat the old couple from the one-and-two settee, first down the drive on the eighteenth with the lot empty at seven forty-three, who would put up their team's hand tonight with the rest.

Ray was in the Tuesday rows, four back, with Hollis beside him. She knew where he was before she turned her head, and she turned it.

Verna read the bylaws aloud in full, dues and nights and lob line, in a voice that did not hurry for anybody. "The lineage and the house's contribution to the prize fund shall hold at the rates of the September agreement until the January meeting has voted upon them. Adopted January, two thousand five, on the motion of R. Gagnon."

"The house shall set the lanes and the hours of championship weekend," Verna read, "and the league shall bowl its championship within them."

Ellen filed it: whoever held the building held that Saturday. Beside Ray, Hollis wrote something in his waterproof notebook with the golf pencil, and nobody else in the room wrote anything at all.

"The owner may speak for three minutes," Verna said. "I have a watch. So do you, I see."

Ellen stood at the end of the counter. "Thank you. Three items. First, the league's rate has not moved since two thousand four. Second, the house has paid the difference every week since, and the house is now me. Third, if this meeting approves it, the new rate takes effect on the twelfth. That is all."

Her voice went out over the chairs level and courteous, and under it she was counting Verna's second hand. She gave the two minutes and thirty-four seconds she had left back to the chair, and stayed on her feet at the end of the counter. In the front row the captain uncrossed his arms and crossed them the other way.

Verna looked over the binder. "The chair recognizes the gentleman with mustard on his sleeve."

A Legion man looked down at his sleeve, and then stood up, since he had been recognized. "If we vote her down, what happens to the Memorial? Derby's that weekend. She going to rent our Saturday to the fish people?"

Ray stood. His hands had nothing to do, so his thumbnail found a screw backed out on the chair in front of him and worked at it, and Ellen had seen him do that hardest when the talk was about himself.

"Most of you heard about my mail before I did, so here it is," he said. "The credit union would've lent me against the store to buy this place off her clean, and I let their five o'clock go by on the third of December without making the call, and the other loan's with the bank in Concord, to buy it March first if she can't pay Arlene, and I paid them to hold it. So weigh what I say next by that."

"Dale's crew pushes snow twelve hours a night from now till mud season, and half the Legion's on a fixed check, and they can't pay it. This is the league Linda bowled."

The screw went home under his thumbnail.

"And Mrs. Daly gave me her word, the night of the storm, in the pit behind five, that Linda's Memorial stays the way it is." He turned his head and looked at her, for the first time that night. "'It stays,' she said. That book of yours lets the house set that weekend any way it likes, so her word is what the Memorial's got, and I'll hold her to it. Put it in the book, Verna."

His voice was low enough that the back rows leaned forward to hear it, and he did not raise it for them. He had called her by her last name in front of the league, five days after he had said her first name with his hand in her hair, and she heard the formality and what it cost him to put it back on. She wanted him to keep talking. She wanted it even now, with every sentence of it against her, and she stood at the end of the counter and listened to her own two words in his mouth, and the other five nowhere in it, and kept back the one error of her life she would not report, because it was priced in him.

Verna wrote, taking her time, and then gave it to the room in the voice she kept for the minutes. "Mr. Gagnon states that the owner gave him her word, on the night of the storm, in the pit behind five, that the Linda Gagnon Memorial stays the way it is. Quote, it stays, unquote. Mr. Gagnon will hold her to it."

It was all there, and no more of it than he had heard.

"Does the owner wish to respond?"

Ellen sat down on the stool and put her hands on her knees. She had the five words, and the floor, and the time she had given back.

"No."

"The owner declines," said Verna, and wrote that down too.

"The vote is by team." Ellen stood to take it, and Verna called the teams in the order of the binder and counted the hands twice, and then wrote a slip of her own and folded it into the binder without adding it to either column.

"Opposed, eighteen. In favor, two. The September rate holds."

Ray had said mostly. It had not been close.

The room broke up toward the shoe cubbies, and the captain came along the counter and laid his crew's lineage by the register.

"Ray says you gave your word," he said. "That'll do for my crew."

She rang it.

She went to the panel and threw the ten toggles herself, under her own tape, for the league that had just voted her down at the rate it had voted to keep, and the lanes came up the length of the building. On six Ray hung his jacket over the end of the settee and did not look toward the counter, and the first ball of the night went down six.

\* \* \*

At a quarter to seven Ellen stood at the adding machine with the office door shut on the Tuesday league, and all ten lanes went on behind it, muffled, the sound she liked best in the building, every rack of it bowled at a rate that lost her money. The lineage notice still lay face down under the tape where she had laid it on the nineteenth.

Denise came in without knocking, with a paper boat of fries, and set it down on the back of the notice and sat in the second chair. It was Ray's chair, and Denise sat in it with her elbows out, the way she sat on a stool at the counter.

"You're standing," Denise said. "What happened?"

Ellen took a fry. It was too hot to hold, and she held it, and the malt vinegar went up the back of her nose, and she ate it standing at the desk and reached for another before she had swallowed. The paper had gone clear in patches where the vinegar soaked through, and Denise had salted them twice. For a little while there was nothing in the office but the heater ticking and the lanes through the door and the fries.

She wanted it said in this room, to Denise, before it was said anywhere a hand could go up against it.

"At the September rate the winter comes in at twenty-six thousand," Ellen said. "The bank's number is thirty. I am four thousand short."

"I can subtract, I was in the third row, I watched eighteen hands go up and half of them belong to men who carry a ninety average and think their lineage pays for the lights," Denise said, "so I've got the number, and you're still standing, so there's a second half, and I'd like it before these get cold."

"An Open," Ellen said. "Championship weekend, its own cup, anyone who pays may enter. The derby brings the whole lake to the town dock that weekend, and none of them has anything to do after the weigh-in. Without a name on it, that closes it with nothing over. Put Linda's name on it and the town comes out for Linda, and I have room for one bad thing."

Denise put down the fry she was holding. "Don't you touch Linda's night."

"Unless the numbers say otherwise."

Denise's head came up. "Who'd you say that to?"

Ellen ate a fry. Through the door a ball came rumbling up the return on one of the far lanes, and Denise waited until it was over, and did not ask again.

"What about your own money?"

"Not enough to matter. And I swore to Kate."

"So it's a cushion."

"It is separation."

Ellen sat down in her own chair, across the desk from Denise in Ray's, and Denise watched her do it and picked her fry back up.

"Now you're sitting," Denise said.

An Open without Linda's name would clear the bank's number by a little, if nothing went wrong between now and the last Saturday in February; she had run it both ways on the adding machine before Denise came in. She was taking Linda's name for the margin.

Ten minutes later she sat in her car in the lot with the engine running and the heater still blowing cold, and down at the road the sign was lit, red on the snow. The lot had been plowed to the gravel that afternoon, and the banks stood higher than the roof of the car. Through the glass block the league moved in shapes, a swing and a turn and somebody walking back to the settees. She called Hollis, and he picked up on the second ring.

"How thick is the ice off the public access?"

"Why?"

"I'm walking out to your bob-house Saturday."

## Commitments

- **Ray let the credit union's deadline pass on purpose.** He tells the whole league on Jan 5 that the credit union would have lent against the store and he let their five o'clock on Dec 3 go by "without making the call." This is now public. Danny's signed form (bible) stays unmentioned. Later chapters may have townspeople know Ray chose not to put the store up.
- **The two loans are named on the record.** The credit union is the store loan, now lapsed. The bank in Concord is the purchase loan for March 1, which Ray paid to hold. Ch 07 ("I keep it") and Ch 08 (released; lapses Feb 17) must match.
- **Verna's slip.** On Jan 5 she wrote a slip of her own and folded it into the binder, outside the 18–2 team count. Its contents are not shown. Ellen saw it happen. If Ch 08 reveals Verna voted yes, it should be this slip, and it was not part of the count.
- **The league is dark the week of New Year's.** Thursday Dec 31 was open-bowling glow night with no league. The bible calendar may want this noted.
- **Ray sat in the second chair on six of the eleven nights from Dec 20 to Dec 30.** There was no kiss between Dec 18 and Dec 31.
- **Ellen's time.** Her three-minute allowance left 2:34 unused. Anything in Ch 07 or Ch 08 that recalls the meeting ("she had the floor and gave back the time") should not use "forty seconds."
- **Ellen sat for "No."** She was seen sitting on the stool, hands on her knees, when she declined to respond. That is her decision posture, in front of the room. Ray, Denise and the captain may recall it.
