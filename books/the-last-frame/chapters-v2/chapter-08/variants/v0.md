# Chapter 8: Position Relief

Gil Arsenault, in a good wool overcoat, held the house ball out across the settee on the flat of his hand, with the bowling glove he had no use for done up at the wrist.

"House rules, Ray. Three strings, total pins, my house balls. You'll forgive the house balls; they're older than you."

Ray took it. It sat lighter in his hand than his own ever had. "Then I'll treat her with respect, Gil," he said. "Same as I'd want."

Pine Ridge was warm clear to the back wall, because somebody there could afford to heat it, and Ray resented liking it. Sixteen lanes ran off under the scoring screens and music he didn't know, over a carpet printed with planets.

Gil gave the terms as referee. "Ray takes it, the Highway Department and the Legion go home to Kettle Lanes Tuesday at the season rate, and they bowl Linda's Memorial. My man takes it, Ray pays their lineage here the rest of the season and bowls for Pine Ridge."

Dale Hutchins sat at the end of the settee with his coat zipped and his bag between his boots. Behind him the Highway crew stood along the rail in their coats, and at the far end of it Hollis stood with his notebook in his pocket and said nothing. The Legion's captain sat one seat down from Dale and studied his laces.

"And Linda's cup?" Dale said.

"You won't pay for it twice," Ray said. "That one's mine."

Dale looked at him a while. "Bowl, then."

Denise was at the scorer's table before Ray got there, with a Kettle Lanes score sheet flat in front of her and a pencil out of the cigar box on Ellen's counter. Nobody said what she was doing there, or a word about Tuesday. Ray took out his pocketknife and held out his free hand, and she put the pencil in it. He brought it to a point in six long strokes, the shavings curling down onto the planets, and gave it back to her blunt end first.

Ray started badly. The house ball rode high off his hand and a little late, like a truck with nothing in the bed, and his first ball took three.

"Holy mackerel," Ray said, and one of the Highway crew laughed and quit.

Gil's anchor was a lineman for the power company, about half Ray's age, and he bowled clean and quick and didn't talk. In the second string Gil called a foul on his own man's toe, quietly, on a line only Gil had been watching. The anchor turned and looked at him. Gil kept his eyes on Denise's sheet. Ray was still behind going into the third.

Between strings Gil stopped at Denise's elbow.

"He's a hundred-and-four average with a hundred-and-forty heart," Denise said, loud enough to carry. "Don't tell him I said either."

"Mrs. Lavoie." Gil took his time with it. "I watched you pick up a four-horse with your third ball in twenty fifteen. I still think about it."

Denise's pencil stopped over the sheet for about a second. Then it went on.

In the tenth box of the third string Ray needed every pin on the deck. His first ball took the front of the rack, and one pin went over sideways and lay across the deck in front of three still standing on the left. His second, thrown too hard, took one of the three and left the other two side by side at the back, with the fallen pin lying out in front of them like a plank across a ditch.

Then he had the third ball in his hand, small and heavier than it looked, cold from the rack and worn smooth by other men's palms, and the song in the ceiling changed to another one he didn't know. The lane ran out longer than it ever did at home, sixty feet of somebody else's maple with no winter of his in it, and he had to trust it anyway. He picked the near end of the pin lying down and set his eyes there and kept them there, and he let the ball go off his hand easy, and it went where he sent it.

The lying pin came up off the deck and turned end over end into the two at the back, and all three went over together with a sound like a cupboard coming down, and then the deck lay bare under the lights with nothing on it at all. Ray stood at the line with his hand still out. It took him a second to bring it home.

Denise's pencil was moving before the last pin lay down. Gil keyed his wireless mic, and two words came out of the ceiling over all sixteen lanes, under the music.

"Kettle Lanes."

Dale stood up. "Tuesday, then." He picked up his bag.

The lot was cold enough to ache in his teeth. Hollis was waiting at Ray's truck with frost on the shoulders of his coat.

"Verna read it to the Thursday division last night. Every word. Nobody said one back," Hollis said. "You read it yet?"

"You sat on that rail three strings without a word, Hollis, and I beat a lineman with a ball older than I am, and now you want to talk about reading."

"You read it yet?"

"No."

Hollis opened his waterproof notebook and took a sheet from inside the back cover, folded in three, and held it out. At the top, a line in her printing sent a copy to the derby committee.

Ray got in and turned on the dome light and read it himself. Most of it he had heard at her counter with the lanes going dark. Two lines he hadn't.

*The silence and the name are mine. I ask the secretary to strike "That's mine" from the minutes.*

He knew the hand. He had watched it write his answer down at his own counter in December. At the foot of the page, under her name, she had dated it. The third of February.

\* \* \*

Arlene's hands set a plate of ham in front of him, with a boiled potato split down the middle and a spoon of her mustard on the rim, and went back to the oven before he had his coat over the chair. She hadn't asked whether he was hungry. She never had.

The ham was hot through and sweet at the edges where the glaze had caught, and the potato steamed when he broke it open with the fork, and for a while the kitchen did the talking. The radiator under the window knocked twice and settled. Wintergreen and cough drops came up through the floorboards from the pharmacy, under the ham, the way they did every time he sat at this table.

Boxes stood three high along the wall, each marked for The Pines in the round green-ledger hand she had kept the alley's books in, and at his elbow fifty years of photographs lay face down in a stack, ready to go. The police scanner muttered on the counter by the bread box.

"Why'd you sell it to her, Arlene?"

She sat down across from him with a plate that had half of what his had on it.

"You'd have kept it a museum, Raymond. I wanted it to live."

He ate. He had been angry about that since the fall, and it was the right answer, and he didn't argue it.

The scanner said a car was off the road by the town beach, one unit responding.

"That'll be the Pelletier girl," Arlene said. "She takes that corner like it owes her money."

Ray put the fork down. "I want off the March paper. I'll have your lawyer draw up whatever it takes, and I'll carry it round to her myself."

"Do you know what you're asking me?"

He picked the fork up again and turned it over and laid it square beside the plate. "Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain, because I'd rather hear it from you than work it out by myself in the truck."

She was up before he had finished. She went to a box by the window that had no lid on it yet and came back with a folder, and out of the folder she took her copy of the contract and laid it flat on the oilcloth beside his plate, turned round to face him.

He put his hand on it. Three pages stapled at the corner, and on the last one Ellen's signature sat on its line in her printing, every letter standing separate, the same as the numbers she had printed on the lane tape. She had signed it the morning of the first of December, at the closing, six hours before he knocked on her door, and that night she had stood in the doorway in the snow and told him who would be paying Arlene. *By me.*

His own name was under hers, in the scrawl he put on invoices. He had watched that hand press a strip of tape flat and finish it before she came down off the ladder, the first time he ever saw her, and he had liked it then. He liked it now, on Arlene's oilcloth, with her nowhere in the room.

Arlene folded her hands on the table and gave it to him as flat as an oil bill.

"If she misses and you aren't standing there, nobody buys it on the first of March. The Pines gives my room to the next name on the list, and I go on the bottom of that list at eighty-four, and I may not live to the top of it. I'm paid months on, when my lawyer's done selling it out from under her. And that condo man buys the lot for what the dirt is worth."

"I know it."

"Then what are you doing in my kitchen?"

The cupboard door over her sink had dropped on its hinge since he was last up here, an eighth of an inch at the top corner, and he could see the gap from where he sat, and he looked at that while he said it.

"Every clutch I put in over there might be going into my own machine come the second of March, and she knows it, and so do I, and I'm done standing at the bottom of her drive."

Arlene reached across and turned the contract face down on the oilcloth, beside the photographs.

"In nineteen seventy-one I held Normand to a building I wanted and he didn't. He gave it eighteen years and never once said so. I won't hold you to one you've stopped wanting, Raymond."

"Then you'll sign it."

"Voyons donc. Bring me her signature too. It's hers to risk as much as mine."

\* \* \*

Ten to six by the clock over the key cutter, and the store was dark except for the light over the counter, and Ellen was outside the door glass with her hands bare at her sides. Ray had the drawer out and half of Danny's count laid across his palm. He had never in his life turned that bolt before six. He put the bills down and went and turned it. Behind him the store smelled of kerosene and cut brass, as it had every morning of his life.

She came in with the cold on her, the backs of her hands red to the wrist, and the bell went once over her head. At the counter she stopped. He went back around and went on with the count, laying the bills down on the counter one over the next.

"Dale's crew and the Legion bowl in my building tomorrow night," she said. "Denise told me on Friday."

He stopped counting. The bills lay fanned on the counter where they were.

His gloves were by the drawer, where he had pulled them off to count. His hand went to them. He squared them one on the other, cuff to cuff, and left them lying on his side of the counter.

She let the store be quiet, and the clock over the key cutter used up most of the ten minutes without help from anybody.

"I came here after I knew it."

Behind her the clock went to six. The bell went again, and the Bench came in out of the dark stamping snow onto the mat, three of them in a row, and stopped when they saw who it was. One of them got as far as "And the—" and shut his mouth.

She did not turn around. "You have no reason to believe I would have come anyway, and I have no way to give you one."

Ray said nothing. The Bench stood inside the door in their coats with the cold coming off them and nothing to call. The pot he had put on at half past five stood on the plate by their stools, full, and still too hot to drink.

The man on the end stool went past her to it with his coat still on. He poured a cup, black, and looked at it, and held it out to her across the end of the counter.

"Coffee's on."

Her bare hand went out for it.

\* \* \*

Ray rapped twice on the front door, and the glass rattled in its frame. The door came open before he could give it the third. She was already on the other side of it.

She stood back against the corkboard to let him by. The vestibule smelled of wet wool and road salt off the Tuesday division's boots, and the lot behind him was empty. He had been through this door with his toolbox once since the second, the night four's sweep hung up, and had left the invoice with Denise and gone out the way he came, like a customer.

"I called Laconia at noon," he said. "They're bending you a K."

She didn't answer that, and neither of them said anything about coming in.

The counter was to the left with the one light on over it, and she went to it. Ray went right, past the dark snack bar, to the trophy case.

Locker fourteen hung on the wall beside the case, gray steel gone dull, with a brass padlock through the hasp. He took out his key ring, and the clank of it went off down the dark lanes, and Arlene's old key swung under its orange tag, and his thumb found the gap on the coil where Ellen's front key had been before he meant it to. The locker key was the smallest one he carried, a stubby brass thing, and he picked it out by feel with his eyes on the lock.

Linda's ball had been behind that door since the week she died, on the shelf where she had set it down herself. He fit the key into the padlock. It went in stiff, the whole length of it, and the tumblers took it, and he stood with the brass cold between his finger and thumb and his other hand flat on the steel door. He did not turn it.

He drew the key out and put the ring back in his jacket. Ellen watched from behind the counter and said nothing. When he came across to her she was standing with both hands on the worn front edge of it, and he stood across from her, and neither of them sat.

"What I said about the radio," Ray said. "I picked it because I knew it'd land."

"It did."

He let that stand. "You stood at my counter and said I had no reason to believe you'd have come anyway, and Hollis had already shown me what you wrote Verna, and it's dated the third."

Her right hand lay flat on the counter. The first finger lifted, and then the second, and they went back down onto the wood with nothing put in any order, and stayed there.

"Here's what I came to ask you," Ray said, "and you'll want it short."

"Affirmative."

"You'll get it my way. Linda's name comes off your Open, and the league bowls its own Memorial at two o'clock on the last Saturday, on all ten lanes, the way it always has, and your Open gets the morning and the evening and a cup of its own, with whatever name you like on that one." He looked at her hands. "I told Dale at Pine Ridge his crew wouldn't pay for Linda's cup twice. I said that one was mine."

She looked at him for a while. Then she went into the office and brought the rotary phone out on its long cord, and set it on the counter between them and dialed from memory. When Hollis picked up, a plow's engine came out of the earpiece loud enough to cross the counter, with his voice under it.

"Hollis. This is Ellen Daly. Did the derby committee vote on Linda's name?"

"We voted on an Open. Nobody voted on a name." The engine geared down. "Mornings and evenings suit our people. Afternoons they're on the ice."

"Thank you, Hollis."

She hung up.

"Agreed."

She put both hands back on the counter. "Nine teams wrote Linda's name on their entry form. In the morning I will call all nine and offer their money back, and all nine will take it. After that I have no room at all."

"That was my room," she said.

Ray's hand went inside his jacket, past the envelope he had carried there blank since the first of the month, and left it where it was, and came out with a single folded page. Arlene's lawyer had taken a week and a half to get it onto one page, and all it said was that the three of them let one another go from the March first sale. He unfolded it on the counter and turned it to face her. Arlene had signed first, to stand only if the third name followed, and his own name was under hers, and a line left.

"I'm asking you to sign it."

She put on the reading glasses that hung from the black cord at her neck and read it through once.

"You have asked an eighty-four-year-old woman to bet her bed on me."

"I know what I asked."

She kept her eyes on the page, and he didn't help her. The heat came up in the building, and over by lane one the trophy case ticked as it warmed.

"If I miss, Arlene loses her place, and it will be my name that put it there." She took a pen out of her vest and uncapped it. "If I ever ask you to stand beside me, I won't have you standing at the bottom of my drive as well."

She signed. Ray watched her hand do it, slow, the same hand that had signed Arlene's copy on the first of December, and the pen scratched on the paper, small and dry, in a building with no other noise in it. Where the counter was worn hollow at its front edge the pen skipped, and she went back over the gap and closed it.

"I'll call Concord at nine and let it go," Ray said. "They'd want ninety days to write it again."

"There are thirteen."

She put two fingers into her vest pocket and brought out his key, the one he had worked off his ring at this counter two weeks ago, and held it across to him on her open palm.

"Not yet," Ray said.

He went out through the inner door and across the vestibule, over the old cigarette burn in the linoleum, and at the outer door he looked back through the glass. She was still holding out the key.
