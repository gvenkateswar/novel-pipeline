1. **Winner:** A

2. **Why:** A keeps the small dry lines and physical detail that make people sound like themselves (Hollis asking "in exactly the same voice," "She had them counted before he was done," the signing as "steady as a good sweep setting down on its stop"), while B explains a few of its own beats after they have already landed.

3. **Where A lost me:**
   - About 5%: "He gave the terms short, for the settee. If Ray took it, the Highway Department and the Legion went home to Kettle Lanes on Tuesday at the season rate and bowled Linda's Memorial. If Gil's man took it, Ray paid their lineage here..." This is a stake recital in summary, the couple-doing-business register of A1. It is short, but my thumb moved. B's version of the same information has Dale cutting in, so it doesn't happen there.
   - About 25%: the tenth-box paragraph, "...and set his eyes there and kept them there and let the ball go easy, so easy somebody on the rail groaned, and it rolled the length of Gil's lane at a speed a child could have walked beside..." The one breathless sentence is a good idea, matching the slow ball. But it runs long enough that I was waiting for it to end, not for the pin. I'd call it a near miss, not a stop.

4. **Where B lost me:**
   - About 75%: "He had picked it because it would land. He had heard it land in this building, and he had driven home and sat in his dooryard with the heater running and the radio off and heard it go on landing." Ellen's line "I have heard it every time I have keyed that microphone since" had already done this. B says it a second time, in Ray's head.
   - About 88%: "He had not got as far as counting them, and she had. Whatever happened to her number now, he could not buy her building if she put the keys in his hand." The first sentence is fine. The second is the narrator doing the plot math for me, right where A just trusts "She had them counted before he was done."
   - Smaller: "A hundred and fifteen dollars was a kids' birthday party on a Saturday with the fryer going, or most of one..." This is a paragraph of Ray priced out loud before the fries joke.

5. **Confidence:** Medium. It's close. B is cleaner in the opening, where Dale interrupts Gil ("as though nobody had spoken") and the bowling is split into readable paragraphs. B's "In fries." and Ray laughing out loud is also a better laugh than A's flatter "The snack bar sells fries in paper boats." A wins on the back half, where A trusts the page and B explains it. A's single-sentence climax could flip some readers.

6. **Same-author check:** Yes. They share the same beats, nearly identical dialogue (the radio apology, "Not yet."), and the same habits (squaring objects, hands on counters, "the man from the end stool"). They are two revisions of one draft.
