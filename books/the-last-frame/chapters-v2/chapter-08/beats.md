## The safer option, and how it fails on the page

**The safer option:** leave the paper alone. Ray holds the backup sale to the end, Arlene keeps her bed either way, and if Ellen makes her number he tears it up afterward.

**How the page shows it failing, in order, before anyone asks to tear it up:**

1. **His word (s1).** Dale won't take Ray's word about her while Ray is the buyer: "Last time I took your word about her, the book was wrong. And it still says you paid a bank to buy her out if she's short."
2. **His bet (s1).** Ray puts up the stake Dale can't refuse, and Dale turns it over: "Everything you put up, losing pays you." Ray has to win on the lane, in public, because his word counts for nothing.
3. **What the Bench bets (s1).** The end stool had a dollar against him: "Nothing personal, Ray. Man that's buying the place come March don't bowl hard to fill it."
4. **His league night (s1 → s2 → s3).** He won her teams back and can't stand in the room when they come home, because then it is the buyer bringing his league back. He tells Hollis to anchor ("Where'll you be?"). Ellen tells him Dale asked where he was. Arlene saw his truck idling at the bottom of Main all night.
5. **What she can accept from him (s2).** Her hands are red from the cold. His gloves are on the counter, and she has taken nothing from him since the hood that wasn't on an invoice. He squares them and keeps them.
6. **Every call he takes (s3, named).** "Every clutch I put in over there might be going into my own machine come March."

**Named and answered:**

- **s3. Arlene names it:** "Then hold it." Ray's answer is the clutch line and "I'm done standing at the bottom of her drive." Arlene's own reason comes from her window: two people sitting in cars because of a paper she made them sign.
- **s4. Ellen names it:** "You could keep it in your jacket until I have made the number, and tear it up then." Ray answers with the mirror of her own line from the hood: "I'd be fixing your machines for one of us all month, and I couldn't tell you which." Her reason: she is a hundred and fifteen dollars under, she may have to ask him to stand beside her before this is done, and she won't ask the man who wins if she misses.

The cost is said out loud before she signs: "You have asked an eighty-four-year-old woman to bet her bed on a hundred and fifteen dollars."

### Scene 1

**Setting:** Pine Ridge Lanes, Halsey Falls, Friday evening. Pop music, overhead screens nobody in this match looks at, the planet carpet. **Sensory anchor:** the warmth of a building somebody can afford to heat, which Ray resents enjoying (one clause). Then the lot, cold enough to ache in the teeth.

**Characters:** Ray, Gil Arsenault, Dale Hutchins, Denise, Hollis, the man from the Bench's end stool (unnamed), Gil's anchor (a lineman half Ray's age, no lines), the Legion's captain (silent), the Highway crew along the rail.

**Want:**
- **Ray** wants Ellen's two teams back on her lanes before Linda's night, and he wants it done without her asking. In the way: Gil's anchor, a house ball, his own first string, and the fact that he is the man buying her building if she's short. The reader learns his want at Gil's terms. The obstacle arrives at Dale's "Everything you put up, losing pays you."
- **Dale** wants it settled before Linda's night, and he'll take Ray's word if Ray carries the risk. The reader learns both from his own lines.
- **Gil** likes a match, and he has the Tuesday division in his lounge either way.

**Surface action, in order:**
1. **Open on Gil's hand**, in the good wool overcoat with the glove he has no need for, holding out a house ball. **"House rules, Ray. Three strings, total pins, my house balls. You'll forgive the house balls; they're older than you."** Ray takes it; it sits lighter in his hand than his own.
2. **Who set it up, and why Gil risks it (Gil, one line):** **"You called me for a match, and I like a match, and half your Tuesday division drove over to watch it and they're in my lounge eating pizza, so I've had my night whoever bowls."**
3. **The terms (Gil as referee, short).** If Ray takes it, the Highway Department and the Legion go home to Kettle Lanes on Tuesday at the season rate and bowl Linda's Memorial. If Gil's man takes it, Ray pays their lineage here to the end of the season, and Gagnon & Son bowls for Pine Ridge.
4. **Why Dale lets one game decide, and what the paper costs (Dale on the settee, coat zipped, bag between his boots):** **"I want it settled before Linda's night."** Then: **"Last time I took your word about her, the book was wrong. And it still says you paid a bank to buy her out if she's short."** He looks at the stake Ray put up. **"Everything you put up, losing pays you."** Ray: **"Then watch me bowl."**
5. **Dale: "And Linda's cup? My crew paid for it in September."** Ray: **"You won't pay for it twice, Dale. I'll ask her to give the league its Memorial back."** Dale looks at him a while. **"Bowl, then."**
6. **Denise is already at the scorer's table** with a Kettle Lanes paper sheet and a pencil out of Ellen's cigar box. Nobody says a word about Tuesday. **Gil accepts her sheet over his own screens. That is his fairness, and the Book 2 seed, doing a job in one line:** **"I'll take Mrs. Lavoie's pencil over my screens. I watched her pick up a four-horse with her third ball eleven years back, in the spring doubles. I still think about it."** Ray's clause: a leave he had seen her make twice in thirty years. Her pencil stops over the sheet for a second, then goes on. Ray takes out his pocketknife, holds out his free hand, sharpens her pencil to a point, and gives it back blunt end first. That is the whole mend.
7. **Ray starts badly.** The house ball rides wrong, and his first ball takes three. **"Holy mackerel."** One of the Highway crew laughs and quits. The first two strings pass in **two sentences at most**, both about the lineman bowling clean and quick.
8. **The Bench bet, before the third string.** The man from the end stool, on the rail in his coat: **"Had a dollar on your man here, Ray. Nothing personal. Man that's buying the place come March don't bowl hard to fill it."** Denise, loud enough to carry, to nobody: **"He's a hundred-and-four average with a hundred-and-forty heart. Don't tell him I said either."**
9. **Interior passage 1: the tenth.** Gil's man has finished. **Ray is nine pins behind with ten on the deck**, so he needs every pin.
   - First ball: the front of the rack goes. One pin goes over sideways and lies across the deck in front of three still standing, like a plank across a ditch. Put the image **here, on the first ball, so the listener can see it from the start**.
   - Second ball: thrown too hard, it takes one of the three and leaves two at the back, with the plank still in front of them.
   - Third ball: small, heavier than it looks, and worn by other men's palms. Sixty feet of somebody else's maple. He sets his eyes on the near end of the lying pin and lets the ball go easy.
   - The lying pin comes up off the deck end over end into the two at the back, and all three go over together with a sound like a cupboard coming down.
   - Long sentences, landing on a short one. **Nobody names a rule, and neither does the narration.** No memory of the Ch 04 lesson, and no Ellen in his head.
10. Denise's pencil is moving before the last pin lies down. Gil keys his wireless mic, and two words come out of the ceiling: **"Kettle Lanes."** Dale stands. **"Tuesday, then."** He picks up his bag.
11. **The lot.** The end stool is at Ray's truck ahead of him, and he puts a folded dollar in Hollis's glove without a word. Hollis puts it in his waterproof notebook.
12. Hollis: **"She wrote the league a letter. Verna read it out to the Thursday division, every word, and copied it in the book, and gave me the page for the committee."** Then: **"You read it yet?"** Ray: "No." Hollis: **"You read it yet?"** Ray holds out his hand.
13. **The letter in his hands (orchestrator item 6, one line, Ray's side):** it is a yellow sheet off her legal pad with the gum still along its top edge. At the head of it, underlined, is the date he watched her write while he stood at her counter working her key off his ring: **the third of February.** One clause of longing that isn't about the plot: her printing, every letter standing separate.
14. **Quote only what is new. No nested quotation (listener pass).** Model:
    > *The silence was mine, and so was the name. Mr. Gagnon took the fault for it on himself in this room, and I ask the secretary to strike that from the minutes.*
15. He folds it in three and puts it inside his jacket.
16. **Ray: "You anchor Tuesday."** Hollis: **"Where'll you be?"** End.

**Subtext:** He wins her teams back and finds that winning proves nothing while his name is on the March paper. Dale won't take his word, and his own Bench bet against him. Then he finds she had already put her wrong in the town's book in her own hand, dated the night he left. Her side of the book is right. His isn't.

**Texture budget:** Two. The side-match terms and stake: these show the paper's cost, because losing would pay him. The tenth won on the wood: Ray playing what's left, unspoken.

**Point of view:** Ray. Comic and dry in the room, slow and physical in the tenth, and plain in the lot.

**Ends on:** **Question.** "Where'll you be?" Unanswered. The truck isn't started and there's no reaction beat.

### Scene 3

**Setting:** Arlene's apartment above Ambrose Pharmacy, Saturday noon. Up the steep side stair. **Sensory anchor:** wintergreen and cough drops rising through the floorboards under the smell of the ham. The police scanner is low on the counter, and **the window looks down Main Street to the dock, to Gagnon & Son at the bottom of it.** One clause for the boxes marked for The Pines.

**Characters:** Ray, Arlene.

**Want:**
- **Ray** wants off the March paper, so he stops being the man waiting at the bottom of Ellen's drive, and he wants Arlene to say yes without making him ask Ellen. In the way are Arlene's bed and the safer option, which she names. The reader learns his want at "I want off the March paper."
- **Arlene** wants to be paid on the day, to the dollar, and the alley alive after her. She wants Raymond to hear exactly what he's asking, and she wants the two people her paper has kept apart out of their cars.

**Surface action, in order:**
1. **Open on the plate set down in front of him:** ham, a split boiled potato, a spoon of her mustard on the rim, put there before his coat is over the chair. She doesn't ask if he's hungry. **Two sentences of pleasure, at most.**
2. **Ray: "Why'd you sell it to her, Arlene?"** **Arlene: "You'd have kept it a museum, Raymond. I wanted it to live."** He eats and doesn't argue it. No gloss.
3. **The scanner breaks the silence (the laugh):** a car off the road by the town beach. **"That'll be the Pelletier girl. She takes that corner like it owes her money."**
4. **Ray: "I want off the March paper. I'll have your lawyer draw whatever it takes."** **Arlene: "Do you know what you're asking me?"** **Ray (bible Ray 8, verbatim): "Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain, because I'd rather hear it from you than work it out by myself in the truck."**
5. She goes to a box with no lid yet and lays her copy of the contract on the oilcloth beside his plate. **One clause only (orchestrator item 5):** three pages stapled at the corner, with Ellen's printing on the last one and his scrawl under it. **No closing, no "By me," no tape, no first sight of her.**
6. **Arlene's cost, plain and short (texture, one use):** **"If she misses and you aren't standing there, nobody buys it. The Pines gives my room to the next name, and I go on the bottom of that list at eighty-four, and I may not live to the top of it. I'm paid months on, when my lawyer's done selling it out from under her, and that condo man gets the lot for what the dirt is worth."** Ray: "I know it."
7. **Arlene names the safer option:** **"Then hold it. You keep it in your drawer, she makes her number, you tear it up after, and I've had my bed the whole time."**
8. **Ray answers it.** Afraid register: the fork goes down and square beside the plate. He looks at the gap in the cupboard door over her sink while he says it, one clause. **"Every clutch I put in over there might be going into my own machine come March, and she knows it, and Dale Hutchins knows it, and I'm done standing at the bottom of her drive."** No further callback to s1 than "Dale Hutchins knows it".
9. **Arlene turns the contract face down. Her own reason, from her window (orchestrator item 1):** **"Tuesday night your truck sat outside your own store from supper till the league let out, with the engine running. I watched it from that window. Thirty years I've known where you were on a Tuesday night. And the next morning her car was at your door before your light was. That paper's mine. I made the pair of you sign it so I'd be sure of my bed, and I've been sure of it all winter, and the two of you have been sitting in cars."** At most 75 words. This is her one long speech. He doesn't answer it.
10. **Then Normand:** **"In nineteen seventy-one I held Normand to a building I wanted and he didn't. He gave it eighteen years and never once said so. I won't hold you to one you've stopped wanting, Raymond."**
11. Ray: **"Then you'll sign it."** Arlene: **"When she has. It's hers to risk as much as mine."** He came for a paper he could carry round finished, and now he has to ask Ellen to her face. That is the reversal.
12. **Consequence, in action.** Arlene gets up, takes the phone off the wall by the bread box, and dials her lawyer from memory. One line to identify herself. Then the last line: **"Draw it so I sign first, and Raymond under me, and leave a line for Ellen Daly. He'll carry it to her himself, and he'll ask her."** End on that.

**Subtext:** He asks an old woman to bet her last home so that one day he can stand beside a woman he isn't speaking to without owning a claim on her. Arlene gives it to him for her own reasons, and they are better than his. She made the paper, and she has watched it put both of them in parked cars. Then she arranges for him to do the one thing he came to avoid, which is ask.

**Texture budget:** One: what a miss means for Arlene (no sale, The Pines list, paid months on, the condo man at land value), said once in her mouth, plainly.

**Point of view:** Ray. Afraid register: something in his hands (the fork, the contract).

**Ends on:** **Consequence.** Arlene's line into the phone, last. Ray's reaction stays off the page.

**Word target:** 750

### Scene 4

**Setting:** Kettle Lanes, after league, Tuesday. He wasn't in it. The lot is empty, the lanes dark, and there's one light over the counter. **Sensory anchor:** the front door's glass rattling in its frame under his knock (Ch 01). Inside the vestibule, wet wool and road salt off the Tuesday division's boots, from the room he wasn't in.

**Characters:** Ray and Ellen. Nobody else; no phone call.

**Want:**
- **Ray** wants three things in this order. First, to name his wrong properly. Second, to keep his word to Dale by asking her for Linda's night, which is the first time in the book he asks her for something to her face. Third, **her signature on the release, which is the reason he came**, so that he stops being the man who wins if she misses. The reader learns the third want at once (the folded page on the counter, unopened) and hears it said at "I'm asking you to sign it."
- **Ellen** wants him inside and wants to give him the key back. In the way: a key handed over is her briefing him again, and he is waiting to be asked. The reader learns it when the door opens before his third rap, and again when the key comes out of her vest.

**Surface action, in order:**
1. **Open on the door glass rattling under two raps.** The door comes open before the third; she was already on the other side of it. **The touch:** her hand closes on his raised wrist and brings him in out of the wind, holds it a second past the door, and lets go. Nobody says "come in." No gloss. (Never "knuckle" and "glass" in one sentence.)
2. **The release is the reason they're in a room.** At the counter he takes a single folded page out of his jacket and lays it on the wood between them, unopened. **"That's what I came for. I'll ask you for it last."** Optional, one clause: as he crosses, his eyes go to the padlock on locker fourteen, and nothing more.
3. **His wrong, said more fully than before (reader: "barely counts").** Standing; she stands to take bad news. Model:
   - **Ray:** **"What I said about the radio. You told me that night in the pit with a pin in your hands, and I kept it, and when I wanted something that would land I went and got it."**
   - **Ellen:** **"It did. I have heard it every time I have keyed that microphone since."**
   - **Ray, plain, with the important word last:** **"I'm sorry for it, Ellen."**
   - No absolution and no "It's all right." She lets it stand.
4. **The verification reaches her (World rule 20).** **Ray: "Hollis gave me what you wrote. I watched you put the date on it."** She has no answer in order. Give one beat of her body (not a hand flat on the counter), and nothing in the narration about what it means.
5. **The ask, then the terms in three lines.** **"I told Dale I'd ask you to give the league its Memorial back. I'm asking."** Then the three lines:
   - "Linda's name comes off the Open."
   - "The league bowls its Memorial at two on the last Saturday, all ten lanes."
   - "Your Open gets the morning and the evening, and a cup of its own, with whatever name you like on it."
6. **Hollis's one line, carried by Ray** (he asked at cribbage Sunday; that isn't on the page): **"Hollis says the committee voted on an Open. Nobody voted on a name."**
7. **She accepts and pays. The money fact, one plain figure (orchestrator item 7).** **"Agreed."** Then: **"Nine teams entered to bowl for Linda's cup. I will call all nine in the morning and offer their money back, and all nine will take it. That puts me a hundred and fifteen dollars under thirty thousand, with every lane of the Open full."** Ray offers nothing. Optional laugh, Ellen literal: if Ray says the snack bar on Linda's night can find it, she answers with the exact thing it has to sell. Writer's choice; keep it to one exchange, and no figure for the fries.
8. **The release opened.** He unfolds the page and turns it to face her. **One line, no legalese:** Arlene's lawyer's page lets the three of them go from the March paper, with Arlene's signature first, his under it, and a line left. **"I'm asking you to sign it."** He asks once, never twice.
9. She puts on the reading glasses on the black cord and reads it once. **"You have asked an eighty-four-year-old woman to bet her bed on a hundred and fifteen dollars."** **Ray: "I know what I asked."**
10. **Ellen names the safer option:** **"You could keep it in your jacket until I have made the number, and tear it up then. Arlene would have her bed whatever I made."** **Ray: "I could. I'd be fixing your machines for one of us all month, and I couldn't tell you which."** This is the mirror of her line at the hood. The narration doesn't point it out.
11. **She says what her signature does, then her own reason, then signs:** **"If I miss, Arlene loses her place, and it will be my name that put it there."** Then the chapter's one Ellen contraction: **"If I ask you to stand beside me before this is done, I won't have you standing at the bottom of my drive as well."**
12. **Interior passage 3: her hand signing.** She takes a pen out of her vest. Ray watches her hand do it, slowly, in real time: one sensory detail of the pen on paper in a building with no other noise. Her printing, every letter standing separate. No pen skipping on the worn edge, and no gloss.
13. **The turn, said.** **Ray: "I'll call Concord in the morning and let it go. They'd want ninety days to write it again."** **Ellen: "There are thirteen days."**
14. **The K, accepted.** This is the first thing she has taken from him that wasn't on an invoice, and the reader supplies s2. **Ray: "I called Laconia this morning. They're bending you a K."** She looks at him. **"Thank you."** No gloss, and no reference to Ch 04's refusal.
15. **The key, last.** She brings his key out of her vest pocket, where it has been since the night he gave it back, and holds it across the counter on her open palm. He looks at it.
16. **Ray: "Not yet."** End. Nothing after it: no exit, no glass, no headlights.

**Subtext:** He comes to her door the way he came the first night, without a key, and lets her pull him in. He says his cruelty was chosen and says sorry with the plain word last. He asks her for Linda's night, which makes his word to Dale hers to keep or break, and then asks her to tie Arlene's bed to a number she is already short of, so that he can stop being the man who profits if she fails. She pays for all of it out loud. She signs for a reason that is a promise to ask, and then takes a gift from him for the first time. Then she hands him the key instead of asking him back, and he waits for the asking.

**Texture budget:** Two. The release, one line: nothing now stands between her and the deadline but her own winter. Ninety days against thirteen: he has made it impossible to be her buyer, in a figure she finishes before he does.

**Point of view:** Ray. Tender register for the door and her pen. Plain and short for his wrong. The terms in three plain lines, not a chain.

**Ends on:** **Question.** "Not yet." The key is still on her palm.

**Word target:** 1,100   **Word floor:** 850 (the repair). Play it from the knock to "Not yet" in real time. No summary of the reading, the signing or the refunds.

## The orchestrator's eight requirements, and where each lands

1. **The release's logic.** The paper's cost shows before anyone asks: Dale's word and stake, and the Bench bet (s1); his empty league night and her refusing anything not on an invoice (s1 → s2, seen by Arlene in s3); every call he takes (s3). The safer option is named by Arlene (s3) and by Ellen (s4), and each time it is answered with something already shown to fail. Arlene has her own reason, her window: "the two of you have been sitting in cars." Ellen signs a hundred and fifteen dollars under, knowing it, for a reason the D-side ask in Ch 09 pays.
2. **The match.** Ray called Gil (Gil's line). Gil likes a match and has the Tuesday division in his lounge either way. Dale wants it settled before Linda's night and will take Ray's word on a stake. Ray put up what Dale couldn't refuse: their lineage, and his own team bowling for Gil.
3. **The teams come home.** Ellen at his counter, with what she saw: cash, a receipt, "He asked me where you were."
4. **The final scene is the two of them.** The touch at the door; his wrong, more fully; the terms in three lines; Hollis's one line, carried; the figure; the release as the reason; the K accepted; the key last. No phone call.
5. **Arlene's kitchen.** The contract is one clause; the retelling is cut.
6. **The letter.** It is the yellow page he watched her date at her counter that night, in his hands in the Pine Ridge lot.
7. **The money.** "A hundred and fifteen dollars under thirty thousand," in Ellen's mouth as she accepts the refunds.
8. **Retired.** One date (the third of February); no clock openings; no "the way" similes; the full list is in flag 9.

## Tournament lead instructions

*One per variant. The beats above are fixed for all three: same scenes, same lines in bold, same endings, same word targets and floor. The lead changes what each variant puts weight on, never what happens. Each variant is written against the same retired list (flag 9).*

### V1: The couple, present in objects where absent

Ellen is absent from s1 and s3, and the reader stopped aching for the couple in the version that failed. In every scene she isn't in, keep one thing of hers in Ray's hand or line of sight, and give it one clause of attention that is about her, not the plot:
- s1: Denise's pencil out of her cigar box; then her page, her printing, the date.
- s2: the page in his jacket against her red hands on his counter.
- s3: her printing on the last page of the contract, then the line Arlene tells the lawyer to leave for her name.

In the scenes they share, write their bodies at close range: the inch of counter between them, her hand on his wrist at the door held a second past need, her pen, the key on her palm. Let s4 run to its full 1,100. The touch at the door and her hand signing are the two high points, slow and in real time. Nothing anyone says about the paper should be louder than what their hands are doing while they say it. The test: a reader should miss her in s3.

### V2: Pressure and pace

Every scene runs on a visible pressure, never a clock:
- s1: nine pins behind with ten on the deck, and Dale's "losing pays you" hanging over every ball.
- s2: the Bench is due through that door, and she has to finish before they do.
- s3: Arlene's bed against Ray's ask, and the lawyer on the line.
- s4: a hundred and fifteen dollars, thirteen days, and the page on the counter from the first beat.

Enter each scene as late as the opening beat allows, leave on the assigned last line, and cut every transition. No setting paragraph longer than two sentences outside the three interior passages, and the interior passages stay long, because pace here means pressure, not short sentences. The logic lines are short and land fast: "Everything you put up, losing pays you." "Then hold it." "I could." Overlap and interrupt in the dialogue wherever the beats allow it. Run 3–5% under the targets, never under the floor. The test: a reader should finish s1 needing to know why Ray won't be there Tuesday.

### V3: Play the predictable thing early, then spend the scene on what it costs

The cold reader predicted almost every outcome in this chapter: Ray wins the match, she's forgiven, Arlene says yes, she signs, the key waits. Stop hiding those outcomes. Let the reader see each one coming in the scene's first third, then spend the rest of the scene on what it costs and who pays:
- **s1:** it is obvious he'll win. The scene is about Dale's "losing pays you" and the end stool's dollar, and the win lands with nobody in the room sure he meant it, until the page in the lot.
- **s2:** it is obvious she's come to make it right. The cost is that he won't let her, and the man who bet against him is the one who pours.
- **s3:** it is obvious Arlene will agree. The scene is her bed, said plainly; the safer option she offers him; and the window, where she makes him hear what her own paper did to them.
- **s4:** it is obvious she'll sign. The scene is the hundred and fifteen dollars and Arlene's bed said before the pen moves, and the key, which is the one thing that doesn't go the way the reader expects.

Every cost is said by the person who pays it, in their own syntax. Ray's narration never softens a price after it's named. The test: a reader who predicted the ending should still feel what it cost.
