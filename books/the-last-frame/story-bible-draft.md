## THE CORE

**Premise:** **[v3]** Ellen Daly, 58, retired by federal statute after 31 years as an air-traffic controller, buys Kettle Lanes, a failing ten-lane 1958 candlepin alley in Ambrose, New Hampshire, and owes the former owner a quarter of a million dollars on March 1. Arlene wouldn't carry the note without a backup buyer, so she asked Ray Gagnon, 61, to sign behind Ellen. If the refinance hasn't funded by March 1, the contract Ellen signed sells him the building that afternoon at appraised value. Ray is the widowed hardware man who has coached the alley's winter league for thirty years. He lost the building to her by eight days, he had meant to retire into it once his son had the store, and he is the only man in town who can keep her 1950s pinsetters running until then. The bank will lend the day the winter clears thirty thousand dollars, with every dollar of it still in the account.

**Emotional question:** Can a woman who has always held the position alone share it without feeling relieved of it, and can a man who keeps everything the way it was learn to want to be asked more than he wants to keep?

**Tone:** Deadpan, warm, charged.

**This story is NOT:**
- **[v3]** A rescue. No windfall, no viral video, no rich relative, and none of Ray's money: his cash and his name are both closed by facts said on the page. The winter comes up nine hundred dollars short. At nine on March 1, before the committee votes, Ellen sends about $7,200 of her own savings to Arlene's note, by her own choice and in Ray's hearing.
- A grief book or a healing journey. Linda is six years gone and stays there. Feeling shows in hands, in what people fix, and in the sentence someone chooses not to say.
- **[v3]** A business procedural with a romance in it. The money, the bank's rules and the machines are seasoning: one or two pieces per scene. The meal is two grown-ups who want each other and argue about everything.
- **[v3]** A villain story. Dale, Judy's committee, Arlene and Denise all have reasons a reader would accept.

**Prose register, at a high point:**
At a fight, a kiss or a crisis the sentences get longer and the scene gets slower, never shorter and faster. Stay in real time and play the whole moment on the page, every beat of it, with no summary and no cutaway before the floor is met. Each lead breaks in their own way. Ellen's ordered interior gives way: the list collapses into one long sentence joined by *and*, out of order, and she notices that it's out of order and leaves it that way. **[v3]** That collapse has run twice on the page (Ch 01 at the ball, Ch 03 at the sign) and has one use left in the book, and that one may not be at a kiss. From Ch 05 her desire breaks something else she keeps: the time (she loses the minute), the count, the title she gives him. Under fear her dialogue goes calm, polite and imperative while the interior runs fast underneath, and the reader is shown the gap. Ray's long, patient sentences end on one short accurate one; in anger he drops to five-word sentences and "Mrs. Daly"; hurt, he says the one line that will land, and the narration stays on it while it goes on landing. Dialogue overlaps, interrupts and gets the line it can't take back. The body is present as hands, cold, breath on a wrist, the weight of a pin or a key, and each beat gets one sharp sensory detail. Feeling is never named; it shows up in what a hand does next.

**Prose register, at a quiet point:**
A quiet scene is built on one want the reader can feel (she wants to stay; he wants her to ask) and still turns something. Sentences average fifteen to eighteen words with real spread: long sentences that move through a room picking things up, then land on a short one. The comedy lives in the gap between a question and its answer, and nobody laughs at their own line. Give one ordinary pleasure room for three or four sentences (a hot bowl, a heater ticking in a truck, the crash of a full house) and let the character enjoy it without comment. One piece of texture at most. What matters is under the talk: the subject neither of them raises, the thing one of them notices and keeps. Never three sentences of five words or fewer in a row unless a door has just slammed. Audit targets: mean sentence 13–18 words, sentences of five words or fewer under 15%.

### The strip-the-names test (for the dialogue-doctor and voice-editor)

Run on every chapter's final pass, with names and tags removed. The cues:
- **Ellen:** full stops; items and ordinals; exact numbers; no hedges; imperatives under fear; contractions dropping in anger; true sentences used as cover; any line that comments on order ("no second item", "lose my place", "any order she could use").

## WORLD RULES (ceiling: 20; each prohibition paired with what to do instead)

*Twenty rules. Rules that only require are marked (positive).*

1. **No swearing, of any kind.** No "damn," "hell," "crap," no "God," "Lord," "Jesus" as exclamations (including "Oh my God," "Jeez," "Geez," "Cripes," "Jeezum crow"), no censored swears, no "he cursed under his breath." — *Instead:* give each character their own substitute and use it at the moment of heat. Ellen: "Oh, for crying out loud" in real extremity, or understatement ("Well. That's suboptimal."). Ray: "For Pete's sake," "Son of a gun," "Holy mackerel" once (Ch 8). Hollis: "Wicked" as an intensifier. Arlene: "Voyons donc." Denise: "Heaven help us," sparingly. Anger shows as the register shift in each lead's syntax profile.
2. **Closed door, and no innuendo.** No nights together, on or off the page; no bedrooms; no "pins" or "balls" double meanings (check every bowling sentence). — *Instead:* put desire on the page as wanting, in every chapter: a hand that stays, a look held past the point of manners, a line said too close, a thought about the other that isn't about the plot. See HEAT RULES.
3. **[v3] No rescue money.** No windfall, inheritance, grant, viral fame, or money from Ray, Kate or anyone else; Ray never owns any of it. His cash is closed in Ch 05 s4 (seasoning; it's Arlene's floor), and his name is closed in Ch 09 s4. Gagnon & Son's ordinary bookings (the staff party, the team's Open entry) are customer money the store has always spent there, never help. The K is the house's, on net thirty after the winter. — *Instead:* trace every dollar of the margin to a decision Ellen made earlier, and make the end cost her: about $7,200 of her own savings, sent to Arlene's note at nine before the committee votes, chosen aloud in front of Ray.
4. **Nobody does skilled work they aren't qualified for.** — *Instead:* Ellen hires and holds the flashlight, and her competence shows as judgment (whom to call, what to say, in what order). Ray fixes pinsetters, rekeys locks, and mounts the K the Laconia shop built, with the sign's power off at the breaker and the shop's instructions in his pocket (one line). Hands-on work for Ellen is work anyone could do: setting pins by hand, digging out the sign, holding the ladder. **[v3]** The Laconia shop sets the transformer, and Ray mounts the glass with the disconnect open.
5. **No sentence states the theme or the meaning of the scene.** No closing aphorism, no moral, no "and that was enough." — *Instead:* end every scene on an action or a line of dialogue, using the ending type the outline assigns (question, cut, consequence, reversal, open image), and never two soft endings in a row. **[v3]** At most one scene per chapter ends on a placed button line. At least one ends mid-motion or on a line nobody answers. "Noted." is never the last word of an exchange (log Ch 02, 03, 09 flags). A chapter never answers its own cliffhanger (genre map).
6. **ATC phrases are capped.** At most one ATC phrase per scene in Ellen's dialogue and three per chapter (narration included); six per chapter in the three set pieces (Ch 1 PA, Ch 4 radio, Ch 9 position relief). Never two in one sentence; no acronyms (no "TRACON" more than once a chapter; never ATSAP, MOR or 7110.65). — *Instead:* use the approved vocabulary (cleared, unable, say again, stand by, say intentions, say request, affirmative, negative, hold short, sequence, number two, traffic twelve o'clock one mile, ground stop, readback, relief briefing, the D-side), make each one funny or exact because it's applied to bowling, and keep Ellen's interior thinking in order and numbers, never jargon. Other people mocking her phrases don't count against her cap but do count as the scene's texture.
7. **[v3] Write for the ear: no codes, slash-strings or runs of figures on the page.** — *Instead:* write every figure in words a text-to-speech voice reads cleanly ("fourteen hundred under"), one money fact per chapter after Ch 2 (the outline's four statements), and Ellen's labels as one or two words in capitals at most once a scene. Never stack two clock times or two dollar figures in a sentence. Lanes always take the word "lane" ("lane four," never "on four"). Cue every speaker at an emotional peak with an action, not only a tag. Normand is always "Arlene's Normand," and Normie stays unnamed on any page where Normand is spoken. Audit targets: codes under 2 per 1,000 words, numerals under 12, all-caps under 6.
8. **No recap at the top of a scene or chapter, and no re-staging what the reader already saw.** — *Instead:* open in motion at the outline's "Opens with" moment, inside someone's want. When one character tells another something the reader knows, give it one clause and stay on the listener's face. Quote "unless the numbers say otherwise" exactly each time it's quoted. **[v3]** At most one scene per chapter opens on a clock, weekday or date. The rest open on hands or a line (log Ch 06 flags).
9. **Candlepin is never wrong and never a lecture.** — *Instead:* name the game in Ch 1 in one spoken line from Hollis, then explain a rule only when a scene turns on it. Use box, string, wood, sets, ten box, fill balls; lobs are called by an official. Give the sport's sound and feel (the crash like wooden spoons, the small heavy ball, the long lane) more than its rules.
10. **Texture is seasoning.** One or two pieces of trade, sport, money or procedure per scene, never a run of them. — *Instead:* pick the one that reveals the character or moves the romance (Ray naming a part by ear, Ellen's relief briefing), and give the rest of the scene to people talking.
11. **No metronome.** The same rhythm in a crisis and at supper is a failure. — *Instead (positive):* sentence length follows temperature, as in THE CORE. Long sentences are required at high points and in quiet interior passages; each POV chapter has at least three interior passages that run longer than the dialogue around them. Mean 13–18 words; five-word-or-shorter sentences under 15%.
12. **Every chapter carries a turn, a longing, a laugh and a cost. (positive)** The laugh is one line a specific person says at a specific target. The longing is attraction a reader would call attraction; business between the leads doesn't count. Every scene has a want the reader can feel, and each chapter visibly moves the money, the deadline or the town's opinion, in a scene.
13. **[v3] Both leads are written at their ages, and the hero has his half. (positive)** Ellen is fifty-eight (said once, Ch 01; spent); Ray is sixty-one (never said, or said once). Give her what a woman her age has now: the phone she uses (the radar in Ch 03, the recording in Ch 01, texts with periods and no question mark), her clothes, a fit body with one bad knee and reading glasses on a cord, work she is good at, and desire on the page. No "at our age," no "like a teenager again," and no aches played for comedy beyond Ray's clicking knee and his own mock-solemnity about the one supper.

15. **No convenient coincidences, and big wins never come from luck.** — *Instead:* when chance helps or hurts the leads, someone arranged it (Hollis puts Ellen on lane 5 because Ray is on 6), and every win pays off something a character did earlier.
16. **[v3] No villain, no death, no accident to move the plot.** — *Instead:* give every opponent a reason a reader would accept, in their own line (Dale's 3:10 call and his "Still no," Judy's underwriter and her committee, Arlene's bed, Ray's "It's my wife's name"). Linda's death stays six years past.
17. **[v3] The calendar and the weather are fixed.** Two weather crises only (Dec 17, Feb 27); Feb 28's slush is a consequence, not a third. League nights are Tuesday and Thursday, dark from Dec 22 through Jan 3. Christmas is off the page. Hollis's calls go out by phone, the club phone tree and the Laconia station, never the weather band. — *Instead:* make the cold a working condition, one detail per scene through what someone has to do about it (shovel, plow, measure, refund), and never open on weather unless the weather is the plot.
18. **Side characters don't speechify or swap voices.** One short speech each per chapter at most; no town monologues in turn. — *Instead:* give each one the line only they would say (their profile above), let the town talk in overlapping short lines, and run the strip-the-names test on every chapter.
19. **[v3] Running bits retire.** A gag that has landed three times is done; signature lines appear at most twice a chapter, and a chapter may skip them. Ray's comparisons to motors, clutches and machines run two per Ray chapter at most, and none in Ellen's narration. Retired as devices (log Ch 02, 03, 05, 07, 09 flags): the PA's hum before she speaks; a zipper "to the chin"; "Ray saw her sit"; "did not raise his/her voice"; "a beat longer, and then another" and "and then for one more"; the ice going off as "a long boom" (two uses in the book); the air-traffic filter on a silence (once at most, at the bank); "the voice she kept for the minutes"; planting with a one-word echo of the other lead's line (used once, for "supper," in Ch 09 s1); Ellen's list collapsing into *and*s (one use left, never at a kiss); ordinal lists more than once a chapter. — *Instead:* count against the MOTIF LIST, and when a bit is spent, change it (the radio joke becomes "Gagnon, say request" in Ch 10) or let a new detail carry the beat. A set piece the page has already played (the stranded night, the owner's ball, the clock at five) is never re-staged.
20. **[v3] Adults talk; nothing is hidden between the leads after Ch 04 s1.** The book's one withheld fact (Ellen watched Ray not hear "Unless the numbers say otherwise," Dec 18) ends when she self-reports it at 7:02 a.m. on Dec 19, after he has vouched to the Bench on half her sentence. Ellen never states a false fact, and after Ch 04 s1 no true sentence of hers hides one either (the outline's checkpoints: Ch 05 s4, 06 s3, 06 s4, 07 s3, 08 s4, 09 s4–s6, 10 s1). — *Instead:* when a reasonable adult would ask, the lead asks, on the page, inside the scene (the outline lists every ask). ### High-temperature sample (Ch 3, dawn, Ellen POV: the first kiss)

Ray had handed her a grain scoop with a cracked handle and kept the good shovel for himself, and she was using the scoop badly, and he was letting her, which she meant to hold against him once she had a free minute. They had dug the drift off the north side of the sign pole down to frozen gravel. Over the lake the sky had gone from black to the gray of a radar screen with nothing on it, and behind the glass every person she had stranded was asleep in a parka on a burnt-orange settee, in the order she had put them in at nine o'clock last night. For twenty minutes she had not checked on a single one of them. She noticed that now, standing in the trench with the scoop in her bare hands, and it frightened her more than the power going out had.

"You've got your gloves off again," Ray said. He leaned the shovel against the pole. "Every time I look over you've taken them off to do something, and it's never anything a glove would get in the way of, and I've about decided you just like your hands cold."

"I like to know what I'm holding."

"Give them here, then."

She gave them to him. He opened his canvas jacket and put both her hands inside it, flat against the flannel over his ribs, and held them there with his own on the outside, and she stood in the trench they had dug and felt the cold go out of her fingers one at a time, and she meant to tell him first that Hollis would be up the drive within the hour and second that Denise would be awake before that and third that there was a sentence from the pit he had not heard and needed to, and instead she was looking at the snow caught in his eyebrows, and the gray along his jaw, and his mouth, which had told her about Linda's best night in the dark in a voice so careful she had wanted to put her hand over it, and none of it was in any order, and she knew it, and she left it that way.

"Ellen," he said, in the voice she had been listening to all night over the noise of the machines, low and unhurried, a three-in-the-morning voice with the whole sky going gray behind it. ### Quiet sample (Ch 9 s1, Ellen POV: supper at a quarter past five)

She knocked at fourteen minutes past five, and Ray let her stand on the back step until a quarter past, and then opened the door and told her so. His kitchen was small and too warm and painted a yellow somebody had picked out around 1979 and nobody had argued with since. A radio on top of the refrigerator was reading the Bruins' lineup to the canisters. There were two bowls set out on an oilcloth table that she suspected was used to one, and on the stove a dented aluminum pot whose lid sat crooked, and the whole room smelled of ground beef and green pepper and canned tomatoes cooked down past the point where anyone could call them sauce.

"American chop suey," he said, before she could ask. "Elbow macaroni and the rest of it, in the same pot since the pot before this one gave out, the winter the furnace went. I'd tell you it was Lucien's recipe, and he'd have liked that, and it's on the side of the macaroni box."

She sat before he asked her to. She did not sit in other people's kitchens until she knew where everything was, and she did not know where anything was in this one, and he saw her do it and said nothing, and set the bowl down in front of her with the care she had watched him use on a clutch spring. Under the table his boot touched her left knee, and he moved it at once and asked what she had done to it. She told him. A ski fall in 2009, on a trail she had no business being on, and the ride down in the patrol's toboggan, and the orthopedist who had said *at your age* to a woman of forty-one, and she told it in order, all of it, which she had done for no one else, and he laughed at the toboggan and nowhere else, and that was the right place.

"What do you make the second night?" she said.

"That one."

"And the third."

"Same pot." He moved the salt an inch to the left for no reason she could find. "It's never once failed me, and I don't see why I'd start taking chances at my age."

It was good, and she said so, and he took the compliment the way he took one on a repair, by looking at the pot. Outside the window over the sink it was full dark at twenty to six, and a little snow was coming down into his dooryard under the porch light, and the radio had gone on to the weather, and she let it go on without her. "That's a good supper," she said, and heard which word had come out of her mouth, and decided to leave it there.
