## THE CORE

**Premise:** Ellen Daly, 58, retired by federal statute after 31 years as an air-traffic controller, buys Kettle Lanes, a failing ten-lane 1958 candlepin alley in Ambrose, New Hampshire, and owes the former owner a quarter of a million dollars on March 1. To get the loan, she signed a backup contract: if the refinance hasn't funded by March 1, the building goes that afternoon, at appraised value, to Ray Gagnon, 61, the widowed hardware man who has coached the alley's winter league for thirty years, lost the building to her by eight days, and is the only man in town who can keep her 1950s pinsetters running until then. The bank will lend the day the winter clears thirty thousand dollars, with every dollar of it still in the account.

**Emotional question:** Can a woman who has always held the position alone share it without feeling relieved of it, and can a man who keeps everything the way it was learn to want to be asked more than he wants to keep?

**Tone:** Deadpan, warm, charged.

**This story is NOT:**
- A rescue. No windfall, no viral video, no rich relative, and none of Ray's money. The winter comes up $1,100 short and Ellen pays it from her own savings, out loud.
- A grief book or a healing journey. Linda is six years gone and stays there. Feeling shows in hands, in what people fix, and in the sentence someone chooses not to say.
- A business procedural with a romance in it. The money, the bylaws and the machines are seasoning: one or two pieces per scene. The meal is two grown-ups who want each other and argue about everything.
- A villain story. The captain, Gil, Judy's bank, Arlene and the league all have reasons a reader would accept.

**Prose register, at a high point:**
At a fight, a kiss or a crisis the sentences get longer and the scene gets slower, never shorter and faster. Stay in real time and play the whole moment on the page, every beat of it, with no summary and no cutaway before the floor is met. Each lead breaks in their own way. Ellen's ordered interior gives way: the list collapses into one long sentence joined by *and*, out of order, and she notices that it's out of order and leaves it that way. Under fear her dialogue goes calm, polite and imperative while the interior runs fast underneath, and the reader is shown the gap. Ray's long, patient sentences end on one short accurate one; in anger he drops to five-word sentences and "Mrs. Daly"; hurt, he says the one line that will land, and the narration stays on it while it goes on landing. Dialogue overlaps, interrupts and gets the line it can't take back. The body is present as hands, cold, breath on a wrist, the weight of a pin or a key, and each beat gets one sharp sensory detail. Feeling is never named; it shows up in what a hand does next.

**Prose register, at a quiet point:**
A quiet scene is built on one want the reader can feel (she wants to stay; he wants her to ask) and still turns something. Sentences average fifteen to eighteen words with real spread: long sentences that move through a room picking things up, then land on a short one. The comedy lives in the gap between a question and its answer, and nobody laughs at their own line. Give one ordinary pleasure room for three or four sentences (a hot bowl, a heater ticking in a truck, the crash of a full house) and let the character enjoy it without comment. One piece of texture at most. What matters is under the talk: the subject neither of them raises, the thing one of them notices and keeps. Never three sentences of five words or fewer in a row unless a door has just slammed. Audit targets: mean sentence 13–18 words, sentences of five words or fewer under 15%.

### Ellen Daly

- **Name:** kept from v1. An Irish-American name common in her generation in southern New Hampshire and the Merrimack valley, and plain enough for a woman who has spent her life being heard on a frequency.
- **Age, body, clothes:** 58. Five foot six, square-shouldered, stands with her weight even on both feet like someone braced for a long shift. Silver hair cut short every five weeks. Reading glasses on a black cord. Navy fleece vest over an oxford shirt, sleeves rolled twice. Plain steel watch. Bad left knee from a ski fall in 2009 that she won't discuss.
- **Physical habit others notice:** she stands to eat and stands to take bad news. She sits down only once something's been decided. Denise is the first to notice it ("You're standing. What happened?"), and Ray is the one who notices when she sits.
- **Background (single lines):**
  - Hired by the FAA at 25. Early years in a tower on paper strips; most of the career at Boston Consolidated TRACON in Merrimack, working arrivals into Logan.
  - One loss of separation in 31 years, at 2 a.m., near the end of a midnight shift. She sounded perfect on the frequency. She wrote it up herself before anyone asked. **[BB]** v1 fact correction stands: it happened *during* the midnight shift, not at its end; write "on a midnight shift" and avoid "at the end of."
  - Retired by statute at 56, with a sheet cake.
  - Divorced about twenty years, amicably. One line in the book at most.
  - One daughter, Kate, 31, an ER nurse in Manchester.
  - Sold the Merrimack house for the down payment. Lives on her pension in a rented winterized camp on the lake. Takes no salary from the alley.
  - **[BB]** Her Thrift Savings Plan went into a TSP life annuity at retirement, so it pays monthly and can't be drawn as a lump. That's why $14,000 in a savings account is the only money she can reach, and why she chose it: guaranteed income, in order, every month. `[CHECK]`
- **Want (the book's plot want) [BB, from v1]:** To make Kettle Lanes pay by March 1 on her own decisions and her own numbers.
- **What she wants that has nothing to do with Ray (or the alley):**
  1. **For Kate to ask her something before it's decided.** Kate calls with outcomes ("I took the night job." "We broke up in March."), the way Ellen has always briefed people. Ellen hears herself in it and hates it. *Before the book:* she drove to Manchester on Kate's birthday in October, the first birthday in twenty-six years she wasn't on the schedule, and Kate was working a night shift. She's still calling every Sunday at seven, and still being told things after they've happened. (Pays off: Ch 5, Ch 9 s5, "Call me first next time. Like this.")
  2. **To sleep past four.** Thirty-one years of rotating shifts, and her body still wakes at 3:50. She has blackout curtains, a white-noise machine and a paperback on sleep hygiene she's annotated. None of it works. It's the small comic want. Hollis's 4:10 a.m. call in Ch 9 finds her up and dressed.
- **Wound, and what it makes her do:** On the worst night of her career her voice was perfect while she came apart, so she learned that her calm can hide anything. *Behavior:* she briefs people instead of asking them. She reports outcomes, not questions. She self-reports her own errors fast and in writing, before anyone else can. She never asks for help, because asking would mean someone had noticed she needed it.
- **Need:** To be relieved without feeling she's failed: to share the position and keep her seat.
- **Strength and how it is also the flaw [BB, from v1]:** Composure and sequencing; she can put forty problems in order. The same gift makes her brief instead of ask, and treat accepting help as losing the position.
- **What she would never do:**
  1. State a false fact. She will mislead with a true sentence. That's the mechanism of her wrong, and she knows it.
  2. Raise her voice in a building she runs.
  3. Walk away from a position without a handover, even in a fight. She leaves the key and the list.
- **The choice a reasonable reader would condemn:**
  - **Primary (Ch 3–6).** She watches Ray not hear "unless the numbers say otherwise," lets it stand, and kisses him. She sits silent while he vouches for her to the league in Verna's minutes. She takes Linda's name for a margin she doesn't strictly need. Asked straight out, "Did you know I didn't hear it?", she answers with a true sentence used as a lie: "I said it." *Why she does it:* every error in her life has cost her a job or a rating, and she has always paid that price at once and in writing. This one would cost her him. It's the first time the price of a readback is something she wants. A woman who self-reports everything finds out she won't self-report this, and the book should let her see it in herself.
  - **Secondary (Ch 8 s5).** She signs the release that puts Arlene's place at The Pines on her number, for her own reason (she won't have the man she asks to stand beside her be the man waiting to buy). A reader can fairly say she bet an eighty-four-year-old's bed on her pride. She says so herself before she signs.
- **How she is funny, and at whom:** Deadpan literalism. She answers the question that was actually asked, exactly, and the gap between the question and the answer is the joke. She never signals she's joking and never laughs at her own line. *Targets:* pomposity and mockery (she wins every heckle by taking it literally), the procedure of things, and herself (she bowls badly and reports it like a weather observation). *Sample:* From lane 3: "Tower, say our altitude." / "Five hundred and twelve feet. It's on the survey." *Who she's funny with:* Denise. They're a double act: Denise talks in paragraphs, and Ellen answers in one line.
- **What she finds attractive in Ray, plainly:** His height; she likes looking up at someone, and has had few chances. His hands: big, nicked, and gentle with small parts. His voice: low and unhurried, the voice of a good pilot at 3 a.m., which is the most attractive sound she knows. That he's funny and doesn't check whether she laughed. That he isn't afraid of her. *First thing she notices:* snow on his shoulders, and that he's taller than she'd planned for.
- **Syntax profile:**
  - *Sentence length and shape.* Dialogue: short-to-mid, complete sentences, subject-verb-object, full stops. She arranges things in items ("Two things." then one, then two) and stops when the list is done. Exact numbers ("forty-one", never "about forty"). She doesn't hedge: no "kind of", "sort of", "I guess", "maybe". Interior: mid-length and ordered, with *first*, *second* and *next* doing the work. It is not clipped; the interior must run longer than her dialogue, with an occasional long sentence that moves.
  - *Under pressure.* **Anger:** slower and quieter; contractions drop out; full names ("Mr. Gagnon"); she turns questions into statements. **Fear:** she gets more polite and more procedural, uses imperatives and present tense, and briefs whoever is nearest. The more frightened she is, the calmer she sounds; the reader must be shown the gap from inside. **Hurt:** one word ("Understood.") and then a task. **Desire:** the list breaks. Her interior turns into one long sentence joined by *and*, out of order, and she notices that it's out of order. This is her high-temperature register, and it must appear on the page. A bible of all-restrained Ellen produces a restrained book.
  - *What she notices first.* Where everyone is and where they're moving, and the time (she knows it without looking). Then voices: after 31 years of pilots she hears strain, fatigue and a lie in a voice before she hears the words.
  - *One habit of thought hers alone.* **Sequencing.** Every problem gets a slot and a number; worries are traffic and get put in order. When something won't take a slot (Ray, mostly), she notices that it won't.
  - *Her words, never Ray's:* "Correct." "Understood." "Go ahead." "Confirm." "at present", "dinner" (Ray says "supper"; she says "supper" for the first time in Ch 9), "Noted.", "in sequence". ATC phrases follow the cap rule in WORLD RULES (rule 6).
  - *Motif caps (v1 tics reduced to motifs):* **counting the room**: three times in the book (Ch 1 on arrival, Ch 3's thirty-one, Ch 9's hundred and fifty), never as a reflex line. **"I'm aware"**: twice in the book, both in anger, never as her answer to a feeling. **Readback** (repeating someone's words back): once as a joke in Ch 4, once as the D-side in Ch 9; "no readback" as a thought once (Ch 3) and a spoken line once (Ch 7), per the outline.
- **Ten lines of dialogue:**
  - *Flirting*
    1. "Stay on the step. I haven't finished disagreeing with you."
    2. "That's three stories about other men tonight. Tell me one about you. You have four minutes, and then I open the doors."
  - *Angry*
    3. "Do not stand in my building and tell me what I signed. I read it twice. I signed it anyway."
    4. "Say that again, slower. I would like to be certain I heard you before I answer it."
  - *Lying (she lies only with true sentences)*
    5. "I said it."
    6. "If I take it, I'll never know which of us carried it."
  - *Afraid*
    7. "Thirty-one cars on Kettle Road in this is a sequence of accidents. Nobody leaves."
    8. "Don't be kind to me right now. I'll lose my place."
  - *Tender*
    9. "Read it back to me. I want to hear it in your voice."
    10. "I love you. There's no second item."
- **Five lines of interior narration:**
  1. He was taller than she'd planned for, and she had planned for most things.
  2. First, the lane. Second, the refund. Third, she would not look at his mouth, and she was already behind on the third.
  3. *No readback.* In thirty-one years she had let none go by, and she let this one go by.
  4. She had sounded perfect at two in the morning once, and she could sound perfect now. That had always been the trouble with her.
  5. She wanted him to stay on the step and keep arguing and take his gloves off and call her Mrs. Daly again so she could correct it, and none of that was in any order she could use.
- **Three ordinary pleasures:**
  1. The first coffee at 4:10 a.m. in the dark camp, alone, while the lake ice booms and settles. It's the one good thing about not sleeping.
  2. A full house: all ten lanes going at once, the overlapping crash like a full frequency on a good night, every voice in its slot.
  3. Snack-bar fries with malt vinegar, eaten standing at the counter out of the paper boat, too hot.

### Ray Gagnon

- **Name:** kept from v1. A Franco-American surname: the Gagnons came down from Québec to the New Hampshire mills, as a quarter of the state's families did. "Raymond" only to Arlene. **[BB]** The "quarter of the state" figure is the commonly cited share of NH residents with French-Canadian ancestry; keep it off the page or say "a lot of the state." `[CHECK]`
- **Age, body, clothes:** 61. Six foot one, a little stooped from forty years of counters. Gray crew cut every other Saturday. Big hands with nicked knuckles. A carpenter's pencil behind his right ear; reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and forgotten there. Brown canvas work jacket with GAGNON & SON over the pocket. A heavy key ring with Arlene's 1996 key on it, orange paper tag reading "R.G. — 1996."
- **Physical habit others notice:** he fixes things while he talks: tightens a chair screw, squares a ball on the rack, pockets a bent nail. Ellen notices that he does it hardest when the conversation is about him. The knuckle on the trophy-case glass, which the v1 reader loved, is a motif capped at three in the book (Ch 1, Ch 4, Ch 10), not a habit.
- **Background (single lines):**
  - Grew up above Gagnon & Son, which his father Lucien opened in 1964. Lucien put the store up as collateral once, in 1974, and nearly lost it.
  - Married Linda Bouchard in 1988. She was the best woman candlepin bowler in the county and holds the house record, 187 (February 1998).
  - Linda died six years ago of a stroke at 55, on a Wednesday in March. She had bowled the night before. Never date it on the page.
  - Coached the Kettle Lanes winter league since 1996; Arlene gave him the key that year. Arlene's unpaid mechanic for twenty years.
  - Son Danny, 35, runs the store day to day.
  - Lost the alley to Ellen by eight days.
  - **[BB]** He is also the town's locksmith; Gagnon & Son has always cut keys and rekeyed locks (Ch 1's joke, Ch 2's rekey). `[CHECK]` NH locksmith licensing.
- **Want (the book's plot want) [BB, from v1]:** To own Kettle Lanes and keep it exactly as Linda bowled it.
- **What he wants that has nothing to do with Ellen (or the alley):**
  1. **To hand the store to Danny properly and be asked back, not checked on.** He has been "retiring after the season" since 2022. *Before the book:* he still opens at six every morning, recounts the drawer Danny already counted, and calls it helping. He knows Danny knows. (Supports Ch 2's signature, Ch 6's "You're allowed to want something that isn't on a shelf.")
  2. **To beat Hollis at cribbage for one whole winter.** Sunday nights at Hollis's kitchen table since 1983, and Hollis has won eleven winters running. Ray keeps the running tally in pencil inside the cupboard door. It's the small comic want, and it shows up in Hollis scenes.
- **Wound, and what it makes him do:** When Linda died he froze everything she'd left: her ball in locker 14, her chair behind the store counter, the league exactly as she bowled it. He calls this loyalty. *Behavior:* he says what he feels by fixing something instead. He speaks *for* other people (the league, the old teams, Linda: "Linda wouldn't have wanted…"), because it's easier than speaking for himself. He vouches for people in public, and he means it.
- **Need:** To want to be asked more than he wants to keep things.
- **Strength and how it is also the flaw [BB, from v1]:** Steadiness; he shows up every time with the right part. The same steadiness freezes everything he loves in place, and he'll fix a machine at midnight to avoid one honest sentence at noon.
- **What he would never do:**
  1. Leave a machine broken on league night, whoever owns it.
  2. Buy anything cheap off somebody's mistake. Appraised value, not a dollar under.
  3. Ask the store twice. He swore it after 1974, and he holds to it in Ch 2.
  *(Removed from v1: "never speak against Ellen in public," which the v2 outline breaks at the Jan 5 meeting, and "never lie, even to be kind." He doesn't lie about anything that matters, but he lies easily about why he's helping.)*
- **The choice a reasonable reader would condemn:**
  - **Primary (Ch 7).** After her confession he picks the one line that will land and says it: "You sounded fine on the radio that night too, I bet." She told him the 2 a.m. night in the dark with her hands on a pin, and he uses it on her. *Why:* he stood up in front of the league twice for her (Jan 5, and an hour earlier at the counter), and she let him do both knowing. A man who speaks for other people has just found out he was speaking for something that wasn't true. His cruelty is never loud. It's one short, accurate sentence, and he names it himself in Ch 8 s5.
  - **Secondary (Ch 8).** So that he won't be the man waiting to buy, he asks Arlene, eighty-four, to put her place at The Pines on Ellen's number, and then asks Ellen to sign it too. He does it to their faces and hears the cost, and does it anyway.
- **How he is funny, and at whom:** A tease with a straight face and a slow fuse. The joke comes at the end of a long, ordinary sentence, on the last word, and he doesn't wait to see if it landed. He has three engines. (a) **Machines and weather as people with moods**: "She's not broken. She's sulking." (b) **The story about some other fella** instead of an answer. These are tall-ish, local, and they end on a turn: "Fella I knew in Tilton bought a boat the week his wife left. Boat was fine. He couldn't swim." (c) **Mock-solemnity about his own habits**, the one supper especially. *Targets:* Ellen's exactness (his favorite target, and gentle: he treats her precision as a weather condition), Hollis, the Bench, and himself. On the radio he does her phraseology badly, on purpose, and that's his flirting. *Sample:* "I've made the same supper since 1996 and it's never once failed. I don't see why I'd start taking chances at my age." *Who he's funny with:* Hollis. Hollis asks a flat question, Ray answers with a story, Hollis asks the question again.
- **What he finds attractive in Ellen, plainly:** Her mouth when she's trying not to laugh, which happens more than she thinks. Her voice, which carries across ten lanes without being raised. Her bare hands; she's always taking her gloves off to do something. That she's bad at bowling in front of the whole town and doesn't pretend otherwise. That she argues him to a standstill and plainly enjoys it. *First thing he notices* (his POV, Ch 2, remembering Ch 1): when he knocked, she finished the strip of tape before she came down the ladder.
- **Syntax profile:**
  - *Sentence length and shape.* Dialogue: longer and looser than hers. Clauses chained with *and*, approaching sideways ("Thing is," "Funny thing about that table,"), with the important word last. Where Ellen uses full stops, Ray uses *and*. He asks questions he already knows the answer to ("That the K? That's been out a while, hasn't it?"). He rounds numbers ("fifty-some", "a couple thousand") and leaves the exact ones to her. Interior: plain and physical and patient. Long sentences that end on a short one. Memory slides in as a single clause, not a paragraph.
  - *Under pressure.* **Anger:** he stops talking. When he starts again it's short and formal: "Mrs. Daly." Five-word sentences, no *and*. **Fear:** he picks something up and fixes it, and talks to the object or the task instead of the person. **Hurt:** he goes quiet and leaves; if he speaks, it's one accurate sentence, and it's the cruelest thing in the book. **Tender:** he talks about her hands or whatever she's holding, never about her, and the plain word comes last.
  - *What he notices first.* Sound: a machine's knock, a table slipping, a furnace short-cycling, and he can hear them from the parking lot. Then wear: boot heels, the shine on a ball return, how somebody holds a tool, what a pair of gloves has been through. He reads people by what they've worn out.
  - *One habit of thought his alone.* **He dates time by what broke.** "The winter the K went." "The year seven's clutch let go." "Two pinsetters after Linda." He also gives machines, weather and the lake a *she* and a temper. Ellen never does either.
  - *His words, never Ellen's:* "supper", "down cellar", "dooryard", "fella", "some" as an intensifier ("some cold"), "she" for a machine, "I'd guess", "Might be", "about", "Mrs. Daly". "Holy mackerel" once (Ch 8). "Ayuh" at most once in the book. No phonetic dialect: no dropped g's spelled out. He never says "correct", "confirm", "understood", "noted", "I'm aware" or "I feel."
  - *Motif caps:* **the story about some other fella:** three in the book, and Ellen calls it out once (her flirting line 2). **The knuckle on the trophy glass:** three. **"Mrs. Daly" / "Ms.":** it runs as the book's joke and pays off in Ch 10 when she lets it stand, so give it no more than once a chapter. **"Linda wouldn't have wanted":** twice at most, both before Ch 7; Ch 10's "Linda would have entered on day one" reverses it.
- **Ten lines of dialogue:**
  - *Flirting*
    1. "Nice arc. Wrong lane."
    2. "I eat supper at a quarter past five, have for thirty years, and you've made me late twice this week, and I can't tell if you're doing it on purpose or you're just no good with a clock."
  - *Angry*
    3. "You sounded fine on the radio that night too, I bet."
    4. "Read paragraph four, Mrs. Daly. Take your time. It's short, and it's been sitting in your desk since September."
  - *Lying*
    5. "Didn't do it for you. Seven's been sulking since Arlene's time, and she was getting on my nerves."
    6. "Chair's there because nobody's moved it, is all."
  - *Afraid*
    7. "Hand me the nine-sixteenths. No, the other one. Keep talking, I'm listening, I just need something in my hands."
    8. "Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain, because I'd rather hear it from you than work it out by myself in the truck."
  - *Tender*
    9. "Give me your hands. No, keep going, you were winning."
    10. "Thirty years I've had the same supper and the same key and the same chair, and I'd let go of the lot to be the one you ask."
- **Five lines of interior narration:**
  1. The K had gone out the winter Danny got his license, and nobody fixed it, because Arlene said the sign still said what it meant.
  2. Seven had been sulking since Thanksgiving. You could hear it from the lot if you knew her.
  3. She held the flashlight steady, which most people can't, and close, which most people won't, and he could feel her breath on the back of his wrist like weather coming in off the lake.
  4. He hadn't wanted to watch anybody do anything in six years. He watched her write his answer down.
  5. He'd picked the line because it would land, and it landed, and he stood in her dark building and listened to it go on landing.
- **Three ordinary pleasures:**
  1. Supper at a quarter past five: American chop suey (elbow macaroni, hamburg, canned tomatoes, green pepper), the same pot, the same bowl, at the kitchen table with the radio on. It has never failed. (Pays off Ch 9 s1.)
  2. The first quarter-turn of a seized bolt giving. He feels it in his back teeth.
  3. The Bruins on the truck radio with the heater running, parked in his own dooryard, not going in yet.

## RELATIONSHIPS

### Ellen and Ray

- **What each finds attractive in the other:** see each lead's profile above (stated plainly there; repeated here only by reference so the two never drift apart).

  1. **The short version against the long way round.** She wants the answer to the question she asked; he answers with a fella in Tilton. He asks questions he knows the answers to; she tells him he knows the answer. "I wanted to hear how you'd put it."

### The strip-the-names test (for the dialogue-doctor and voice-editor)

Run on every chapter's final pass, with names and tags removed. The cues:

## WORLD & SETTING

**Time and place:** Ambrose, New Hampshire, an invented town in the Lakes Region on Great Kettle Lake (named for the glacial kettle that formed it). About 2,300 year-round residents, swelling to roughly 9,000 in summer. The story runs from Tuesday, December 1 to Monday, March 1. Weekdays match the 2026–27 calendar; the year is never named. Christmas happens off the page.

**Town geography (invented; never attach it to real state route numbers):**
- **Main Street** runs downhill to the town dock: Granite Lakes Savings Bank, Ambrose Pharmacy (Arlene's apartment upstairs), the barber, the town hall with plow-ban notices taped inside the door, and Gagnon & Son Hardware at the bottom near the dock.
- **Kettle Road** runs along the north shore. Kettle Lanes sits on a rise across from the town beach, a quarter mile out of the center. Ellen's rented camp is a mile farther out on the same road.
- **Dock Road and the public access:** where derby trucks park and Hollis measures the ice.
- **School Street:** up the hill behind Main; Ray's house.
- **Halsey Falls:** the next town over, forty minutes by the winter road. Pine Ridge Lanes.
- **Laconia** (real): the sign shop that bends the new K. **Concord** (real): Ray's lender and the condo man. **Manchester** (real): Kate. The Merrimack TRACON and Logan are real and offstage; mention them sparingly.

**Atmosphere:** A town in its off-season, keeping itself going. Summer cottages shuttered with plywood. Plow trucks at 4 a.m. Woodsmoke. A lake that turns into a neighborhood when it freezes: bob-houses in loose rows, trucks parked on the ice by people Hollis disapproves of. Everyone knows everyone's business, and most of them mean well about it. The cold is a working condition: shoveled, plowed, measured and complained about, and nobody calls it magical.

## WORLD RULES (ceiling: 20; each prohibition paired with what to do instead)

*Twenty rules. Rules that only require are marked (positive).*

1. **No swearing, of any kind.** No "damn," "hell," "crap," no "God," "Lord," "Jesus" as exclamations (including "Oh my God," "Jeez," "Geez," "Cripes," "Jeezum crow"), no censored swears, no "he cursed under his breath." — *Instead:* give each character their own substitute and use it at the moment of heat. Ellen: "Oh, for crying out loud" in real extremity, or understatement ("Well. That's suboptimal."). Ray: "For Pete's sake," "Son of a gun," "Holy mackerel" once (Ch 8). Hollis: "Wicked" as an intensifier. Arlene: "Voyons donc." Denise: "Heaven help us," sparingly. Anger shows as the register shift in each lead's syntax profile.
2. **Closed door, and no innuendo.** No nights together, on or off the page; no bedrooms; no "pins" or "balls" double meanings (check every bowling sentence). — *Instead:* put desire on the page as wanting, in every chapter: a hand that stays, a look held past the point of manners, a line said too close, a thought about the other that isn't about the plot. See HEAT RULES.
3. **No rescue money.** No windfall, inheritance, grant, viral fame, or money from Ray, Kate or anyone else; Ray never owns any of it. — *Instead:* trace every dollar of the margin to a decision Ellen made earlier, and make the end cost her: $8,800 of her own savings, said out loud.
4. **Nobody does skilled work they aren't qualified for.** — *Instead:* Ellen hires and holds the flashlight, and her competence shows as judgment (whom to call, what to say, in what order). Ray fixes pinsetters, rekeys locks, and mounts the K the Laconia shop built, with the sign's power off at the breaker and the shop's instructions in his pocket (one line). Hands-on work for Ellen is work anyone could do: setting pins by hand, digging out the sign, holding the ladder.
5. **No sentence states the theme or the meaning of the scene.** No closing aphorism, no moral, no "and that was enough." — *Instead:* end every scene on an action or a line of dialogue, using the ending type the outline assigns (question, cut, consequence, reversal, open image), and never two soft endings in a row.
6. **ATC phrases are capped.** At most one ATC phrase per scene in Ellen's dialogue and three per chapter (narration included); six per chapter in the three set pieces (Ch 1 PA, Ch 4 radio, Ch 9 position relief). Never two in one sentence; no acronyms (no "TRACON" more than once a chapter; never ATSAP, MOR or 7110.65). — *Instead:* use the approved vocabulary (cleared, unable, say again, stand by, say intentions, say request, affirmative, negative, hold short, sequence, number two, traffic twelve o'clock one mile, ground stop, readback, relief briefing, the D-side), make each one funny or exact because it's applied to bowling, and keep Ellen's interior thinking in order and numbers, never jargon. Other people mocking her phrases don't count against her cap but do count as the scene's texture.
7. **No codes, slash-strings or runs of figures on the page.** — *Instead:* write every figure in words a text-to-speech voice reads cleanly ("twenty-eight thousand nine hundred"), one money fact per chapter after Ch 2, and Ellen's labels as one or two words in capitals at most once a scene. Audit targets: codes under 2 per 1,000 words, numerals under 12, all-caps under 6.
8. **No recap at the top of a scene or chapter, and no re-staging what the reader already saw.** — *Instead:* open in motion at the outline's "Opens with" moment, inside someone's want. When one character tells another something the reader knows, give it one clause and stay on the listener's face. Quote "unless the numbers say otherwise" exactly each time it's quoted.
9. **Candlepin is never wrong and never a lecture.** — *Instead:* name the game in Ch 1 in one spoken line from Hollis, then explain a rule only when a scene turns on it. Use box, string, wood, sets, ten box, fill balls; lobs are called by an official. Give the sport's sound and feel (the crash like wooden spoons, the small heavy ball, the long lane) more than its rules.
10. **Texture is seasoning.** One or two pieces of trade, sport, money or procedure per scene, never a run of them. — *Instead:* pick the one that reveals the character or moves the romance (Ray naming a part by ear, Ellen's relief briefing), and give the rest of the scene to people talking.
11. **No metronome.** The same rhythm in a crisis and at supper is a failure. — *Instead (positive):* sentence length follows temperature, as in THE CORE. Long sentences are required at high points and in quiet interior passages; each POV chapter has at least three interior passages that run longer than the dialogue around them. Mean 13–18 words; five-word-or-shorter sentences under 15%.
12. **Every chapter carries a turn, a longing, a laugh and a cost. (positive)** The laugh is one line a specific person says at a specific target. The longing is attraction a reader would call attraction; business between the leads doesn't count. Every scene has a want the reader can feel, and each chapter visibly moves the money, the deadline or the town's opinion, in a scene.
13. **Ellen never states a false fact.** — *Instead:* when she evades, give her an exact true sentence that does the lying ("I said it."), and show the gap from inside her head in the same beat.
14. **Ellen doesn't win every round.** No competence beat settles an argument for her. — *Instead:* in every chapter, one exchange that matters ends with her wrong, without an answer, or paying for being right (paragraph four, the vote, the walkout, the evening refunds). The town pushes back for real.
15. **No convenient coincidences, and big wins never come from luck.** — *Instead:* when chance helps or hurts the leads, someone arranged it (Hollis puts Ellen on lane 5 because Ray is on 6), and every win pays off something a character did earlier.
16. **No villain, no death, no accident to move the plot.** — *Instead:* give every opponent a reason a reader would accept, in their own line (the captain's 3:10 call, Gil's lights, Judy's underwriter, Arlene's bed, Verna's minutes). Linda's death stays six years past.
17. **The calendar and the weather are fixed.** Two weather crises only (Dec 17, Feb 27); Feb 28's slush is a consequence, not a third. League nights are Tuesday and Thursday. Christmas is off the page. — *Instead:* make the cold a working condition, one detail per scene through what someone has to do about it (shovel, plow, measure, refund), and never open on weather unless the weather is the plot.
18. **Side characters don't speechify or swap voices.** One short speech each per chapter at most; no town monologues in turn. — *Instead:* give each one the line only they would say (their profile above), let the town talk in overlapping short lines, and run the strip-the-names test on every chapter.
19. **Running bits retire.** A gag that has landed three times is done; signature lines appear at most twice a chapter, and a chapter may skip them. — *Instead:* count against the MOTIF LIST, and when a bit is spent, change it (the radio joke becomes "Gagnon, say request" in Ch 10) or let a new detail carry the beat.
20. **No secrets beyond the one exception.** The only withheld fact is Ellen's (she watched Ray not hear, Dec 17 to Feb 2); the reader knows it throughout. — *Instead:* every other fact either lead holds reaches the other in the chapter it arises, and every complete thread (Denise's trophy, the teams, the derby, Kate, the key, the K) resolves on the page by Ch 10.

## HEAT RULES (romance)

- **Level:** clean, closed door, from the brief. No swearing (WORLD RULES 1). No alcohol scenes: the snack bar is dry, New Year's Eve is glow night with sparkling cider, Pine Ridge's lounge stays in the background. No affairs or flirtation with anyone married; Denise and Gil are both single. No mockery of faith.
- **Kisses: three, at the outline's positions.** The optional fourth at the ladder stays off. No kiss in the rupture chapter (Ch 7).
  1. **Ch 3, dawn, under the dead K (~29%).** Ellen moves first. *Changes:* rivals become two people who have kissed, and she kisses him knowing something he doesn't. Her wrong and her want arrive in the same minute. It ends with Hollis's plow and Denise in the door, so the town has seen it.
  2. **Ch 5, midnight, New Year's Eve, the pit behind lane 7 (~42%).** He finds her, and for a whole minute doesn't say Happy New Year. *Changes:* the first kiss could have been the storm; this one is chosen by both, sober, in a building full of people. It makes the vote five days later a public betrayal in both directions.
  3. **Ch 10, the declaration, the pit behind lane 7 (~94%).** After the words, never instead of them. *Changes:* from terms to words. He says it his way, few words, the important ones last; she answers in hers, plainly, with no list. "Both words." may close it.
- **The longing rule (genre map):** attraction on the page in every chapter from Ch 1, in a form the reader would call attraction (a touch that lasts, a look held, a line said too close, a thought about the other that isn't about the plot). Business between the leads doesn't count. Clean is a content rule, not a register. Each chapter's assigned longing is in the outline; Ch 7 carries it as wanting she refuses (the hood) and his shoulder an inch from hers at the counter.
- **Vocabulary allowed:** "kissed," "kissed him back," mouths named at most once per kiss, hands (on a face, the back of a neck, inside a coat), gloves, breath, cold, a sound someone makes, who moves first, the line said after. One embrace per kiss scene. Attraction is noticing: his hands, height and voice; her mouth, voice and bare hands; competence; a laugh. **Never:** tongues, bodies pressed together, heat or fire as a metaphor for desire, moans, any body part below the collarbone in a romantic beat.
- **Floor per scene:** each scene that contains a kiss runs at least 800 words, and the kiss beat itself (approach, kiss, the first line after) gets at least 150 words on the page, played in real time. Never a one-line kiss, never cut to aftermath, never forgotten in the next scene. No kiss sentence repeats an earlier one.

### High-temperature sample (Ch 3, dawn, Ellen POV: the first kiss)

Ray had handed her a grain scoop with a cracked handle and kept the good shovel for himself, and she was using the scoop badly, and he was letting her, which she meant to hold against him once she had a free minute. They had dug the drift off the north side of the sign pole down to frozen gravel. Over the lake the sky had gone from black to the gray of a radar screen with nothing on it, and behind the glass every person she had stranded was asleep in a parka on a burnt-orange settee, in the order she had put them in at nine o'clock last night. For twenty minutes she had not checked on a single one of them. She noticed that now, standing in the trench with the scoop in her bare hands, and it frightened her more than the power going out had.

"You've got your gloves off again," Ray said. He leaned the shovel against the pole. "Every time I look over you've taken them off to do something, and it's never anything a glove would get in the way of, and I've about decided you just like your hands cold."

"I like to know what I'm holding."

"Give them here, then."

She gave them to him. He opened his canvas jacket and put both her hands inside it, flat against the flannel over his ribs, and held them there with his own on the outside, and she stood in the trench they had dug and felt the cold go out of her fingers one at a time, and she meant to tell him first that Hollis would be up the drive within the hour and second that Denise would be awake before that and third that there was a sentence from the pit he had not heard and needed to, and instead she was looking at the snow caught in his eyebrows, and the gray along his jaw, and his mouth, which had told her about Linda's best night in the dark in a voice so careful she had wanted to put her hand over it, and none of it was in any order, and she knew it, and she left it that way.

"Ellen," he said, in the voice she had been listening to all night over the noise of the machines, low and unhurried, a three-in-the-morning voice with the whole sky going gray behind it. She took one hand out of his coat and put it on the back of his neck, because she wanted to, and left the other where it was, over his heart, because it was warm there. Then she came up on the toes of her snow boots and kissed him under the sign, with the dead K over both of them. He made a sound like a man setting down something heavy he had carried a long way. His hand came up to the side of her face and stayed there, bare and warm and smelling of pin grease, and he kissed her back slowly, taking his time over it the way he took his time over anything he meant to keep, until the plow's yellow light came swinging up off Kettle Road and lit the snow around their boots.

### Quiet sample (Ch 9 s1, Ellen POV: supper at a quarter past five)

She knocked at fourteen minutes past five, and Ray let her stand on the back step until a quarter past, and then opened the door and told her so. His kitchen was small and too warm and painted a yellow somebody had picked out around 1979 and nobody had argued with since. A radio on top of the refrigerator was reading the Bruins' lineup to the canisters. There were two bowls set out on an oilcloth table that she suspected was used to one, and on the stove a dented aluminum pot whose lid sat crooked, and the whole room smelled of ground beef and green pepper and canned tomatoes cooked down past the point where anyone could call them sauce.

"American chop suey," he said, before she could ask. "Elbow macaroni and the rest of it, in the same pot since the pot before this one gave out, the winter the furnace went. I'd tell you it was Lucien's recipe, and he'd have liked that, and it's on the side of the macaroni box."

She sat before he asked her to. She did not sit in other people's kitchens until she knew where everything was, and she did not know where anything was in this one, and he saw her do it and said nothing, and set the bowl down in front of her with the care she had watched him use on a clutch spring. Under the table his boot touched her left knee, and he moved it at once and asked what she had done to it. She told him. A ski fall in 2009, on a trail she had no business being on, and the ride down in the patrol's toboggan, and the orthopedist who had said *at your age* to a woman of forty-one, and she told it in order, all of it, which she had done for no one else, and he laughed at the toboggan and nowhere else, and that was the right place.

"What do you make the second night?" she said.

"That one."

"And the third."

"Same pot." He moved the salt an inch to the left for no reason she could find. "It's never once failed me, and I don't see why I'd start taking chances at my age."

It was good, and she said so, and he took the compliment the way he took one on a repair, by looking at the pot. Outside the window over the sink it was full dark at twenty to six, and a little snow was coming down into his dooryard under the porch light, and the radio had gone on to the weather, and she let it go on without her. "That's a good supper," she said, and heard which word had come out of her mouth, and decided to leave it there.
