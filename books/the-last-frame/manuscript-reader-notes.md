# Manuscript reader notes: The Last Frame

**Draft read:** `books/the-last-frame/manuscript.md`, Oct 29 2026, the first full draft (ten chapters, 2,273 lines). I read it start to finish in one sitting and opened nothing else in the folder.

**Warning above the scores:** I finished it, but the way a reader finishes a book she has decided to be fair to, not one she couldn't put down. There were two places where I picked up my phone (positions below). A reader who had already been bored by two quiet books would most likely leave in Chapter 4 and not come back.

## Where I would have quit

**Most likely, for good: Chapter 4, the office scene, around line 796–830.**
> "Second," Ellen said. "Paragraph four says 'as the league's bylaws provide.'"
> "And what do the bylaws provide?"
> "Third. I asked Verna for them the day after I closed."

By this point I had sat through the contract, the credit union, the Concord bank's hold fee, and now a 2004 lineage notice and a bylaw clause. The radio stuff earlier in the chapter was the most fun I'd had since the pit, and the chapter then shut me in an office to go through the paperwork. It's also the third "First/Second/Third" in a row from her, and here it is used on a bylaw.

**Second likeliest: Chapter 2, the clock, lines 370–382.**
> "He read the loan officer's number on the slip beside the phone, all ten digits, and read them again."

A full minute of a clock, a key and a damp form, and I couldn't say what was at stake. She had already told him no. So what did the call do? The scene asks for suspense I wasn't equipped to feel. "That's the bell," Normie said, is a nice button on a scene I didn't follow.

## Where I got bored (the three worst passages, with positions)

1. **Ch 4, the office, lines 782–830.** "Then you will want to know what your league costs me," she said. "Seven hundred dollars every week it bowls." From there it is rates, the 2004 notice, paragraph four, bylaws, the January meeting. Two people who want each other, a foot apart in a closet-sized office, and they talk league finance.
2. **Ch 5, the meeting, lines 949–991.** "The secretary will read the bylaws in full," Verna said, and did, dues and nights and lob line. Then Ray: "The credit union would've lent me against the store to buy this place off her clean, and I let their five o'clock go by on the third of December without making the call, and the other loan's with the bank in Concord..." A recap of financing, read out loud to a room.
3. **Ch 8, Arlene's kitchen into the release signing, lines 1644–1754.** "I want off the March paper. I'll have your lawyer draw whatever it takes." Then "That puts me a hundred and fifteen dollars under thirty thousand, with every lane of the Open full. The Open is my whole margin, and the weather owns the Open. Page two refunds every entry in cash if weather stops play..." The emotional beat (he wants to stand beside her, not at the bottom of her drive) is buried under a release form.

Smaller drifts:
- Ch 1, line 49: the census of the room ("First, the ones moving... Next, the ones who had stopped...").
- Ch 6, lines 1304–1340: the bank teller scene.
- Ch 9, lines 1913–1923: the refund envelopes, one through ten.
- Ch 10, lines 2180–2240: the bank, the notary snag, the wire.

The pattern: every time the book has a choice between two people in a room and a document in a room, it gives me the document.

## Where I got confused (across chapters)

- **The money machinery.** I had to hold all of this: Arlene's $250K due March 1. Ray's backstop contract that buys it the same afternoon if she misses. A Granite Lakes loan that needs the winter to "clear thirty thousand, with every dollar it makes still sitting in the account." A credit union loan against the store that expires at five on Dec 3. A Concord bank commitment good to New Year's, held to March for a fee. A cashier's check worth one dollar off the target for every eight put on the note. A release. A notarization. At the end she is "seventeen hundred and fifteen under," and then "Eighty-eight hundred, out of your savings." I could not get from one number to the other. A listener has no chance.
- **The key on the counter at five o'clock (Ch 2).** Bribe, gift, answer, dare? Ch 3 half-explains it ("It was a no to selling." / "The rest of it was a key."), but in the moment I didn't know what I was watching.
- **"The sign, Mrs. Daly. You knew." (Ch 7, line 1473).** I took "the sign" to be the neon sign. It took me a beat to work out he meant the kiss under the sign: she knew he hadn't heard the clause when she kissed him. Then "New Year's too?" and I had to rebuild it again.
- **Kate's speaker call (Ch 10, lines 2034–2050).** "Then do it." / "And call me first next time." / "Like this." / "I'd have said no." / "You weren't asked." I can't tell what "Like this" means. I think Ray's "I'd have said no" means he'd have refused her the money, but I'm guessing.
- **Who won the Memorial.** "The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins." Then Denise sits, folds her hands, and later takes the cup out of the case. Is Denise on Lakeview Diner? The book told me early that she wanted that cup, but never told me her team. I worked it out. I shouldn't have had to.
- **The second betting sheet at Gagnon's (Ch 4).** "A dollar a square... Nothing on it said what it was for... Valentine's is gone... D.G." Danny bet on Valentine's for... the two of them? Never paid. The first sheet ("When she quits or loses it") gets one glance at the end ("every square pencilled in but the last") and I had to infer that meant nobody won.
- **Kate's first call (Ch 2).** "The house." / "Not the rest." / "I swear." It pays off in Ch 5 and Ch 9, so it's a fair plant. But for three chapters it was just noise.
- **Lines 883–885 (Ch 5):** two Kate lines in a row with nothing between them. I thought I had misread the speaker.
- **Normie disappears into "the man from the Bench's end stool" / "the one off the end stool" in Ch 8.** I wondered if it was a different person.

## Where I stopped believing

- **The drowned clause.** Denise's strike lands exactly on "Unless," in a pit, fifteen inches away. That's the kind of coincidence a book gets once, and this one builds its whole second half on it. I would have accepted it if Ellen had cleared it up within a day. She doesn't.
- **Ellen's silence at the vote (Ch 5).** The book's central fact about her is that she writes up every error herself before anyone asks. Then she stands in a room while the man she kissed on New Year's puts her half-sentence into the minutes as a promise. Asked directly, "Does the owner wish to respond?", she says "No." That same night she plans the Open around Linda's name. The book gives a reason (it would cost her him), but the obviously safer option, telling him privately in any of six nights in the second chair, is never closed. She just doesn't. This is where she stopped being the competent woman I signed up for and became someone running out the clock.
- **Using Linda's name for margin.** A controller who prides herself on her word puts a dead woman's name on a fundraising flyer six days after promising the widower it "stays." The text admits it ("She was taking Linda's name for the margin"). I believed she'd do the arithmetic. I did not believe she'd do it this way, to this man, without saying a word first.
- **The Pine Ridge match (Ch 8).** Dale, who has been hard all winter, agrees to let a three-string grudge match decide where his crew bowls. Gil, who gains two teams, puts them up as stakes for an evening's entertainment. It's a sports climax rented for the middle of the book.
- **Arlene signing the release (Ch 8).** An eighty-four-year-old who has spent the book protecting her bed at The Pines gives up her guarantee because the two of them "have been sitting in cars." It's a lovely speech and an unlikely decision. Her own suggestion, that Ray keep it in his drawer and tear it up after, is the safer option, and the book closes it only with Ray's feelings.
- **The notarization snag (Ch 10).** A loan officer who knows everyone discovers on the last afternoon that the release was never notarized. It's there to give the bank scene a clock.
- **Ray never hears "unless" for a month and nobody repeats it.** Denise asks Ellen "Who'd you say that to?" in early January and lets it go. In a town where the Bench reads Ray's outgoing mail, nobody asks again.

## Where it sounded like a machine

- **The enumerations.** "First... Second... Third" is Ellen's tic, and the book uses it as a structural device: Ch 1 (bowling steps, room count), Ch 3 (the two reasons, and the third held back), Ch 4 (the bylaws), Ch 5 (her three items at the meeting), Ch 7 ("First. Linda's name was for room."), Ch 9 (the relief briefing headings), Ch 10 ("Third. I love you."). By Ch 7 I flinched when she said "First."
- **The run-on "the order went out of it" paragraph.** Ch 1 line 99 ("she knew it had all come out of order and left it that way"), Ch 3 line 577 ("somewhere back around the motor the order had gone out of it, and she heard that it had, and she let it run"), Ch 7 line 1373 ("none of it would take a slot, and she let it come in whatever order it came"). The same move every time desire arrives.
- **Feeling explained after the gesture.** Line 35: "and none of that was in any order she could use." Line 129: "which she would have to think about later and in private." Line 350: "owing him, which sat on her about as well as a wet coat." Line 917: "She heard herself use the short words with him for the first time since the sign, and she did not take them back." Line 1387: "It was true, and it was the sentence she could say. The item under it was her word, status broken, unreported." Line 1869: "For the first time in her working life she let a rounded number stand."
- **Scenes ending on a neat line.** Almost every one: "Nobody," Denise said. "Ray won't." / "That's the bell," Normie said. / "Honey." / "Unable." / "I know what time it is." / "I'm walking out to your bob-house Saturday." / "Page two." / "Then I keep my commitment." / "She put it in her own vest pocket." / "Coffee's on." / "Not yet." / "Ten." / "Both words." Each one is good. Thirty of them in a row make the book feel built rather than lived.
- **Ray's motor comparisons.** "the way a motor runs fast before it's warmed through," "the small change you hear in a motor the second before it catches," "the way he heard a pinsetter from the lot when a clutch was about to go," "the way he'd seat a part he meant to keep," "quicker than any motor on the catwalk." The book even gives a heartbeat a motor comparison twice. It's his POV, fine, but every emotion gets the same one.
- **Line 1419:** "On the boards an inch would have been a loss of separation, and she would have written it up before morning." The ATC metaphor is pressed onto a shoulder.
- **"She filed it."** (Ch 1 twice, Ch 3, Ch 5.)
- **Everybody says "some" New England.** Ray, Denise, Arlene and Normie all have the same rhythm of long, comma-spliced, folksy sentences. Denise and Normie are distinct. Arlene and Ray blur together.

## Predictability

I knew how it would end by the last line of Chapter 1: she makes March first, the K gets lit, he gets a key that works, and the pool's last square stays blank. That's the genre's contract, and fine. What I minded is that by Chapter 3, line 539 ("*No readback.*"), I also knew the shape of the middle: the clause would come out, he'd feel betrayed, she'd confess, they'd reconcile. It went exactly there.

What surprised me after Chapter 3:
- Ellen saying "It is not yours" in front of the room (Ch 7). I expected Ray to find out privately.
- Ellen sending everyone home and refunding the Open in the storm (Ch 9). I expected another overnight lock-in to save her.
- Ray losing the Memorial by two pins with Linda's ball. That was the best decision in the back half.
- Linda's sheet with a bad box on it.

Nothing surprised me about the money: once a book shows you a number, the heroine makes it.

## What the book is about, in one sentence

A retired air traffic controller who can't trust her own calm voice buys a failing candlepin alley in a New Hampshire lake town, and has to learn to say the true thing out of order to the widowed locksmith who is contracted to take it off her if she fails.

## The romance

**Did I want them together?** Yes, more in the first half than the second. The best stretch is Ch 2 to Ch 4: the light going ahead of his hands, the pit, the radio. After the clause comes out, the book spends a long time with them apart, communicating through legal pads, invoices, a letter read to the league and a release form, and I stopped feeling the pull between them and started watching the paperwork move.

**The kisses.**
- Under the sign (Ch 3): earned. She closes the distance herself after a night of setting pins. "Your hands are warm now. That's all I was after." / "Noted." is lovely. But I knew she was kissing him on top of a lie, which spoiled it a little in hindsight, as the book intends.
- New Year's behind seven (Ch 5): the best one. Him not saying Happy New Year for a minute, on purpose. "It's four minutes past." / "I know what time it is." Earned.

**The declaration.** "Third." She stopped on it, a heading with nothing yet under it... "I love you." Then he reads it back: "Third item. You love me." It pays off the held-back third reason from Ch 3 exactly, and that's the problem. It's a callback designed seven chapters earlier and delivered like a bow on a box. I admired it more than I felt it. His "since about the first night, when you kept me knocking at your door to finish your tape" is the same thing, another callback. "Twice behind lane seven and once under the sign. I've kept count." Written, not said. The D-side briefing in Ch 9 ("I keep the position. I want you on it with me") is the real declaration, and it's dressed up in procedure.

## The ending

**Did it pay the promises?** All of them, every last one: the K, the number, Arlene's bed, Linda's locker, Linda's ball onto the house rack, the cup (to Denise), Kate's blessing, the key on his ring, the pool's last square. Nothing was left open. Nothing cost anyone more than money. Ray losing the Memorial is the one place the ending refuses to give him what he wants, and it's the best choice in Ch 10.

**Scene or ceremony?** Ceremony. Ch 10 is a procession: locker opened, ball bowled, cup handed over, "I love you" in item form, a bank signing with a manufactured snag, Arlene's key returned, her key put on his ring with "Will you take it?", the sign thrown. Each beat is the payoff of a plant, in order, like a checklist. "Both words." is a genuinely good last line, and I'd have felt it more if I hadn't watched nine other doors close first.

## Character I will remember

**Denise.** "Those shoes get sprayed toe and heel, every pair, every time..." / "He's a hundred-and-four average with a hundred-and-forty heart. Don't tell him I said either." / "I lost to that ball twice, Ray Gagnon, and the second time was worse, because she was nice about it." She talks like nobody else in the book. Then Hollis, who says almost nothing and means all of it ("Hollis said nothing, which from Hollis was a measurement."). Ray and Ellen I'll remember as a premise more than as people.

## What I want to know next

Nothing. The book ties off every thread. Partway through, the real question was whether Ellen would tell him before he found out, and that was answered in Ch 6. From Ch 7 on, the only open question was whether she makes the number, and the genre had answered that on page one. From the midpoint on I was reading to confirm, not to find out. That is the most important finding in these notes.

---

## Recommend?

**Yes, narrowly.** To a reader of quiet literary small-town fiction (Strout, Haruf, Russo's gentler books) who wants a sixty-something heroine, competence on the page, and a romance that runs on gestures over heat. **No** for a general romance reader or anyone listening on audio. They'll leave in the first meeting or the first spreadsheet.

**Would I buy the next book by this author?** Not on the name. I'd read the sample. The sentences are good and the minor characters are alive. I'd want proof the next one has a plot that runs on people rather than paperwork.

**Star rating: 2.5 / 5.** Treat it as a ceiling, not a floor. I finish everything, and I enjoyed the pit, the radio and the glove scene. A reader with other books on her phone rates this 2 to 2.5.

## The one outline-level change

Take the money machinery out of the spine. Right now the plot runs on a revenue threshold, three lenders, a backstop contract, a lineage rate, a bylaw, an eight-to-one multiplier, a release and a notarization. Each turn of the story is a number moving, and the reader can't hold the numbers, so the turns don't land. Give the book one stake a listener can hold in a sentence, so the obstacle between them is what they want from each other rather than what the bank wants from her. As written, the misheard clause makes the middle of the book a secret one character keeps for a month while the other attends meetings. If her word had come out between them in Chapter 4 or 5, face to face, the middle would be two people fighting instead of a ledger waiting to be read aloud.
