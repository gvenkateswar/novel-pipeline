# Manuscript Reader Notes: *The Last Frame* (Chapters 1–10)

Cold read, in order, of the ten `final.md` files only. Edit logs ignored.

---

## Overall verdict

**4 stars.** I'd recommend it, but I'd pick who to hand it to. It suits readers who like quiet, competent people, small-town procedure, and romance built from restraint: fans of Olive Kitteridge-adjacent small-town fiction, older readers, anyone who has worked a job with checklists. It is not for readers who want heat, banter, or a lot of interiority.

What earns the 4: the book is specific. Candlepin, flight strips, the R.G. card, the ledgers in green ink, the pool sheet at the hardware store, Verna's minutes. Everything has a texture, and almost nothing is generic. Ellen's air-traffic voice is a real idea, and the book follows it all the way through: the voice that "could carry anything," the Merrimack key, the key in the car in Ch 7, and "D-side" in Ch 9.

What costs the fifth star: the money is too hard to follow, the romance has too little warmth on the page, and nearly every character talks in the same clipped way. By Ch 6 the restraint stopped feeling like style and started to feel like a rule the author was following.

## Characters

**Ellen and Ray together:** Mostly believable. The pairing works because they speak the same language of procedure: readbacks, cards, invoices, tape. The book is best when that shared language turns intimate. Examples: the R.G. card, "Not mine. Checked anyway," the readback of "I share the position." Their arguments are real arguments, and both of them have a point.

What's missing is warmth between the set pieces. Before the Ch 5 kiss they've had a business exchange, a handshake, one lesson, and one night in the pit. The kiss lands because of the pit scene in Ch 4, but only barely. I never saw them simply enjoying each other's company, eating together, or doing something unrelated to the building. Ch 10's declaration also tips into explaining:

> "If I had taken that check, you would always have wondered whether it was you I wanted or the fifty-five thousand dollars. You told me you would have known it was you. I believe you. I would have spent the rest of my life running that tape..."

That's a summary of Ch 7, delivered as a speech. Ray's version ("I eat supper at a quarter past five... I love you.") is much better because it's specific and sideways.

**Side characters that work:**
- **Denise.** The book's engine. "You'll live," "Honey, you don't move the lunch ladies," her broken speech holding the trophy. She's the only character who talks at length, and the book needs her for it.
- **Verna.** Every ruling is a delight. "The chair will not go into the brother." "Three minutes and four seconds. The chair let the four go."
- **Hollis.** "Purpose of visit" is a great running device, and the ice-measuring scene is a clean piece of character.
- **Arlene.** "I know where the margins are, Raymond. I kept them." The ham, *Voyons donc*.
- **Normie and the Bench.** The play-by-play is funny and gives Ray's world a sound.

**Side characters that don't quite work:**
- **Danny** is efficient and likable but has only one note: the dry, competent son. The store handoff in Ch 8 should hit harder than it does, because we barely know what the store means to Ray beyond Linda's stool.
- **Kate** is underused. She's a phone voice in Ch 2 and a brisk nurse in Ch 9. "I'm thirty-one, and I've never been on the strip board" is a good line with nothing behind it.
- **The 112** stays a label. He's fine as an antagonist, but his last line in Ch 8 ("you picked the Daly woman over the league") wants a person behind it.
- **Judy** is good in her two scenes, but her trout cameo in Ch 9 ("I'm here as a fisherman today") is cute in a way that feels arranged.
- **Linda** is felt everywhere and seen almost nowhere. The 187 story in Ch 4 is the only time she becomes a person.

## Repetition (across the whole book)

These came back often enough that I started noticing them as tics:

- **"Mrs. Daly." / "Ms."** Seven or more times. The Ch 8 flip to "Ms. Daly" and the Ch 10 "Fourteen minutes, Mrs. Daly" are good payoffs, but they'd land harder with half as many setups.
- **Ray touching the trophy glass with one knuckle.** Ch 1, 2, and 4, plus standing at the case in Ch 8 and 9.
- **Ray's key ring.** Turned over once, fist closed, jingle against the orange tag in Ch 1, 2, and 6.
- **The sign buzzing on its pole.** Nearly every chapter.
- **The baseboard ticking.** Ch 2, 3, 5, 6, and 9.
- **The popcorn machine and space heater tripping the breaker.** Ch 3, 4, 5, and 9.
- **"Pine Ridge is forty minutes."** Ch 4, 6, 7, 8, and 9.
- **The number 22.** Denise's 22 years, the Highway man's 22 years of Thursdays (said twice, in Ch 4 and Ch 7), Hollis's 22 derbies, 22 inches of snow. The number is starting to feel like a tic.
- **Hollis taking his cap off and putting it back on.** Ch 1, 6, 7, and 9.
- **Verna's "lavender parka zipped to the chin."** Nearly word-for-word every time she appears.
- **Ellen counting the room under her breath.** Every chapter. It's character, but it's also wallpaper by Ch 6.
- **Ellen's "I'm aware," "Negative," "Understood."** Charming in Ch 1, a verbal tic by Ch 5.
- **Someone reading something upside down.** Ray, Normie, and Denise, many times.
- **Denise doing something "without looking up" from the crossword.** Too many to count.
- **The dime in the screw slot.** Ch 4 and Ch 8.
- **"Low. Let the lane have it." / "The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."** Paid off twice, by Ray at Pine Ridge in Ch 8 and by Ellen in the final in Ch 9. The second payoff felt mechanical.
- **Ellen's first ball sailing past the lob line and coming down twice.** Ch 3 and Ch 9, in nearly identical words.
- **Pauses.** "He didn't say anything." "She didn't answer." "He looked at it a while." These are the book's default response beats, and nearly every character uses them. After a while everyone sounded the same: Ray, Ellen, Hollis, Judy, and Danny all speak in short, dry, withholding lines. Only Denise, Normie, Verna, and the raffle woman break the pattern.

## Continuity (things that contradict across chapters)

1. **The commitment letter's fold.** Ch 2 has it "folded in quarters behind his driver's license." Ch 8 has it "folded in thirds."
2. **The Tuesday lane count after the walkout.** Ch 1 sets Tuesday at 40 bowlers on 10 lanes, so ten teams. Taylor's Marina and the Legion (both Tuesday teams, Ch 1) leave in Ch 7. That should leave eight teams on eight lanes, but Ch 7 says "Tuesday had bowled on nine lanes, one team against a blind score." An odd number of teams doesn't add up.
3. **Arlene's reason for picking Ellen (Ch 8).** She tells Ray, "That one raised the price her first winter and lost two teams for it. I'll take my money from somebody who does that." But she sold in the fall, before either thing happened. Read literally, it's a reason after the fact. Ch 10 then gives a different answer ("You asked me the name of my boiler man"). The Ch 10 answer is the better one, and the Ch 8 line muddies it.
4. **Hollis's surname.** It appears for the first time in Ch 9 ("Mr. Pike called the ice"). I had to stop and work out who Mr. Pike was.
5. **Gil and Pine Ridge.** Gil gets the surname "Arsenault" and Pine Ridge gets the town "Halsey Falls" only in Ch 9, after five chapters of "Pine Ridge."
6. **Denise in the final.** Nothing earlier says Denise bowls for the Lakeview Diner. In Ch 9, "I don't leave the counter in a rush... Who else knows the refund sheet?" reads at first as if she's being drafted for Gagnon & Son. Then she's on lane 6 for the opposing team. I reread the passage twice.
7. **Vacation week.** "VAC WK 02/22–26 . . . 3,900.00" appears on the final tape out of nowhere. In Ch 5 Ellen says February has "no parties" and nothing like the push. A school vacation week bringing in $3,900 is never set up, and it's a big part of why she clears the bar. It reads as convenient.
8. **The 23,950 vs. 24,000 math in Ch 7.** I couldn't tell what either number measured. "Fifty dollars" then becomes Ellen's key emotional line in the car, and I wasn't sure what it referred to.
9. **Small check.** Ch 7 says Ray coached Gagnon & Son "since 1996," but in Ch 1 he bowls on the team too. That's not a contradiction, but "coach" and "bowler" keep blurring.

Dates and days of the week held up everywhere I checked: Dec 1 Tuesday, Dec 11 Friday, Jan 7 Thursday, Jan 16 Saturday, Jan 26 Tuesday, Feb 16 Tuesday, Feb 27 Saturday, and the ice-out margin of two days, 21 hours, 10 minutes. Ch 9's tape ($30,850 / $25,600) and Ch 10's split ($22,140 + $3,460 = $25,600) also agree.

## Opening and ending

**Opening:** It hooks. The first line ("The deed had carried Ellen's name since nine-forty that morning...") puts me in the story right away. Ellen's ball jumping into lane 6 and wiping out Ray's leave is a great meet-hostile scene. The pool sheet at the end ("KETTLE LANES. NEW OWNER. HOW LONG?") gives me a clock and a town in one image. The risk is the jargon in the first three pages (lineage, foul row, flight-strip columns, lob line). A browsing reader who doesn't know candlepin may feel shut out before the ball is thrown.

**Ending:** It pays off. The safe-versus-statement problem is a smart, character-true final hurdle: she's beaten by a technicality and saved by her own HELD account discipline. The release dated the 16th is the right reveal, and it's quietly done. "I'd like to be asked in" is the best line Ray has. The coda pays off the dead K from page one: "Both words." It's neat, maybe too neat, but the book has earned one tidy bow.

What the ending lacks is a reckoning with Linda. "A record was a record" is told secondhand in one sentence. Ray bowling Linda's ball in the final is written as "Ray unzipped the blue bag on the settee and bowled the final with Linda's ball," and then we never see him bowl it. That's the moment I most wanted on the page, and the book walks past it.

## For clean small-town romance readers with an older heroine

**What it delivers:**
- A 56-year-old heroine with a real career behind her, and her competence is the point.
- A widowed hero whose grief is handled with dignity.
- No sex, no swearing beyond "For Pete's sake" and "Holy mackerel."
- Three kisses, all earned and all specific (the flashlight on the ditch, the fistful of canvas, the knee "like a cap pistol").
- A town full of eccentrics, and a happy ending on the page.

**What's missing:**
- **Heart on the page.** This audience usually wants to feel the heroine's longing. Ellen's interiority is almost all about her calm and her voice, and almost never about what she wants from Ray. The first time she says it is in Ch 10.
- **Female friendship and family.** Denise is close, but the book never lets Ellen and Denise just talk. Kate's relationship with her mother is two scenes.
- **Banter that sparkles.** The ATC jokes are good ("Intentions are to quit") but rare. Most exchanges are clipped and guarded, which reads as cool rather than cozy.
- **A proposal or a wedding.** The wedding-date square teases it. Romance readers will want the scene where she asks him, since the book sets up that she will.
- **Coziness.** No meals, no kitchens, no one's home. Almost the whole book happens at work.

## Where I'd most likely have stopped

Ch 3, at the bank, if I were browsing in a store. Two pages of underwriting arithmetic before anything happens. If I got past that, I'd have finished. If I quit mid-book, it would be in Ch 8, where the heroine disappears for most of the chapter and the scenes don't build.

## Character I'll remember

Verna Tibbetts, honestly, then Denise. Ellen and Ray are well made, but Verna is the one I'd quote to a friend.

## Best moment

Ch 4, under the table on lane 5:

> Ellen was squaring the 10 pin on its spot. She didn't look up.
>
> "Traffic, twelve o'clock, one mile."
>
> He laughed until he had to put a hand down flat on the boards to stay sitting up.

## Top 5 revisions, in priority order

1. **Make the money trackable.** There are too many figures: $2,308/wk, $6,333, 88%, $11,800, $700/wk, $26,000, $19,000, $24,500, $55,000, $23,950, $22k/$17k, $30,850/$25,600, $22,140. Give the reader one running number to watch. Set up the February vacation week before it saves her. Make Ray's check math and "Fifty dollars" readable.
2. **Put more warmth and longing between Ellen and Ray on the page.** Add at least one non-work scene before the Ch 5 kiss, and let Ellen's interior voice want him before Ch 10. Then cut Ch 10's pit declaration down. Her explanation of Ch 7 should be shown, not recited.
3. **Fix the final in Ch 9.** Set up Denise bowling for the Lakeview Diner. Put Ray bowling Linda's ball on the page. Use only one "wood stays on the lane" payoff (Ch 8 or Ch 9, not both).
4. **Cut the recurring tics and vary the voices.** The trophy knuckle, the key ring, Mrs./Ms., "I'm aware," the baseboard, the sign buzz, the breaker, "Pine Ridge is forty minutes," Hollis's cap, Verna's parka, "without looking up," the number 22. Let at least Ray, Judy, and Danny talk differently from Ellen.
5. **Fix continuity and restructure Ch 8.** Letter folded in quarters vs. thirds. Tuesday's nine lanes after two teams leave. Arlene's after-the-fact reasoning in Ch 8. Hollis "Pike," Gil "Arsenault," and "Halsey Falls" introduced too late. Bring Ellen into Ch 8 earlier so it isn't a Ray-only montage.
