# Manuscript Reader Notes: The Last Frame

**Draft read:** `books/the-last-frame/manuscript.md`, v3 r1, 31,110 words, Chapter 1 through the Epilogue, dated Oct 30, 2026. I read nothing else in the book folder.

**Read as:** a buyer who has a phone full of other books and has already been bored by two books from this shop.

---

## Boredom flags first

- **I'd keep reading, but I knew where every thread was going.** From Chapter 3 on, every major payoff (the third item, the K, the key, the locker, Kate's money, the storm) was visible at least two chapters ahead. Most of them showed up looking exactly as planted, often with the same words. What pulled me was the texture. It was not suspense.
- **Chapter 6 is where I would have put the book down for good.** That chapter is a release form, a credit-union form, a three-way signing order and a cribbage anecdote. The first explanation of the contract was the third time I'd heard it (Ch 1, Ch 4, now Ch 6), and it was the most confusing of the three.
- **Five chapters score 5 or lower** (Ch 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, and the Epilogue at 4).
- **The climax happens offstage.** The book's last open question is whether the bank says yes, and it gets settled by a man's voice on Judy's phone while Ellen stands at a glass wall.

## Where I drifted, where I'd have quit, where I came back

**Drift 1. Ch 4, opening (~25%).**
> "Yesterday at your counter you told the Bench, 'Linda's night stays. I've got her word.' My word had two parts, and you heard one."

I had just watched them kiss in a trench under a red sign, and the next scene is a correction about a subordinate clause. It's in character, but it plays like a deposition. "Read it back." "Correct." By the time we reach "thirty hours and nineteen minutes" I'm admiring the precision, not feeling anything.

**Drift 2. Ch 4, staff party (~30%).**
> "A thing said in front of the Bench was said. You didn't get it back; they kept everything they heard on a shelf in their heads, dated, and handed it down to you a year later in front of company."

The paragraph that follows packs Lucien, the drawer count, Danny, retirement, and "something else, to do with the hours after supper" into one block, and none of it happens on the page. I skimmed.

**Where I'd have put it down for good. Ch 6, Ray at the store counter with Danny (~45%).**
> "I signed that so you could buy it on her yes. You're standing in her paper waiting on her no, and I won't send my name in on that. Half out's not out."

By here I had heard the contract explained by Ray (Ch 1), Ellen (Ch 2), Ray's interior (Ch 4) and Arlene (two paragraphs earlier). Now a second piece of paper, the credit-union form, comes back with a new meaning I couldn't follow. Had I signed it or not, and for which outcome? That's the point where a reader with other books closes this one.

**Where I came back. Ch 6, the gloves.**
> "He worked the glove down her fingers one at a time, the thumb first because a cold thumb fights you, then each finger into its own place, pressed home at the knuckle, slow."

Then Ch 7's seven envelopes in a shoebox ("the same seven") pulled me fully back in.

**Drift 3. Ch 8, the release speech at the counter (~68%).**
> "If you come back, you come back beside me. I'll sign Arlene's paper, and I'll tell her myself. I could keep you in my drawer till the first of March and tear you up the morning I clear..."

That's a paragraph-long legal brief delivered as dialogue.

**Drift 4. Ch 9, storm logistics (~76–80%).** "Relief briefing," the squad rule, Dale's four o'clock, Hollis's count. Ellen running a crisis is the Chapter 3 scene again, and the line of refunds is the only thing in it with feeling. The woman from Saco ("I drove up here to hold her cup one time") was the moment I came back.

## The money, in my own words

- **What she owes:** Ellen bought the alley from Arlene. Arlene is owed about a quarter of a million on March 1 and needs it that day for her bed at The Pines. Ellen plans to pay it with a loan from Judy at Granite Lakes.
- **What the bank counts:** Judy writes that loan only if the winter (December through February) clears $30,000, and the money has to still be sitting in the account. Ellen's own savings count only if they were in her name before she bought the building, and then only at one dollar of credit for every eight she puts in. New money from anyone else counts for nothing.
- **Why Ray's money doesn't help:** it's new money, so the bank won't count it. It's also the money that pays Arlene if Ellen fails, so spending it on her would cancel his own backstop.
- **What the paydown cost her:** at eight to one, the $900 she ended up short cost roughly $7,200 of savings. The page never gives that figure, so I worked it out. That money was Kate's: the savings Ellen swore not to touch, the money Kate "sleeps on." What's left "heats this camp through one bad winter."
- **What fixed it:** Kate said yes. Ellen wired the savings to Arlene's note before the committee met. Linda's Open came back with Linda's name on it and a date for next winter. The committee approved because the money was "paid in before they sat, and next winter dated."

**Could I follow it?** Mostly, but only by Chapter 9, and only because the Kate call finally said it in short sentences. Before that, the eight-to-one rule appears in Ch 5 as "my own money lowers the line, slowly," which told me nothing. Nobody on the page explains why it's eight, and that ratio is where I stopped believing in the bank. A listener would lose the money entirely before Chapter 9.

## Confusion by chapter

- **Ch 1.** She has owned the building since 11 a.m., yet she owes Arlene a quarter of a million on March 1, and Ray gets it if she fails. What kind of contract is that? It's never said plainly in Chapter 1. "Lineage" (the fee per game) means nothing to a non-bowler. The Ms./Mrs. joke only works on paper.
- **Ch 2.** Why does she lay her own front-door key on his counter at 4:58 and say nothing? I had to wait for Ch 3 ("It was a no to selling." / "The rest of it was a key.") to learn it was both a no and a yes.
- **Ch 3.** "The captain" is Dale. I took a beat to connect the road agent, the Highway team captain and the man on the phone.
- **Ch 4.** "But the paper was what let Arlene hold her note." Why does Arlene need Ray's backup in order to hold her note? I inferred it and never quite got it.
- **Ch 5.** "No. Yours couldn't count if you gave it." The "No." contradicts nothing, because she has already said it. Also: "came out where it had come out on the twenty-second of December, with Judy on the phone." What came out? It's vague.
- **Ch 6.** The credit-union form. Ch 2 says Danny signed it and put it in the drawer *before* Dec 3. Ch 8 says Ray filled it in "for your no" on the Thursday *after* Dec 3. Which outcome was the form for, and when? I never resolved it. Why Ellen has to sign Ray's release at all I worked out myself: it's a three-party paper.
- **Ch 8.** At Arlene's kitchen Ellen walks in alone ("Ellen folded the paper and took her coat off the office door"), and three paragraphs later "Ray took the credit-union form out of his coat." When did Ray get there? The section also slides from Ellen's view to Ray's ("He was clean out of her paper") inside one scene.
- **Ch 9.** "Page two of the terms." Whose terms? The derby's, I think. The phrase "a squad is one field bowled at one time" needed a second read.
- **Ch 10.** Kate's call opens "Yes," without saying yes to what. "Left the four horsemen" is jargon I couldn't picture. The new pool sheet has R. Gagnon on December 1. I only worked out that it's the anniversary of Chapter 1 by counting back; a listener won't.
- **Names.** Normie (on the Bench) and Normand (Arlene's dead husband) are far too close. Pelkey, the Pelletier girl and the Pelletiers' chimney fire blur together too.

## Disbelief by chapter

- **Ch 1.** One locksmith in Ambrose, and it's him. That's comedy-convenient, and I let it go because the scene is funny.
- **Ch 3.** Hand-setting pins by flashlight so people get "three strings" during a power cut is whimsy, but I bought it. Running league against the road agent's call is her error, and she owns it later, which works.
- **Ch 4.** He could tear up the backup contract. The page closes it, though thinly ("without it her place at The Pines went to the next name").
- **Ch 7.** The break needs Ellen to call the meeting with Ray "in the room" when one more conversation with him alone was the obvious move. The page half-closes that ("I'm going to them, with you in the room"), and she admits in Ch 8 that she let it cost him. It held for me.
- **Ch 9, the twelve teams.** She is $1,400 under with her daughter's savings on the line, twelve paid teams are standing in the building, and the lanes are free from 12:30 to 4. She sends them home on a fairness rule ("Nobody pays a cent to bowl a squad the others can't"). The safer option, bowling the twelve and refunding the three, is closed only by her principle, so the storm loss reads as self-inflicted. Refusing Ray's name at the bank ("It might carry them." "It might.") in favor of Kate's savings is also principle over safety. Kate says so on the page ("Then he's got more sense than you"), which helps but doesn't fix it.
- **Ch 10.** A bank credit committee phones mid-vote to ask whether a bowling tournament has a date for next winter. That's cute, but it's the hinge of the whole ending and it's convenient. The eight-to-one savings rule is invented-sounding, and nobody explains it.
- **Ch 10.** Arlene asks for her key back at the bank, in front of Judy. It's staged so the ring has a gap for Ellen's key.

## What I think is going on (committed, as the reader who just finished)

This is a book about a woman who can't trust her own calm voice, and the fix is learning to *ask*. She asks Ray at his counter, Kate on the phone, and Kate again in the last line, about what Kate would miss. Ray's arc is the mirror: a widower who has kept Linda's name and Linda's locker in a museum, and who learns to give them away by saying them into her microphone. The money plot is a clock built so that the heroine's last resource is the thing she swore not to spend. Once Kate's savings went in, I never doubted the committee. The new pool sheet with "R. Gagnon" on December 1 tells me he's bet on proposing by the first anniversary of Chapter 1. If there's a sequel hook, it's Denise and Gil Arsenault at spring doubles in Halsey Falls, and Kate maybe moving to days, which probably means moving closer.

## Listener pass (whole book)

- **"\* \* \*" section breaks.** Depending on the voice, these get read out as "asterisk asterisk asterisk" or vanish, and there are about 35 of them.
- **"Ms." vs "Mrs."** The running joke depends on spelling. A TTS voice will say "Miz" and "Missus," or worse "M S," and the correction scenes in Ch 1, Ch 4, Ch 10 and the end may not land.
- **"Ettle Lanes."** Aloud it sounds like a mispronunciation, not a dark letter. The whole K payoff rests on a joke you have to see.
- **Polysyndeton chains** run a voice out of breath. Examples: Ch 1, "She stood in the doorway with her heat going out past her and wanted him to stay on the step and keep arguing, and to call her..."; Ch 3, the long trench sentence ("and the frost on the shoulders... and Hollis at seven, and a sweep motor that held in 'ninety-eight, and his thumb...").
- **Denise's run-ons** (Ch 7, "Gil Arsenault called at two, from Pine Ridge, and was this the one Linda used to win, he wants to know...") are written to be heard and mostly work. A flat TTS voice will flatten them, though.
- **Speakerless runs:**
  - Ch 3, the cubby voices ("My dog's been shut in since noon.").
  - Ch 6, the bob-house. Hollis says three things in a row with only gestures between, then "Judy's the other vote..."
  - Ch 10, Kate's speaker call: "Leave him on. Slept an hour in the hospital garage with the seat back. Don't. I'm fine." Aloud, "Don't." is unreadable, and "A beat." / "Another beat." will be voiced as narration.

## The ending

**Does it pay what Chapter 1 promised?** Mostly. Chapter 1 promised all ten lanes running, a dark letter on a sign, a key on an orange tag, a padlock, a pool sheet betting she'd lose, and a man who wouldn't hold the bet. All of it is paid, often word for word. "Both words" is a lovely payoff of "Ettle Lanes" and should have been the last line of the book.

**Is the ending earned?** The emotional climax, Ray's last ball leaving two pins standing so Denise wins, is earned and unsentimental. The plot climax is not. It's a phone call to a committee Ellen never meets, settled by a question about next year's date. She does nothing in that scene but stand at a window.

**Did the last line land?** "Tell me what you'd miss." lands softly. I understood it as Ellen finally asking instead of briefing, which closes Kate's Ch 5 complaint ("You could ask me something sometime"). It's a thinking reader's last line, not a feeling one. Coming after the epilogue retells the driveway story I'd already been given twice, it was a sigh rather than a lift.

**Which character I'll remember:** Ray. Specifically Ray on his knees on her mat, telling his offer to the lock.

## The ten blind questions (from the page only)

**1. What does the bank count toward the line, and why does Ray's money not help?**

The bank counts what the winter earns, kept in the account: "writes me the loan the day this winter clears thirty thousand, with every dollar it made still sitting in the account" (Ch 2). It also counts Ellen's own pre-purchase savings, at a discount: "Only if it was in my name before I bought the building. Then it's eight of mine for one of the winter's." (Ch 9).

Ray's money doesn't help: "The bank only counts money that has sat in my name since before I signed for the building. Yours would be new money. And yours is what pays Arlene if I miss." (Ch 5).

**2. Why could the twelve teams standing in the building not bowl on storm day?**

"Three are on the road," Ellen said. "A squad is one field bowled at one time, or it's a Tuesday league. Nobody pays a cent to bowl a squad the others can't. And the roads close at four." (Ch 9). In other words, three of the fifteen evening teams were not there, she won't let part of a squad compete, and Dale closed the roads at four.

**3. What happened to the December loan form, and when does Ellen learn of it?**

Danny signed it and put it in the drawer: "Danny had signed it anyway, put it in the drawer under the register, and never said a word." (Ch 2). It "had lain under the count slips ever since and gone nowhere." (Ch 6).

Ellen learns of it at Arlene's table on Presidents' Day, the fifteenth (Ch 8): "Thursday after you put that key on my counter, I filled it in for your no, and he put it in the drawer under the register and left it there. I'd have let him send it." Ray tears it up there.

Note: Ch 2 has the form already in the drawer before Dec 3, and Ch 8 has Ray filling it in after Dec 3. I could not reconcile the two.

**4. What does the paydown cost Ellen, in the rule's terms?**

"Every dollar the winter's short costs me eight. And whatever Judy's committee puts on top of it Monday morning." (Ch 9). She ends "Nine hundred dollars under" (Ch 10). The page never states the total; at eight to one I make it about $7,200 of Kate's savings, "more than half of the rest."

**5. Who drew the release, who signed it and when?**

- **Drawn by** Ray's lawyer: "My lawyer in Laconia drew it Monday." (Ch 6).
- **Ray signed first** at Arlene's table in January: "He signed first, fast and plain, and didn't read it over." (Ch 6).
- **Ellen signed second**, standing, at Arlene's kitchen on Presidents' Day, the fifteenth of February: "Ellen took her own pen out of her vest and signed standing, on the second line." (Ch 8). Confirmed in Ch 9: "On the fifteenth I let him out of it, with Arlene."
- **Arlene signed last**, the same day: "Arlene signed last, once, and handed the pen back." (Ch 8).

**6. Who is Normand?**

Arlene's late husband: "since Arlene's Normand, her husband, and he's been gone a long while now" (Ch 2). Also "In nineteen seventy-one I held Normand to a building he never wanted, and he gave it eighteen years." (Ch 6). He is not Normie on the Bench, and the names invite the mix-up.

**7. How old is Ellen?**

"Ellen was fifty-eight and two years retired by statute." (Ch 1).

**8. When did Ray order the K, and who hung it?**

Ordered: "Ordered it the Monday I came up to knock on your glass, on my own account" (Ch 10). That Monday is the eighth of February ("The eighth was a Monday," Ch 8).

Hung: "The Laconia shop's man had hung the tube that morning, and Ray had only put the face back on." (Ch 10).

**9. Was the committee's vote in doubt on March 1, and what settled it?**

Judy says it was in doubt: "You bought your way to the line. They can say no to that, and they've said it to closer." (Ch 10). What settled it: "'Paid in before they sat,' Judy said, 'and next winter dated.'" The money reached Arlene's note before the vote, and the committee asked whether next winter's Open had a date ("The last Saturday in February... Linda's."). As a reader I never felt the doubt.

**10. What is a miss on March 1, in plain words: who sells the building and how?**

It changes partway through the book.

- **Before the release (Ch 1–Ch 8):** Ray pays Arlene and the contract sells him the building at appraised value. "Either you hand Arlene her quarter of a million that morning, or I pay her off that afternoon, and the contract you signed sells it to me by suppertime." / "Appraised value. Not a dollar under." (Ch 1).
- **After the release (Feb 15):** Arlene, who holds the note, forecloses and the building goes to auction, probably to the condo developer at land value. "I get my money months from now, after I've foreclosed on that woman of yours." (Arlene, Ch 6). "If I sign this and I miss, it's your auction. You wait for your money and go back on the list. The condo man gets the lot for the land" (Ellen, Ch 8).
