## 1. Fixes (do these before the external round)

### F1. Ch 10: "left the four horsemen across the back of the deck"
- **Page:** Denise's second ball "left the four horsemen across the back of the deck, with daylight all round them." Her third ball "took all four."
- **Verdict: fix (terminology).** The four horsemen are four pins in a diagonal line running out from the head pin: 1-2-4-7 on the left, 1-3-6-10 on the right. The head pin is at the front of the rack, so this leave cannot lie across the back. A row across the back (7-8-9-10) is called the **picket fence**. Do not swap in the picket fence: one ball cannot take all four of those with no wood on the deck, and the page says "daylight all round them."
- **Should say:** "left the four horsemen running back from the head pin to the corner, with daylight all round them."
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia, Candlepin bowling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candlepin_bowling) ("Four pins in a diagonal line, from the head-pin outward"); [SONH Candlepin Coaching Guide](https://www.sonh.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Candlepin-Bowling-Coaching-Guide.pdf) (four horsemen 1-2-4-7 or 1-3-6-10; picket fence 7-8-9-10)

### F2. Ch 10: Ray's last box against "by two pins"
- **Page:** Hollis says "You need all ten." Ray's three balls take eight pins: most of the rack, then two more, then one of the last three, leaving two. Verna: "The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins."
- **Verdict: fix (arithmetic).** Eight pins and a two-pin loss means Ray started ten behind. Ten pins would only have tied, so "You need all ten" to win is wrong. The quoted line and the margin need to agree.
- **Should say (pick one):** "The secretary has Lakeview Diner by one pin." (He was nine behind: nine ties, ten wins, eight loses by one.) Or keep "by two pins" and have Hollis say "All ten ties it."
- **Source:** the page's own count. The tenth box always gets three balls, and a ten-box scores ten with no bonus: [Wikipedia, Candlepin bowling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candlepin_bowling).

### F3. Ch 4 and Ch 7: house balls by weight ("the lightest on the rack," "lightest to heaviest," "the heaviest ball")
- **Page:** Ch 4: "One was the weight most people took. The other was the lightest on the rack… the heavier ball." Ch 7: Ray puts the balls "in order lightest to heaviest"; Dale "took the heaviest ball out of Ray's new order."
- **Verdict: fix (terminology). The Oct 29 correction landed in Ch 1 only.** Ch 1 now sorts the rack "by wear," which is right. Candlepin rules set one ball: at most 4½ in and 2 lb 7 oz, no finger holes, and no minimum weight. Balls are sold at one weight per model (for example EPCO, 4½ in, 2 lb 6 oz). House balls differ by an ounce at most, so racking them by weight is a tenpin habit, and a candlepin bowler will notice it. Grip and wear are what differ.
- **Should say:** Ch 4: "One was the kind most people took. The other was the one with the most grip left on the rack… put the worn ball back." Ch 7: "putting the balls in order, worn smooth to good grip"; Dale "took the best ball out of Ray's new order."
- **Sources:** [Wikipedia, Candlepin bowling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candlepin_bowling) (max 2 lb 7 oz, no minimum stated); [SONH Coaching Guide](https://www.sonh.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Candlepin-Bowling-Coaching-Guide.pdf) (maximums only); [EPCO candlepin ball listing, 2 lb 6 oz](https://www.walmart.com/ip/BuyBocceBalls-New-Listing-EPCO-Candlepin-Starline-Galaxy-4-Ball-Package-Black-4-1-2-2lbs-6oz/2072675614)

### F4. Ch 9: "How much is more than half?" / "Fourteen hundred dollars"
- **Page:** Kate asks how much of Ellen's savings ("the rest") the paydown will take. Ellen answers: "Fourteen hundred dollars, and whatever Judy's committee puts on top of it Monday morning."
- **Verdict: fix (units).** $1,400 is how far short the winter came. It is not the savings needed to close the gap. The page sets the multiplier itself: Ch 5 says her money "lowers the line, slowly," and Ch 10 Judy says "It takes a good many of your dollars to buy down one of the winter's." At the bible's 8:1, a $1,400 shortfall costs about $11,200 of savings. At the 1.25× coverage arithmetic in §4 it costs about $11,800. Either is "more than half" of the bible's $14,000, which leaves about $2,800 for "one bad winter" of camp heat.
- **Should say:** "Eleven thousand, near enough, and whatever Judy's committee puts on top of it Monday morning." If the book keeps dollars off the page: "Eight of mine for every one the winter's short."
- **Source:** the arithmetic in §4; [Wikipedia, Debt service coverage ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt_service_coverage_ratio).

### F5. Ch 9: the derby raffle drawn at the alley, when the "tickets say four o'clock at the dock"
- **Page:** The derby treasurer tips the stubs into the ball return at the alley during the morning squads. He says "Tickets say four o'clock at the dock," Ellen says "They'll still say it," and a child draws.
- **Verdict: fix.** NH law requires every raffle ticket to carry "the date and place of the drawing." The statute is silent on the time, but this draw happens at a different place from the one printed, and earlier. The book's raffle firewall still holds (it is the derby's raffle, and Ellen only hosts), but a club officer or a selectman will notice the draw moving.
- **Should say (pick one):** The treasurer keeps the stubs in the bucket until four, at the dock if the roads allow. Or the tickets read "four o'clock, derby headquarters," so the room fee makes the alley headquarters for the day. Or cut the draw from the scene.
- **Sources:** [RSA 287-A:3](https://gencourt.state.nh.us/rsa/html/XXIV/287-A/287-A-3.htm); [Swanzey, RSA 287-A synopsis](https://swanzeynh.gov/how_do_i/apply_for_obtain/raffle_permit.php)

### F6. Ch 6 and Ch 8: "a miss is a bank sale" / "it's a bank sale"
- **Page:** Ellen (Ch 6): "Without you behind me, a miss is a bank sale." Ch 8: "If I sign this and I miss, it's a bank sale. You wait for your money…" Arlene (Ch 6) says it correctly: "after I've foreclosed on that woman of yours."
- **Verdict: fix (terminology).** The note is Arlene's (seller financing), so a miss means Arlene's own foreclosure sale under the power of sale: a public auction after notice. No bank is involved. NH requires at least 25 days' notice to the mortgagor and newspaper notice for three weeks, starting at least 20 days before the sale, so Arlene's "months from now" holds. A real-estate reader will stop at "bank sale."
- **Should say:** "a foreclosure sale" or "an auction on the courthouse steps" ("a foreclosure auction" in Ch 8).
- **Source:** [RSA 479:25](https://gencourt.state.nh.us/rsa/html/XLVIII/479/479-25.htm)

### F7. Ch 6: "The commitment says subject to committee"
- **Page:** Ellen to Judy on the ice: "The commitment says subject to committee." / "I read that as a formality."
- **Verdict: fix (terminology, small).** In commercial lending a commitment letter comes *after* underwriting and credit approval. The paper that says "subject to credit approval" is the **term sheet** (or a letter of intent). A commitment can carry conditions, and "subject to the winter clearing thirty thousand" is a fine condition, but "subject to committee" belongs on a term sheet. Judy would hear the wrong word.
- **Should say:** "The term sheet says subject to committee." Or "The letter says subject to committee."
- **Source:** [Poyner Spruill, A Refresher on Term Sheets and Commitment Letters](https://www.poynerspruill.com/thought-leadership/a-refresher-on-term-sheets-and-commitment-letters/)

### F8. Ch 10: "Eighty minutes"
- **Page:** Judy's phone rings "at the half hour" (10:30; the committee sat at ten). Then "An hour went by, and a good piece of another, and the phone rang the second time." Ellen: "Eighty minutes."
- **Verdict: fix (arithmetic).** From 10:30, an hour and "a good piece" of another is at least ninety minutes. From ten, eighty minutes makes the second call 11:20, only fifty minutes after the first, which contradicts "an hour went by." Ellen is the one person in the book who gets this right.
- **Should say:** "An hour went by, and twenty minutes of another." Or keep the narration and have Ellen say "A hundred and five minutes."

**Money and law**
- **The release (Ch 6, Ch 8).** It has one clause, all three sign it, and a Laconia lawyer draws it at Ray's own expense, with "Have your own lawyer read it before you sign anything." *What it does:* it ends the backup sale contract. *Consideration:* the contract is unperformed on both sides, so each party giving up the other's performance is consideration, and nothing more is needed. *Why Arlene must sign:* her note was conditioned on that contract, and once a beneficiary has assented, the original parties cannot vary it to her detriment without her. *What it does not do:* it does not touch the note, the March 1 balloon, or Arlene's power of sale. It needs acknowledgment and recording only if the contract was recorded, and v3 never says it was (the Oct 29 notary/registry item is now moot). Ray telling Ellen to see her own lawyer is right practice for a lawyer's client dealing with an unrepresented party. [LexPlug, modification and rescission](https://www.lexplug.com/topics/contracts/modification-rescission); [LexPlug, third-party beneficiaries](https://www.lexplug.com/topics/contracts/third-party-beneficiaries)
- Ch 4: "He could tear up the backup contract tonight" against Ch 6: "I can't tear that paper up." Ch 6 is the legally correct one, since neither party can rescind alone. Ch 4 reads as Ray's thought, and the next sentence undercuts it. Fine as character; a lawyer-reader might prefer "He could ask to tear up…"
- **Seasoning (Ch 5, Ch 9):** "The bank only counts money that has sat in my name since before I signed for the building." A longer bank-set window (Dec 1 to Mar 1, about 90 days) is plausible, and the page wisely never says "seasoning." [LegalClarity, seasoning of funds](https://legalclarity.org/seasoning-of-funds-how-the-60-90-day-rule-works/)
- **Guarantor (Ch 9):** "Put my name behind yours at Judy's." / "Behind mine, your name pays if I miss." / "It might carry them."
