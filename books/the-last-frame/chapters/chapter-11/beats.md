### Scene 1

**Setting:** Kettle Lanes, the counter and then the office with its door open on the lanes. Saturday, April 17, about seven o'clock. **Sensory anchor:** the new K's red coming through the glass block over the door, and through the open office door the house's noise, every lane at once with nothing in between.

**Characters:** Ellen, Hollis, Denise (opening only), Kate, Ray (seen, not speaking).

**Want:** Ellen wants to pay what she owes in full and in order, the way she has always paid an error: written up and complete. What's in the way is that the order won't hold past Kate's light, and that Kate asks the questions a write-up leaves out. Kate wants the one fact under the facts: whether her mother was fine, and why she was kept on the other side of the glass. The reader learns Kate's want at "Were you?", and Ellen's at her answer to it.

**Subtext:** Ellen has paid for every error in her life by reporting it whole, and this one she reported to the FAA and never to the person it happened to. Kate isn't after the incident. She wants to be told she was right that morning, at twelve, through a car window. Under that is the thing neither says: Kate has spent nineteen years doing to her mother what her mother did to her.

**Texture budget:** None beyond the room. The K's red on the glass block (beat 1) and the open office door (beats 5 and 13), which let the house and Ray into a private scene. The night is told without trade: no phraseology, no terms, no procedure beyond "they played me the tape". What that reveals: she has no vocabulary to hide behind, so the telling costs what it should.

**Point of view:** Ellen. Her dialogue is short-to-mid, exact and complete; her interior runs longer than her speech. Fear makes her more polite and more procedural, so the briefing *is* her fear, and the reader sees the gap through what she notices: Kate's keys, the door, Ray's hands. Kate's lines are fast and clinical, history-taking, warm under it. In this two-hander, tag Kate's questions often enough for a listener: after any narration beat, the next line carries a tag (the voice editor's rule; cold reader §7).

**Ends on:** **Dialogue.** Kate: "I learned the voice anyway." Nothing after it: no reaction beat, no Ellen interior, no scene-break gloss.

**Word target:** 550 (opening and arrival ~100; office ~450). **Word floor:** 450 for the office, beats 5–15. The price inside it (beats 6–15) never runs under 300. If the scene runs long, trim beats 1–4 and never the questions.

### Scene 2

**Setting:** Kettle Lanes, lane five and then the counter. About twenty minutes later. **Sensory anchor:** the crack of Kate's ball landing on the maple, too high and too late.

**Characters:** Ellen, Kate, Ray, Denise (two short beats with Ellen alone).

**Want:** Ellen wants her daughter to stay in the room with her and to ask her things. What's in the way is that Kate has only ever brought her outcomes, and Ellen has only ever answered with numbers. Kate wants to see what she paid for. Denise wants one person to know she's frightened. The reader learns Ellen's want in the book's last line.

**Texture budget:** None. The pool sheet is a prop, not trade. The only lane mechanics are the ball coming back up the return.

**Point of view:** Ellen. Her interior stays light and brief in this scene. The comedy works by the gap between a question and its answer, and nobody laughs at their own line. Ray speaks once in the whole epilogue, and his line is the arc.

**Ends on:** **Question.** Ellen: "Tell me what you'd miss." It is the last line of the book. No interior gloss before it explains why she asked instead of answering, and nothing comes after it: no Kate reaction, no image, no K.
