# Reader notes: Epilogue, "Ice-Out"

**Draft read:** `chapters/chapter-11/final.md`, v3, Oct 30. For context I read `chapters/chapter-01/final.md` through `chapters/chapter-10/final.md` first, in order. I opened no other file.

---

## 1. Pull score: 6 / 10

The first scene, Kate in the office, earns its place: there is one new image in it (Kate's knock on the car window) and one line that hurts ("I learned the voice anyway"). The second scene is a curtain call. Every beat in it is a callback, and I watched the book take its bow instead of reading something happen. The score rests on one scene.

## 2. Where I got bored

- **Line 23, about a quarter in:** *"First. I was on a midnight shift. At two in the morning, two airplanes came too close to each other, and they were both mine."* This is the sixth time the book has touched this night: the pit in ch 3, Kate's "car-in-the-driveway voice" in ch 5, Ray using it as a weapon in ch 7, his apology in ch 8, and Kate's terms in ch 10. I already knew it was two in the morning, that nobody was hurt, that she sounded perfect on the tape, that she wrote it up herself, and that she sat in the car until the bus came. For the first half-dozen lines the scene is a recap I'm being asked to sit through for Kate's sake. I came back at "Including you?"
- **Lines 3–11, the opening:** Hollis turns in his shoes and announces ice-out, Denise says "You look rested," and Ellen answers "I slept till ten past five." This is a roll call that tells me who is still in the book. Nothing happens and nobody wants anything. I drifted for about the first tenth.
- **Line 67, the pool sheet:** one 60-word sentence I had to read twice to find out what the sheet was. Not boredom exactly, but my attention slid off it.
- **Would I have stopped?** No. It's short and it's the end, so I'd finish it. That proves nothing about whether it's good.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **Line 5:** *"Hollis came for his shoes. 'Called ice-out yesterday.' She handed them across."* Who says the line? Probably Hollis, but the "She" straight after it made me stop and work out who was handing what to whom. Why does a league bowler collect his shoes at the counter?
- **Line 11:** *"Denise's bowling bag stood zipped by the vestibule door, where she never left it."* I read "where she never left it" as either "it's never usually there" or "she never leaves without it." Only Denise's lines at 73 told me it was the first. It plants something I couldn't read yet.
- **Line 73:** *"Handed me the flyer with the cup in my other hand. I kept this one."* Kept which one? The flyer? As opposed to what? I connected it back to Gil's "folded flyer in his glove" in ch 10 and guessed it means she kept Gil's flyer, the first one from a man she didn't throw out. I'm still guessing.
- **Line 67:** I get that the new sheet is a pool on a proposal or a wedding, and that Ray bought a square. The page never says which date he bought. I wanted it badly enough to read the sentence twice looking for it.
- **Line 79:** *"Scale of one to ten?"* In ch 5 and ch 9 this was Kate asking how Ellen was. Here Kate is asking Ellen to rate Kate's decision, and on first pass I couldn't tell whose number she wanted. Ellen's answer cleared it up a line later.
- **Lines 59–65:** "Ray came down," "went back up," "Ellen followed him up." Up and down relative to what? The geography is the counter at one end of the house and the settees down near the lanes, which I only half-held by now.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **Kate's arrival.** Kate drove up from Manchester. She put her savings into this building, and in ch 10 she said *"I'm coming up in April to see what I paid for."* She walks in, says nothing to anyone, watches Ray take the register, and goes into the office. There's no hello, no look round the room, and not one word to the man she knew was "behind" her mother. Both women are brisk, so the skipped greeting is believable, but the promise to see "what I paid for" is never kept. Kate never sizes Ray up. Her only exchange with him is "She told me you'd say that." That's the biggest gap in the epilogue.
- **"Ellen left the door open on the lanes."** This is a private woman about to tell the worst morning of her life to her daughter, for the first time. She leaves the door open on a full house, with no reason given on the page. I could feel it being done so Ray could see her face. The payoff is good (Ray holding the shoes still), but the choice isn't hers, it's the author's.
- **Kate's ball into lane six.** On a night with "no gap" in the noise, a ball crosses into the next lane and nobody on six reacts. In ch 1 the same thing needed a ruling from Verna. Here it only exists to set up Ray's line.
- **Denise saying "You don't get to bet on your own flight."** "Flight" is Ellen's vocabulary in Denise's mouth. It's possible she's teasing, but it reads as the author borrowing a voice.
- **Ray buying a square.** I believe it. "Ray won't" in ch 1 against "the first square he had ever bought" here is the one callback that earns itself.
- No choices made under pressure in this chapter, so nothing to test there.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **The callback parade.** In about 800 words I counted: "Understood," "Nice arc. Wrong lane.," the pool sheet, "Owners don't play" turned into "bet on your own flight," "Scale of one to ten," First/Second, Ray stepping up to the register as D-side, the K lit red, and Ray letting go of a ball "at once" (against holding on in ch 1). Each one alone is fine. Together they read like a checklist of plants being ticked off. "Nice arc. Wrong lane." is the one that made me wince. It was a great line in ch 1 because it was spontaneous. Here Kate has to tell us it was predicted, which admits it's a rerun.
- **Line 21:** *"Every line of it had been true, and none of it had gone to Kate."* The narrator tells me the scene's meaning before the scene plays it.
- **"She did not know how many."** Line 77: *"The pins went, and she did not know how many."* This is lovely by itself. But it's the fourth time the book has shown love as Ellen losing count or losing the time (the order going out of it in ch 3, losing the time in the kiss in ch 5, no longer trusting her sense of the time in ch 5, and now this). The device is worn.
- **Both scenes end on a button line:** "I learned the voice anyway." / "Tell me what you'd miss." So does nearly every scene in the book: "Ray won't." / "That's the bell." / "Honey." / "Ms. Daly. Net thirty." / "She had friends." / "Both words." By the last page I knew a short, polished closing line was coming before I reached it.
- **Line 41:** *"Ellen had watched the green numbers on the dash clock change, one minute at a time."* "Dash lit green" is Ray's truck in ch 5 and ch 9. I can't tell if the echo is meant. It reads as a reused texture.

## 6. What I think is going on

The book is resolved, so this is my read of where it leaves everyone. Ray has bought a square on a pool that can only be about when he proposes, and he will propose on whatever date he bought, which the page is keeping from me. Ellen keeps the alley. She has stopped running the place like a scope and lets Ray take the register, so the D-side setup is permanent. Kate will take days. The book has drawn a straight line between her on nights as charge nurse and her mother on midnights, and "Tell me what you'd miss" is Ellen recognizing that and handing Kate a question instead of a ruling. Denise and Gil Arsenault are starting something at the spring doubles in Halsey Falls; "I'm nervous. Don't you tell a soul" is a sequel hook, and if there is a book two, it's Denise's. Next February Linda's name goes on the Open again, and Ray bowls it from a settee.

## 7. The listener pass

- **Line 5:** with a TTS voice, *"Called ice-out yesterday." She handed them across.* sounds like one speaker. A listener won't know who said what.
- **Line 59:** "landed in six" aloud is "landed in six" what? Lane is never said. "The five-and-six settee" is a mouthful that means nothing to the ear.
- **Line 67:** the pool sheet sentence. Its one important fact (Ray's name) comes at the end of a 60-word sentence after a colon, and a voice will say "R. Gagnon" as "R Gagnon" or "Are Gagnon." By ear, the reveal is lost.
- **Lines 59–71:** a run of blocking sentences in the same shape: "Ray came down and lifted…", "Ellen followed him up.", "Ellen walked her to the vestibule.", "Back beside Ray, Ellen watched Kate throw." Heard aloud, it's stage directions.
- **Line 73:** "I kept this one" is opaque to the eye and worse to the ear, because nobody can look back up the page.
- **What works aloud:** the Kate scene. The "First." / "Second," structure is clear by ear, and the break from it is audible. Ellen's long speech at line 43 ("Your light came on at twenty to seven…") is the best-sounding paragraph in the book read aloud: the counted stamps, the bus on the hill, two knocks from a small fist. A narrator could make a listener cry with it. "An hour and four minutes." / "Of course you know" plays well heard.
- **Line 83:** speaker attribution is fine. "Ellen kept hold of his hand" sits right before it, so the voice is hers.

## 8. The central relationship

Yes, twice, both quiet:

- *"He held the shoes still by the heels and stayed where he was, and then he passed them over the counter to whoever was waiting."* He sees her face in the open door and doesn't go to her. He keeps the post she gave him. This is the best Ray moment in the epilogue, and it made me want them together more than the hand-holding.
- *"Under the counter his hand found hers and held it, and neither of them looked down."* This is sweet but standard.

Laughs: "Of course you know," and "She told me you'd say that." I smiled at them but didn't laugh. Ray gets almost nothing in his own book's last chapter: two actions and a recycled line.

The Kate–Ellen relationship is the real romance of this epilogue, and it's the part that works.

## 9. What I want to know next

**Does the book end where I wanted?** Nearly. The real ending is ch 10's "Both words," and that ending is earned. The epilogue is a coda that closes Kate's debt and the ch 1 pool sheet. The Kate debt needed closing. The pool sheet didn't need half a scene.

**What I'd have wanted one more page of:**
- Kate and Ray, alone, for a minute. She said she was coming "to see what I paid for." I wanted her to look at him the way Judy looked at a balance sheet, and I wanted to hear what she decided.
- The date on Ray's square. One line.
- Kate's answer to "Tell me what you'd miss." I can live without this one, since leaving it open is the point.

**What I'd have skimmed past (not a fix, just what my eyes did):** the Hollis/Denise roll call at the top, the recap half of the "First." paragraph, and Denise's Halsey Falls speech up to "I'm nervous."

**Questions still pulling me:** only the Denise/Gil thread. Nothing pulls me back into this couple's story; it's finished, which is correct for an ending.

## 10. Best moment

> "When you heard the bus coming on the hill you came all the way back up to the glass by my head." She still had the sound of it, two knocks, quick and light, from the side of a small fist. "I said I was fine. I said it the way I'd said everything to the airplanes."

And then Kate: *"I learned the voice anyway."* That's the only genuinely new thing in the epilogue and the best line Kate has in the book. The knock on glass also answers Ray's three raps on Ellen's door glass in ch 1 without pointing at it. It's the one echo the book doesn't announce, and it's the one that works.

## The ending questions

**Does the ending pay what the opening promised?** Yes. The opening promised a woman who bought a building "to hear all ten going at once," a man with a key she didn't give him, a dead K, and a pool sheet titled *When she quits or loses it*, with Denise saying "Ray won't." The epilogue's first line is the ten lanes with "no gap in it anywhere." Ch 10 paid the K and the key. The epilogue pays "Ray won't" with Ray's first square. It also pays Kate's ch 5 line, "You could ask me something sometime. Before," by having Ellen finally ask instead of brief. Everything the first chapter put on the table gets cleared.

**Did the last line land, or was it a neat bow?** It lands, quietly. "Tell me what you'd miss" is a question, not a verdict, and it's the whole book's change in one sentence: the woman who briefs now asks. But I heard it coming. As soon as Kate said "Scale of one to ten?" I knew Ellen would flip it, because every scene in this book ends on a turned line. The last line isn't a bow. The scene around it, Kate's ball, the pool sheet and Denise's exit, is one.

**Anything I hadn't predicted?** The knock on the car window. "Including you?" going unanswered. Denise being nervous about Gil. Kate thinking about taking days. Ray buying a square: that one I half-expected but liked anyway. Everything else in the epilogue the page had already given me.

**Which of Kate's questions did the most work?** *"Including you?"* Kate is a nurse doing triage ("Was anybody hurt?"), and then she asks the one question nobody in the book has asked: whether Ellen was a casualty. Ellen doesn't answer. She says "Second," and goes back to her list. The non-answer is the answer, and it sets up *"Were you?" / "No."* six exchanges later, the first time in the book Ellen says it plainly. "Were you?" gets the line, but "Including you?" opens the door to it.

**Would I buy this author's next book?** **Yes**, and the one reason is that the two leads talk like nobody else, and the book has lines that cost the speaker something ("I kept quiet because the price of telling you was you"; "I said it the way I'd said everything to the airplanes"). **This is an "I'd buy it, but…" and should be read as a warning:** the place I'd most likely have put this book down was not the epilogue. It was the middle, where the contract, release, note, hold and committee mechanics of ch 6 and ch 8 ate pages the people should have had. I'd buy the next one on its sample, not blind.
