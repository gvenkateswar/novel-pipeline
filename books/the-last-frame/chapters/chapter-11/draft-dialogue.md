# Epilogue: Ice-Out

There was no gap in the noise anywhere down the house, and Ellen stood in it. Through the glass block the new K on its pole at the road showed red against the last of the daylight.

Hollis came for his shoes. "Called ice-out yesterday." She handed them across.

"You look rested, honey," Denise said.

"I slept till ten past five."

Denise's bowling bag stood zipped by the vestibule door, where she never left it.

Kate came in from the lot in her coat with her car keys still in her hand. As Ellen came out from behind the counter, Ray moved up into her place. Kate watched him do it, looked at him once, and went past him into the office ahead of her mother.

Ellen left the door open on the lanes. Kate hitched herself up onto the steel desk with the keys in her lap, and the noise of the house came in after them and filled the room to the shelves. Ellen stayed on her feet.

"April," Kate said. "In person."

"Understood."

Ellen had written this night up once already, in ink, and every line of it had been true, and none of it had gone to Kate. She began it where a write-up begins, with the time.

"First. I was on a midnight shift. At two in the morning, two airplanes came too close to each other, and they were both mine."

"Was anybody hurt?" Kate said.

"Nobody."

"Including you?"

"Second," Ellen said. "I told one of them to turn, and he turned. They played me the tape before I went home, and you couldn't hear a thing in it. I drove home at the speed limit the whole way. I turned the engine off in the driveway, and then I didn't do the next thing."

The next thing was the door handle, and she had known exactly where it was.

Kate turned the keys over and over. "How long were you out there?"

"An hour and four minutes."

"Of course you know," Kate said.

Ellen had watched the dash clock the whole time. It was the one thing in the car she could still read.

"Your light came on at twenty to seven," Ellen said. "I watched you come out the front door with your coat open and go past the car to the bottom of the drive for the bus. The bus was late, and you stood down there with your back to the house and your bag on one shoulder, and you stamped, the way you did when you'd been told to wait. I counted the stamps. I was still counting things. When you heard the bus coming on the hill you came all the way back up to the glass by my head." She still had the sound of it, two knocks, quick and light, from the side of a small fist. "I said I was fine. I said it the way I'd said everything to the airplanes."

"Were you?" Kate said.

"No."

Through the open door she could see Ray at the register with a pair of rental shoes in his hand, and he had seen her face. He held the shoes still by the heels and stayed where he was, and then he passed them over the counter to whoever was waiting.

"Why didn't you come in?" Kate said.

"Because I'd have sounded fine, and you'd have believed me."

Kate closed her hand on the keys. "I learned the voice anyway."

\* \* \*

Kate swung a house ball through on a straight arm, and it left her hand still climbing and landed in six. Ellen watched from the end of the five-and-six settee.

Ray came down, lifted the ball off the return between five and six, and walked it to Kate. "Nice arc. Wrong lane."

"She told me you'd say that," Kate said.

He put it in her hands and let go at once, and went back up.

Ellen followed him up. Clipped by the register was Normie's new sheet, ruled by hand like the December one, a square for each date and no title across the top to say what for, and every square empty but one: R. Gagnon, in pencil, in the printing she knew from Ray's invoices, the first square he had ever bought. She reached for a pencil.

"You don't get to bet on your own flight," Denise said, passing for her bag.

Ellen walked her to the vestibule.

"Halsey Falls in the morning," Denise said. "Spring doubles with Gil Arsenault. Know what that man did? Handed me the flyer with the cup in my other hand. I kept this one."

She had the door open. "I'm nervous. Don't you tell a soul."

Back beside Ray, Ellen watched Kate throw again. Under the counter his hand found hers and held it, and neither of them looked down. The pins went, and she did not know how many.

Kate came up to the counter across from them. "I'm thinking about taking days. I haven't decided. Scale of one to ten?"

Ellen held on to Ray's hand under the counter.

"Tell me what you'd miss."
