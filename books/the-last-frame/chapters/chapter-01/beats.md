## Flags (read before drafting)

1. **Bible vs outline: Ray leaving lane 7 dead.** The bible says Ray would never "leave a machine broken on league night, whoever owns it." The outline has lane 7 die and Ray say "Not my alley," and Ellen runs nine lanes. **Reconciliation used in these beats (non-blocking):** this is the one night he breaks it, and the page shows that it costs him. He walks as far as lane 7's approach, listens to the knock long enough that Ellen sees him name it to himself, then says "Not my alley." In Scene 4 Denise gives it one clause: in twenty years he has never left a machine down on league night. It happens because of the doorway ("I don't ask."): he will fix it the minute she asks, and she won't. If the orchestrator wants the bible's "never" amended to "never, until Dec 1", that's the author's call. The beats work either way.
2. **Bible wording on the contract.** The bible says never to write that the building "goes to him" automatically; the contract sells it. Ray's doorway line should be "the contract you signed sells it to me by suppertime" (or close to it), not "the building's his." No figure in it.
3. **Not an issue for Ch 1, logged for Ch 3:** the bible's counts table still says 31 stranded; the brief's approval sets it at 33. Ch 3's beats should use 33.
4. **Ray's Ch 2 memory has to happen here:** when he knocks, she finishes the strip of tape before she comes down the ladder. Scene 1 puts it on the page.
5. **Ch 2's opening recording has to be made here:** Ellen records lane 7's noise on her phone (Scene 3). Ch 2 opens with her playing it on his counter.

### Scene 1 — The doorway
**Setting:** Kettle Lanes, the counter and the vestibule. Tuesday, December 1, 5:15 p.m., full dark, snow showers. Sensory anchor: the front door's glass rattling in its frame under his knuckles.
**Characters:** Ellen Daly, Ray Gagnon.
**Want:** Ellen wants Arlene's key back in her hand, tonight, from a man who has a key to her building six hours after she closed on it. In the way: he won't give it, and the only thing she could do to get it is ask, which she doesn't do. Ray wants her to know he isn't going anywhere: not tonight (league) and not on March first. In the way: he finds he likes her, and that wasn't the plan. The reader learns her want on "I'll take the key."
**Surface action:**
1. Ellen on a stepladder behind the counter, re-taping the ten lane-light toggles over Arlene's old, yellowed tape. A knock on the front-door glass. Through it, a tall man with snow on his shoulders holding up a key in his fist: hers, or Arlene's, and that's the first argument.
2. **She finishes the strip of tape before she comes down the ladder.** (Ray remembers this in Ch 2.)
3. She opens the door. He's taller than she'd planned for, and she had planned for most things (bible interior line 1, adapted). Behind his shoulders, out at the road, the sign reads ETTLE LANES.
4. He knocked although he could have let himself in. She sees that, and it annoys her more than it should.
5. The key exchange, as the outline has it: "I'll take the key." / "That's not asking." / "I don't ask." / "I noticed." Arlene gave it to him; Arlene can ask for it back. He calls her Mrs. Daly. "Ms." (once).
6. She tells him he has no business with her building. He tells her he has business with it on March first: either she hands Arlene her quarter of a million, or he pays Arlene off and the contract she signed sells it to him by suppertime. "You know what you signed." / "I know exactly what I signed. I signed so Arlene gets paid. By me." / "Appraised value. Not a dollar under. I'm not buying your mistake cheap."
7. The interior line, plainly, in the middle of her next answer: she wants him to stay on the step and keep arguing (her list breaking, and her noticing it).
8. He says he has a league to coach, and steps past her into the warm, and she moves her shoulder to let him.
**Subtext:** Both of them signed the same piece of paper this morning, from opposite ends. He resents her and has come early anyway. She has spent six hours as an owner, and the first person to knock is the one she can't brief.
**Texture budget:** (1) The contract, carried in the four lines above and nowhere else; it shows she chose it with her eyes open. (2) The masking tape on the toggles; it shows how she takes possession of a thing, by labeling it. Nothing else procedural.
**Point of view:** Ellen.
**Ends on:** **Reversal.** She had meant to keep him on the step, and she steps aside, and he goes in past her with the key still in his fist.
**Word target:** 800

### Scene 2 — The locksmith
**Setting:** The counter and the shoe cubbies, about 6:05 p.m. The league coming in out of the snow. Sensory anchor: wet wool and road salt off forty pairs of boots, and the first breath of fryer air.
**Characters:** Ellen, Ray, Hollis Pike, Danny Gagnon (by phone), Denise Lavoie (at the register, one line at most), league bowlers (background).
**Want:** Ellen wants the lock changed before the man with the key can make it a joke, which means doing it in front of everyone. In the way: there's one locksmith in Ambrose. Ray wants to watch her find that out. The reader learns her want when she picks up the rotary phone with the league's eyes on her.
**Surface action:**
1. She counts the room as it fills (the motif's first use; she gets an exact number). One interior passage, longer than the talk around it: who's where, who's moving, who's watching the new owner, and that she's fifty-eight and two years retired by statute and has never once been the person a room came in to look at.
2. Ray drops his key ring on the counter to lace his shoes. She reads the orange paper tag: his initials and 1996. The key from the door is on it.
3. Ray, without being asked, raps a knuckle once on the trophy-case glass on his way past (motif 1 of 3). She sees the framed card: Linda Gagnon, a hundred and eighty-seven. Beside the case, locker fourteen, padlocked: the one lock in her building she has no key to. She files it. Nobody explains it.
4. Hollis Pike at the counter, lane sheet in hand, assigns her the ceremonial first ball on lane five. Ray's team is on six. No change of tone. His one candlepin line, spoken, for her benefit: small ball, no holes, three balls a box, throw it like you mean to let go of it.
5. Ellen opens the phone book on the counter, finds the only locksmith, and dials, loud enough to be heard. Gagnon & Son. Danny answers, closing up the store.
6. **The laugh.** She asks for the locksmith. Danny: the locksmith is his father, and his father's standing in her alley; does she want to tell him herself? She looks across the counter at Ray, who has been listening with a shoe in his hand.
7. **The turn.** She books it anyway, out loud, with the league listening: eight o'clock tomorrow, the front and the back. Danny writes it down. Somebody at the cubbies laughs and stops.
**Subtext:** She would rather be laughed at than ask him. He is about to be paid to lock himself out of the place he has had a key to for thirty years, and he doesn't argue with her.
**Texture budget:** (1) Hollis's candlepin line, the one spoken explanation of the game in the book; it shows Hollis set the lanes up on purpose. (2) None else. The phone call is talk.
**Point of view:** Ellen.
**Ends on:** **Dialogue.** Ray, lacing the second shoe, about the locksmith as if he were somebody else: he takes his coffee black, and he'll want somebody to hold the light. (This sets up Ch 2's flashlight; don't explain it.)
**Word target:** 700

### Scene 3 — Lane 6
**Setting:** Lanes five and six, then the approach of lane seven. About 6:30 to 7:15 p.m. Sensory anchor: the ball's weight in one hand, like a grapefruit, heavier than it looks, and the lane longer than it looks.
**Characters:** Ellen, Ray, Hollis, Verna Tibbetts, the captains of the two lane-6 teams (unnamed or one named), the bowlers on lane three (one heckler's voice).
**Want:** Ellen wants to throw one ball competently in front of the town and get back behind the counter. In the way: she's bad at this, Hollis has put her beside Ray, and the whole league is watching. Ray wants to see what she does when she's wrong in public. The reader learns her want when she plans the throw as a sequence and the sequence fails.
**Surface action:**
1. **PA set piece (opens the scene, short).** At the gooseneck mike she opens the league in her own phraseology: lanes one through ten, cleared to bowl (the title line). Two more phrases at most. The building laughs at the first and goes quiet at the second, because her voice carries across ten lanes without being raised.
2. Lane five. Ray has already thrown on six and left wood. She throws the ceremonial ball. It hops into lane six and wipes out his wood.
3. Silence, then everyone at once. Verna, with the binder, and the captains rule on the spot: re-rack, re-bowl the box. "No precedent since 1979." Ray loses the wood he built and doesn't complain.
4. **The longing, played slowly (the chapter's high point).** Ray walks her ball back up lane six himself and holds it out, and when she takes it he doesn't let go at once. Both their hands on it. "Nice arc. Wrong lane." She laughs before she can stop it, and catches him looking at her mouth. Her interior runs long here: first the ball, second the ruling, third she will not look at his mouth, and she's already behind on the third (bible interior line 2 is the model).
5. From lane three: "Tower, say our altitude." / "Five hundred and twelve feet. It's on the survey." She wins the heckle by answering it exactly.
6. Ten minutes later, at seven: lane seven's machine gives a noise like a chair dragged across an upstairs floor and stops mid-sweep. Seven goes dark. Eight bowlers stand there holding balls.
7. Ellen takes out her phone and records the noise while the machine is still making it. (Ch 2 opens on this recording.)
8. Ray walks over to seven's approach and listens to the knock long enough that she sees him put a name to it.
**Subtext:** The ball he holds a beat too long is the first time either of them has touched anything the other was holding. He wants her to ask him for seven; she knows it, and the doorway has already settled that she won't.
**Texture budget:** (1) The PA set piece (capped). (2) The interference ruling; it shows the town has rules for everything except her. The lane-3 heckle counts as texture too, so nothing else technical. No pinsetter parts named: Ray names the part in Ch 2, not here.
**Point of view:** Ellen.
**Ends on:** **Cut.** Ray, back to her, still looking at the dead machine: "Not my alley." Cut there, on the line, before she answers.
**Word target:** 900

### Scene 4 — Nine lanes, the snack bar, the sheet
**Setting:** The counter and the snack bar, about 7:20 p.m. to close, about 9:45. Sensory anchor: the split red vinyl stool that catches a pant leg, and the fryer basket's hiss.
**Characters:** Ellen, Arlene Fortier, Denise Lavoie, Ray (across the room, no dialogue with Ellen in this scene).
**Want:** Ellen wants a contingency for March first that doesn't run through Ray. In the way: the woman who holds the note has her own date. Later in the scene she wants to know what the town thinks her chances are, and finds out. The reader learns the first want when she puts "half" to Arlene, and the second when she reaches for her wallet at the sheet.
**Surface action:**
1. On the PA, plainly: lane seven is unable for the night; eight bowlers refunded in full at the counter, now. She hands it back herself, out loud. She runs nine lanes. (One ATC phrase; no money figure.)
2. Arlene, eighty-four, on a split stool with a coffee, has watched all of it. Ellen doesn't ask for an extension; she briefs a contingency, as if Arlene had asked: if she were to bring half on March first and the rest by summer. Arlene: "The Pines doesn't take half, dear." One more line from Arlene at most: they hold her place until March first, and after that somebody else gets it. That's why the date can't move. Ellen says nothing to it.
3. Across the room Ray squares the house balls on the rack by size, as he must have done for twenty years. He peels one of Ellen's masking-tape labels off the maple and re-sticks it on the steel, and doesn't look over (one sentence; neither mentions it). Arlene, watching him: "He'd have kept it nice." Leave the line alone; Ellen doesn't answer it.
4. Denise, at the register (red polo from 2011, pink at the shoulders), rings out and lets the night go by in one line, honey first: she's under for the night. One clause, Denise's, about Ray: twenty years and he has never left a machine down on league night. (If "eight days" goes anywhere in the chapter, it goes here, from Denise, once.)
5. Denise unclips the pool sheet from her clipboard. It lives behind the register at Gagnon & Son; the Bench drew it up at noon, an hour after the closing. A square for every date from tomorrow to March first, the date the new owner quits or loses it. Most of December is taken. Denise holds the sheet and the cash and buys nothing herself; she's the bank.
6. Ellen finds March first, blank. She takes out her wallet. Denise: owners don't play.
**Subtext:** Every person in the building has a date for her, and none of them is betting on her. The one blank square is Ray's purchase date, and it tells her something about him she can't put in order. The chapter promise sits under this: the only man who can fix her machines is the one buying if she fails, and he won't bet against her on the day it would pay him.
**Texture budget:** (1) The PA refund for lane seven, one ATC phrase; it shows she pays for her own failures in public, at once. (2) The pool sheet; it shows the town's opinion in a form she can read. Nothing else.
**Point of view:** Ellen.
**Ends on:** **Question.** Ellen, wallet open: "Then who's holding it?" Denise: "Nobody. Ray won't." Last line is Denise's. No reaction beat, no interior after it.
**Word target:** 700

## Chapter promise (outline)
Can she make it without the one man who can fix her machines, when he's also the one buying if she fails? S4 sets it up: lane seven is dark behind her, she is in the red, Arlene's date can't move, and Ray turns down the only square that would pay him.
