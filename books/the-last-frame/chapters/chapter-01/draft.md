# Chapter 1: Cleared to Bowl

The knock came while Ellen was on the stepladder behind the counter, with a fresh strip of masking tape laid across seven of the ten lane-light toggles, and she kept going. Through the glass of the front door, past the vestibule and its corkboard of bean-supper flyers, a tall man stood in the falling snow with one fist raised at the height of his face, and in the fist, catching the counter light, was a key.

She ran the tape over the eighth toggle and the ninth and the tenth, pressed it flat with her thumb along its whole length, and tore it clean against the edge of the panel. Arlene's old tape still showed under hers at either end, gone the yellow of a smoker's ceiling, with the lane numbers written on it in a hand that had wandered over fifty years. Ellen had written hers in black marker, one number to a switch, and the panel was the first thing in the building entirely in her writing. He watched her finish through the glass, and when her thumb came off the end of the strip he knocked again, three raps, and the glass rattled in its frame. Then she came down the ladder.

She had owned Kettle Lanes since eleven o'clock that morning. In the six hours since, she had found the breaker the popcorn machine tripped, the drawer where Arlene kept the fuses, the cellar light, and the coffee urn's habit of running dry with no warning, and she had put all of it in order on a legal pad beside the register. The man at the door had a key to every item on the pad, and he was nowhere on it. Snow had settled on both shoulders of his brown canvas jacket and in the gray of his crew cut, and he had not let himself in, though her lock would have taken his key as easily as hers. She saw that as she crossed the vestibule, and it annoyed her more than the key did.

She opened the door. The cold came in around her ankles, and the man stayed out on the granite. He was taller than she had planned for, and she had planned for most things. Over his shoulder, down at the road, the sign on its pole had come on for the night, red script under a white candlepin with the first letter dark, so that it told the whole of Kettle Road this was ETTLE LANES.

"Mrs. Daly," he said.

"Ms."

He let that sit a second, which she gathered was the point. "Ray Gagnon. I coach your league, Tuesdays and Thursdays, and I've had a key to this door a good long while, and I thought tonight I'd better knock, seeing as the door's yours."

She put out her hand. "I'll take the key."

"That's not asking."

"I don't ask."

"I noticed." He closed his fingers over it. "Arlene gave it to me, and Arlene can ask for it back, and I'd hand it to her in a minute. She's had all fall to think about it."

"You have no business with this building, Mr. Gagnon."

"Funny thing about that. I've got business with it on March first, same as you." He said it pleasantly, a man reading out somebody else's forecast. "Either you hand Arlene her quarter of a million that morning, or I pay her off that afternoon, and the contract you signed sells it to me by suppertime. You know what you signed."

"I know exactly what I signed. I signed so Arlene gets paid. By me."

"Appraised value," he said. "Not a dollar under. I'm not buying your mistake cheap."

"Then you have a long winter in front of you," she said, "and a cold one." She had meant to shut the door on that. She had meant to go back to the ladder and the legal pad and the eleven items left on it, and she stood in the doorway with her heat going out past her and wanted him to stay on the step and keep arguing, and to call her by the wrong title again so she could correct it, and to tell her one more thing about her own contract that she already knew, in that low voice that took its time getting to the end of a sentence, while the snow went on collecting on his shoulders under her porch light, and none of that was in any order she could use.

"I've got a league to coach," he said. "Ten teams coming up your drive inside the hour, and the northwest side of your lot's drifting in again, and it's some cold out here, and you're letting all your heat out to make a point."

She had meant to keep him outside until she had the key. She moved her shoulder an inch, and he went in past her into the warm with the key still shut in his fist.

\* \* \*

By six minutes past six the vestibule smelled of wet wool and road salt, and the league was coming in out of the snow in twos and fours, unwinding scarves, calling across the shoe cubbies to people they had stood behind at the post office that morning. At the inner door the warm air off the fryer met them, and every one of them stopped a second to breathe it.

Ellen stood behind the counter and counted the room as it filled. First, the ones moving: the Highway Department crew straight off their trucks with their hoods still up, and a small old woman in a lavender parka zipped to the chin with a three-ring binder held against her chest. Next, the ones who had stopped: Arlene Fortier on a snack-bar stool with her coat still on and a coffee in both hands, who had sold her the building at eleven and come back at six to see who she had sold it to, and the dozen at the cubbies holding their shoes and not putting them on. Forty-three people, and every one of them had looked at the counter at least once in five minutes. Ellen was fifty-eight and two years retired by statute, and for most of her working life rooms full of people had looked at a radar scope, or the weather, or the clock, and never at the woman in the chair in front of it, and she stood there with forty-three of them looking at her and could think of nothing useful to do about it but finish the count.

Ray came out through the steel door by lane ten, wiping his hands on a rag. On his way past the trophy case he rapped one knuckle on the glass, once, and kept walking. Ellen looked where he had not. Among fifty years of tarnished cups stood a framed card, Women's House High Single, Linda Gagnon, a hundred and eighty-seven, with a photograph of a dark-haired woman caught at the release. Beside the case hung locker fourteen with a brass padlock through the hasp. Ellen had tried every key Arlene gave her in every lock she owned that afternoon, and the padlock was the one none of them fit. She filed it. Nobody explained it.

Ray dropped his key ring on the counter and sat on the end stool to change his shoes. Among a truck key and a dozen others was the key from his fist, and an orange paper tag gone soft at the edges, with R.G. and 1996 on it in ballpoint.

A broad man in a blaze-orange watch hat set a lane sheet on the counter and turned it to face her. "Hollis Pike," he said. "You thrown one?"

"No."

"Ceremonial first ball. Lane five." He wrote her name in with a golf pencil. "Ray's team is on six." His face did not change when he said it, and Ellen understood it had been arranged, most likely days ago and most likely on a napkin. "Small ball, no holes, three balls a box," Hollis said. "Throw it like you mean to let go of it."

"Noted."

She took the phone book from the shelf under the register, found the page, and found one locksmith in Ambrose. She brought the rotary phone out of the office on its long cord, set it on the counter in front of the league, and dialed, and every number went around and came back with a whir the whole cubby line could hear.

"Gagnon and Son, Danny speaking," a man said, over a cash drawer shutting.

"This is Ellen Daly at Kettle Lanes. I need two commercial locks rekeyed, front door and back, first thing tomorrow. I'd like to speak to your locksmith."

"The locksmith's my father," Danny said. "He's in your building. If I know him he's about four feet from you with a shoe in his hand. You want to tell him yourself?"

She looked across the counter. Ray had one shoe on and the other in his hand, held by the heel, and he had stopped lacing anything.

"No," she said. "I'll book it with you. Eight o'clock tomorrow morning, the front and the back. I'll be here from seven."

She heard a pencil, and a man working to keep his voice level. "Eight o'clock," Danny said. "Front and back. I'll put it on his sheet."

At the cubbies somebody laughed and stopped. Ellen hung up. Ray put his foot up on the rung of the stool and started on the second shoe, a double knot, and spoke to the laces.

"He takes his coffee black, the locksmith," he said. "And he's getting on some, so he'll want somebody to hold the light."

\* \* \*

At half past six she bent to the gooseneck microphone. The PA hummed a second before she spoke, and the noise of the room did not change for it.

"Good evening. This is Ellen Daly, the owner." A few heads turned. "Lanes one through ten, cleared to bowl."

The laugh started at the far end with the Highway Department and came down the burnt-orange settees to the counter, and on lane two somebody said "Roger, roger," into his cupped hands. She let it run out. "Any machine trouble, bring it to the counter with your lane number," she said, at the same level, "and I'll take it in sequence." She did not raise her voice, and it reached lane ten as plainly as the end of the counter, and by the end of the sentence the building had gone quiet to hear her finish. She walked to lane five with the room watching.

Ray had already thrown on six and left four pins standing, with the fallen ones lying across the deck in front of them, and his team was discussing what his second ball would do with all that wood. Hollis put a ball in her hand. It fit like a grapefruit and weighed more than any grapefruit she had ever bought, and from the foul line the lane ran off a good deal farther than it had looked from the counter, sixty feet of old maple narrowing to ten pins no thicker than her wrist.

She put it in order. First, four steps. Second, the arm straight back and straight through. Third, let go low, at the line, like she meant to let go of it. She took the four steps, and her arm went straight back and came straight through, and her hand let go a full second late and a foot too high. The ball came down with a crack that carried to the snack bar, took one hop off the old wood, cleared the low board between the lanes, and rolled into six as if it had been invited. It went through Ray's wood and two of his standing pins and swept the lot into the pit.

For a second nobody said anything, and then everyone did. The Gagnon & Son team wanted the box to stand, and the other team wanted it to stand differently, and the woman in the lavender parka, whom Hollis called Verna, set her binder on the scorer's table at six and turned pages until both captains ran out of things to say. "Interference from the adjoining lane," she said. "The secretary finds no precedent since 1979. Re-rack and re-bowl the box, if the captains agree." The captains agreed. Ray had lost every bit of the wood he had built. He watched the machine on six set a fresh rack, and said, "Fair enough," and nothing else.

Her ball came back up the return between the two lanes with a hollow rumble she felt through the soles of her rented shoes, and Ray lifted it off the rack before she could reach it, walked the three steps over to five, and held it out. She took it, and he kept hold. For a second, and then for longer than a second, they both had a hand on it, his underneath and hers on top, the ball warm from the machine and from his palm, and this close she could see the nicks across his knuckles and an old white seam of scar along the side of one finger where a tool had slipped a long time ago, and she could tell through the ball, or believed she could, the moment he decided to go on holding it a little longer.

"Nice arc," Ray said. "Wrong lane."

She laughed before she could stop it, out loud, in front of the whole Tuesday division, and when she looked up he was looking at her mouth. She took the ball. First, the ball, which she now had. Second, the ruling, which was Verna's and final. Third, she would not look at his mouth, and she had already looked, and she was behind on the third, and he was still standing there with his empty hand at his side to see what she would do about it.

"Tower," somebody called from lane three, a big voice out of the Legion team. "Say our altitude."

She turned with the ball in both hands. "Five hundred and twelve feet," she said. "It's on the survey."

Lane three took a second to decide whether it had been insulted, and then the man beside the heckler slapped him on the back hard enough to put his coffee on the floor.

She went back behind the counter and stayed there. At seven o'clock by the clock over the cubbies, the machine at the end of lane seven made a noise like a chair dragged across an upstairs floor, long and grinding, then a knock, and the sweep came halfway down and stopped with the pins still under it. The motor went on grinding against whatever had stopped it. The light over seven went out. Eight bowlers stood on the approach holding balls they had nowhere to throw.

Ellen was at seven's settee with her phone out before anyone on it had turned around. She held it up toward the pit and recorded the noise while the machine was still making it, twenty seconds of it, the knock coming through at the same interval each time.

Ray came across from six and stood on seven's approach with his back to her and his head a little to one side, and listened. She watched him listen, and saw the moment it had a name for him, a small nod to nobody. Then the motor cut out by itself.

Ray, his back still to her, was looking at the dead machine. "Not my alley."

\* \* \*

"Lane seven is unable for the night," she said into the gooseneck at twenty past seven. "Lane seven, come to the counter, please. You'll be refunded in full, now."

They came in a line with their bowling shoes still on, and she opened the drawer and counted each bowler's lineage back into each hand herself, out loud, so the people at the cubbies could hear exactly what she was paying for. Then she ran nine lanes.

Arlene Fortier had watched all of it from the snack bar, on the end stool, with her coffee in both hands and her pocketbook on her knees. Ellen came around the counter, and the stool beside Arlene's caught her slacks on its split red vinyl and held on until she freed them. Behind the grill the fryer basket went into the oil with a hiss. Ellen stayed standing.

"One contingency," she said, "for your planning. If I were to bring you half on March first, and the balance by the end of June, in writing, at the note rate."

Arlene was eighty-four and had been in business for most of it, and she took a sip of coffee before she answered. "The Pines doesn't take half, dear." She set the cup in its saucer without a sound. "They hold my place until the first of March. After that it goes to the next woman on the list, and she's been packed since August."

Ellen said nothing to that. First, the date could not move. Second, she had known it could not move before she opened her mouth, and she had asked anyway, in front of the fryer, with Denise listening, which she would have to think about later and in private.

Across the room Ray was squaring the house balls on the rack between four and five, smallest to largest, giving each one a quarter-turn until it sat. That afternoon Ellen had labeled every shelf of the rack, a strip of tape on the old maple with the size written on it. He peeled one of her labels off the wood, moved it two inches to the steel upright, pressed it flat with his thumb, and went on to the next ball, and didn't look over.

Arlene watched him do it. "He'd have kept it nice."

Ellen didn't answer that either.

At a quarter to ten Denise Lavoie rang out the register, in a red Kettle Lanes polo from 2011 gone pink at the shoulders, read the tape, and tore it off. "Honey, you're under for the night, and that's counting the fries and the Highway crew, who ate like they'd plowed to Concord and back. And don't look at him like that. Twenty years he's fixed Arlene's machines for nothing and never once left one down on a league night, and he lost this place to you by eight days, and he still came early, so you tell me what that was, because I gave up guessing."

She reached under the register, unclipped a sheet from her clipboard, and laid it on the counter. "Now this isn't mine. It lives on the wall behind the register down at Gagnon's, and the Bench drew it up at noon, an hour after you closed, and I'm carrying it tonight so the Thursday people can get in. I hold the money, and the bank doesn't buy squares."

It was a pool sheet ruled by hand, with a square for every date from tomorrow to March first, and across the top somebody had printed WHEN SHE QUITS OR LOSES IT. Most of December was taken, in pencil, in a dozen hands. Somebody named Normie had Christmas Eve. Somebody had bought the fourth of January and written beside it, *after the oil bill*. Ellen ran a finger down the columns to the last square. March first was blank.

She took her wallet out and opened it.

"Owners don't play," Denise said.

Ellen held the wallet open on the counter. "Then who's holding it?"

"Nobody," Denise said. "Ray won't."
