## 1. Pull score: 5 / 10

The setup has a real engine: a dated deadline, a rival who holds a key, a betting pool on when she fails, and a locked locker. But I had to dig the engine out of slow, dense, chained sentences, and the stakes are a contract clause I only half understood. I would open Chapter 2. I would not be sorry to put it down halfway through Chapter 2.

**Warning: I'd keep reading, but only because I'm curious what the contract actually says. Nothing in the chapter made me feel anything was at risk tonight.**

## 2. Where I got bored

- **Paragraph 2 (the tape).** "She ran the tape over the eighth toggle and the ninth and the tenth, pressed it flat with her thumb along its whole length, and tore it clean against the edge of the panel." A man is knocking and I get a full paragraph of masking tape and Arlene's handwriting. I understand the point (she makes him wait, and the panel is hers now), but it is the first page and I'm already being asked to wait too.
- **Paragraph 4 (the legal pad).** "She had found the breaker the popcorn machine tripped, the drawer where Arlene kept the fuses, the cellar light, and the coffee urn's habit…" An inventory. I skimmed it.
- **Scene 2, the room count. This is where my attention first drifted for real.** "Ellen stood behind the counter and counted the room as it filled. First, the ones moving…" A list of people I haven't met, sorted into categories, about 200 words long. I lost who was who and stopped trying. Too many names in a row: Denise, the lavender woman, Arlene, the Highway crew.
- **Scene 3, the bowling mechanics.** "Ray had already thrown on six and left four pins standing, with the fallen ones lying across the deck in front of them, and his team was discussing what his second ball would do with all that wood." I don't know candlepin. "Wood", "the box", "deck", "re-rack": I was being taught a sport, not told a story. Verna's ruling is a nice beat, but the dispute around it went by in a blur.
- **Scene 4, the refund and Arlene.** "counted each bowler's lineage back into each hand herself." I don't know what "lineage" means here, and it pulled me out. Then comes "One contingency, for your planning. If I were to bring you half on March first…", which is business talk. The scene came back to life only when Denise started talking.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **The contract.** This is the big one. Why does a contract *she* signed sell the building to *Ray* if she misses a payment? Who put him in it? "Either you hand Arlene her quarter of a million that morning, or I pay her off that afternoon." So she owns it now but hasn't paid? Is it a seller-financed note with a balloon payment, with Ray as backup buyer? "I'm not buying your mistake cheap": what mistake? And "he lost this place to you by eight days": lost how? An offer? I can guess at it, but the whole plot sits on this clause and I am guessing.
- **Who Ellen is.** "two years retired by statute", "radar scope", "cleared to bowl", "Tower, say our altitude". I got air traffic controller in the end, but only by working it out. The bowlers clearly already know, which means the town knows things about her that I don't.
- **"Rolled into six without knocking."** The ball then knocks over Ray's pins. I read it twice before I saw it was a joke about Ray knocking at the door. On first read it looks like a contradiction.
- **"The Bench".** Who or what is that? A group of old men at Gagnon's? It's dropped in with no explanation, along with Normie.
- **Ending: "Then who's holding it?" / "Nobody. Ray won't."** Holding what, the March first square? So Ray refuses to bet against her? Or refuses to bet at all? I think it means Ray is the one person who won't bet she'll fail, but I'm not sure. A last line shouldn't need decoding.
- **Gagnon and Son.** She knows his name is Gagnon. She looks up the one locksmith in town, sees "Gagnon", and doesn't connect it? Or she connects it and calls anyway to make a point? The page doesn't say which, and it matters for who she is.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **The rotary phone.** She has a cell phone (she records lane seven on it). Carrying the rotary phone out of the office on its long cord, in front of the league, to look up a locksmith in the phone book is staged. I can read it as her putting on a public show, but nobody remarks on that, so it reads like the author wanted the whir.
- **The one locksmith being Ray.** Convenient, but it's played as the joke, so I accepted it. Ray's reply ("he'll want somebody to hold the light") earns it.
- **Pressure choice, the door.** She is alone, it's night, a big stranger with a key to her building is on the step, and she wants him to "stay on the step and keep arguing". The attraction has been switched on before I've had any reason for it. I believed her irritation. The wanting came too fast, and the narration announces it.
- **Pressure choice, Arlene.** Ellen asks Arlene for a split payment in public "which she would have to think about later and in private." The page does flag it as a mistake, which helps. But this woman plans everything, and she hasn't checked her one payment option before her first night? The page says she "had known it could not move", so why ask? It reads as a scene built to deliver the Pines information.
- **The ball clearing the divider and wiping out Ray's pins.** It's a beginner's throw. I half believe it, and the comedy carries it.
- **"Five hundred and twelve feet. It's on the survey."** This one I believed completely. It's exactly what she'd say.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **The "order" tic.** "put all of it in order", "none of that was in any order she could use", "She put it in order. First… Second… Third…", "and she knew it had all come out of order and left it that way", "I'll take it in sequence". The device is used four or five times in one chapter. By the third time I could see the author's hand.
- **First/Second/Third used as a device three times:** the room count, the throw, and the handoff. The handoff paragraph ("First, the ball, which she now had. Third, she would not look at his mouth…") is clever on purpose, and the cleverness is the problem. It's a feeling explained through a gimmick.
- **Feelings explained after the gesture.** "That annoyed her more than the key did." "She stood in the doorway… and wanted him to stay on the step and keep arguing… and none of that was in any order she could use." The second one is a whole paragraph telling me she's attracted, before he has done anything to earn it.
- **Neat lines closing paragraphs and scenes.** "She filed it. Nobody explained it." "He let that sit a second." "a man reading out somebody else's forecast". Every scene ends on a crisp button: "with the key still shut in his fist." / "somebody to hold the light." / "Not my alley." / "Ray won't." Four scenes, four punchlines. Each works on its own. Together they feel built.
- **"and… and… and" chains everywhere.** The narrator chains with "and", Ray chains with "and" ("I've had a key to this door a good long while, and I thought tonight I'd better knock, seeing as the door's yours"), and so does Denise ("Twenty years he's fixed Arlene's machines for nothing and never once left one down… and he lost this place to you by eight days, and he still came early, so you tell me…"). Ellen's clipped lines stand out, but Ray, Denise and the narrator all share one breathless rhythm.
- **"gone the yellow of a smoker's ceiling", "It fit like a grapefruit".** They're fine, but they're the kind of comparison every quiet literary romance reaches for.

## 6. What I think is going on

Linda Gagnon was Ray's wife. She's dead, I'd bet, and locker fourteen is hers, with her ball and shoes inside. Ray holds the only key, or no one does, and he can't bring himself to open it. He meant to buy Kettle Lanes from Arlene and keep it the way it was, partly for Linda. Ellen beat him to it by eight days with a deal that carries a March first balloon payment, and Ray is the contractual fallback buyer. The money is going to come up short (Denise already says she's under on night one), so the March first date will tighten all winter. Lane seven's machine is broken in a way Ray recognised by ear ("one small nod to nobody"), and he won't fix it because "Not my alley". He will fix it, probably at the low point, and that will be the turn. Ray won't bet in the pool because he doesn't want her to fail. The 1996 key tag dates something about Linda, perhaps the year she died or the year he started keeping the place running. Ending: March first comes, Ellen pays or nearly pays, Ray steps aside or they end up running the place together, the locker gets opened, and the key changes hands as a gift rather than a surrender. I'm fairly confident it ends with Ellen giving Ray a key, or Ray handing his over freely.

## 7. The listener pass

- **Scene breaks "\* \* \*".** With the backslashes, a TTS voice may read "asterisk asterisk asterisk" or "backslash". Flag.
- **The opening sentence is about 70 words**, with a counter, toggles, a vestibule, a corkboard, bean-supper flyers, a man, snow, a fist, a key and an orange tag. Aloud I lost the man by the time the key arrived.
- **The ball-handoff sentence is over 100 words** ("For a second, and then for longer than a second…"). Read aloud it is one breath with no landing. The paragraph after it, with the broken First/Third list, cannot be followed by ear: a listener hears "First… Third…" and assumes they missed "Second". That may be the point, but it sounds like a skip in the audio.
- **The room-count paragraph is a list of people with clauses inside clauses.** I couldn't hold it by ear.
- **Name load.** Ellen, Ray, Arlene, Denise, Hollis, Verna, Danny, Linda, Normie, the Bench, the Legion team, the Highway Department, The Pines, Gagnon and Son, all in about 3,300 words. A listener can't hold that many.
- **"Gagnon and Son" vs "Gagnon & Son".** Same name spelled two ways. Harmless aloud, inconsistent on the page.
- **"Ettle Lanes."** Aloud it sounds like a mispronunciation, not a dark letter. The joke barely survives the ear.
- **"rolled into six without knocking."** Aloud the callback is even harder to catch, and it sounds like a contradiction.
- **"lineage".** A listener will hear the family-tree word.
- **Numbers.** "1979", "1996", "R.G.", "a hundred and eighty-seven", "five hundred and twelve feet", "quarter of a million", "six minutes past six", "half past six", "seven o'clock", "twenty past seven", "a quarter to ten", "Forty-three people". All readable, but the run of clock times in Scenes 2 to 4 starts to sound like a log.
- **Repeated shape.** "She ran… She had owned… She opened… She put out… She took… She looked… She heard… She let… She walked… She put… She took… She turned… She went back…" Aloud, Ellen's paragraphs keep opening the same way.
- **Speakers.** Clear throughout. The "Tower" heckler is well tagged. The last two lines are clear on who speaks but not on what "it" is.

## 8. The romance

Yes, in two places, and I wanted more of both.

- **The laugh:** "Nice arc," Ray said. "Wrong lane." I smiled, and she laughed, so it landed for both of us.
- **The locksmith exchange:** "He takes his coffee black, the locksmith," he said. "And he's getting on some, so he'll want somebody to hold the light." This is the best thing in the chapter. He's beaten, he's charming about it, and he's inviting himself into her morning. This is the line that made me want them together.
- **The ball handoff:** the scar and the warm ball work physically. Then the order gimmick in the next paragraph cooled it for me.
- **Against:** the doorway paragraph, where she wants him to keep arguing, tells me the attraction before I feel it. And Denise's "don't look at him like that" is a stock beat.

## 9. What I want to know next

- What exactly does the contract say, and how did Ray end up written into it?
- Who was Linda Gagnon, and what's in locker fourteen?
- What's wrong with lane seven, and does Ray know?
- How far under is she on night one, and can she reach a quarter of a million by March first?
- Will Ray actually show up at eight tomorrow to rekey his own key out of the door? That's the scene I most want to read.
- Is Ray refusing to bet against her, or refusing to bet at all?

They are real questions, but they're about plot facts. None of them makes me worry for anyone.
