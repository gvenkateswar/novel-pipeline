# Judge: Buyer (Sonnet). A = v2.md, B = v1.md

1. **Winner:** A

2. **Why:** A keeps the two of them talking to each other in the lane scene (the back-and-forth about the time, "Then I'll tell her the time as well"), where B hands me that stretch as a summary of questions asked and answers shouted; A also opens on the scrape and the knock and gets me into the store faster.

3. **Where A lost me:** Barely. My thumb sped up once, late, at the paragraph that explains the key: "The key opened her front door. It opened it at four in the morning, or at noon on a Sunday, or at ten on a Thursday night with seven grinding, without his knocking..." (about 92%). That is a long run of him working out what it means, and I'd already got it from the key on the counter. The paperwork paragraph just before it ("He had left it in the drawer unsigned, and at noon Wednesday he had opened the drawer...") also slowed me a little (about 90%). I kept reading both times.

4. **Where B lost me:** "She asked what the part was called that he'd just set on the floor, and how he could tell from the lot, and he called every answer up the lane louder than it needed." (about 55%). This is the one scene where the couple is alone together, and it gets summarized instead of played. Before that, "He'd said how he took it once, to a shoelace." (about 28%) made me stop and wonder what it meant. The last scene opens on the credit union form and the loan officer's number, "At ten to five the credit union's form lay flat under Ray's left hand, and the loan officer's number was on a slip by the phone" (about 78%), and that read like setup. I'd have kept going, but more slowly.

5. **Confidence:** medium. B has the funnier bowling line ("at about the speed of a man looking for a parking space") and the better "He doesn't take bowlers." A has the stronger banter, the sharper Normie/Ellen exchange ("Score it in the right column"), and the scene I'd actually stay for.

6. **Same-author check:** Yes. Same beats, same Worcester envelope and "That's the bell" ending, and one voice; they read like two passes at the same draft.
