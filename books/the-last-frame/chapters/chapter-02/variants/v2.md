# Chapter Two: Five O'Clock

The sound came out of her phone on his counter at half past seven by the clock over the key cutter: a long scrape, like a chair dragged across an upstairs floor, then a knock, then the same wait, and the scrape again.

Ray had been counting a drawer Danny had already counted, twenties first, when the bell went over the door and the cold came in with her and lifted the birdseed and cut brass up off the aisles. Three retired men sat on three stools by the coffee pot, and Normie, on the end, called whatever happened in the store the way the Saturday show out of Boston called a string. She had pulled one glove off with her teeth, laid the phone flat by his hand and touched it, and she had not said good morning. Her cheeks still had the lot's cold in them. At the far register Danny stopped pricing a box of hinges.

Ray watched her hand on the phone. Last night that hand had finished the tape before it came down the ladder.

"And the owner's brought the game film," Normie said, low.

Ray shut his eyes. The scrape came round, and the knock, and he had it before they came round again. She reached to play it a second time.

"Leave her be," he said. "I've got her. That's seven's clutch, slipping and catching, and the knock's the sweep coming down on her every time she catches. She's sulking, is all. There's a spare on a nail behind seven with my writing on the tag."

She put on the reading glasses from her cord, took a pen out of the cup on his counter without asking, and wrote it on a pad from her vest pocket in small square printing he couldn't read from his side. He hadn't stood and watched anybody do anything in six years. He lost his count of the twenties.

"I'd like the name of the part, and your rate," she said. "Lane seven bowls tomorrow at half past six."

"Name's a clutch. You wrote it down."

"Your rate."

"Haven't got one. Arlene never asked me for one."

"Then I'll find someone who has one."

He'd have given her the part and the hour and both his hands for the asking, and she had brought money. "Go ahead and look," he said. "It's a short list, and most of it winters in Florida." He squared the stack and started the twenties over from the top.

"And the veteran declines," Normie said.

Ellen took the glasses off and let them drop on the cord, and turned her head along the three stools, one man at a time.

"He declined payment," she said. "Score it in the right column."

The Bench took that without a word, and Normie looked into his coffee.

She was at the door, with the bell already going over her head, when she turned.

"Eight o'clock, Mr. Gagnon. I'll hold the light."

\* \* \*

At ten past eight the cylinder was half out of her front door, and Ray reached into the bag for the flashlight, and it was already on, in her hand, aimed at his work. The back door was done. The front stood open to the lot, the wind off the lake found the gap at his collar, and the warm fryer air from inside came out to meet it at the sill, where she had set a cup of coffee, black, beside his knee.

She held the light steady, which most people can't, and close, which most people won't. Her breath was on the back of his wrist. His hands stopped, and he let them stay stopped, and she didn't move the light or say a word. When he went on, the light went ahead of his hands, to where they were going.

He pushed the plug out, and the old pins came down into his palm, small and brass and worn bright at the tips. The trash bucket was two feet from his knee. He tipped them into his shirt pocket and buttoned the flap, and drank some of the coffee, which was the way he took it.

"I've got an offer to make on you and everything behind you," he told the cylinder, "and I'd like it heard before tomorrow."

"You've said that to the lock."

"Lock's the one I'm working on."

He set the cup down and sat back on his heels and said the rest to her face.

"Arlene had the place listed two years, and the only other one ever came up the drive was a fella from Concord who wanted the lot at land value, for condos. So she sold it to you over me. Then she came down to the store and asked me to sign behind you in case you fell, and I signed, because she asked."

Something went across her face at *in case you fell*, and the light stayed exactly where it was.

"Now I'm asking you. Sell it to me now, at what you paid, every dollar back, the house and all." A plow went by down on Kettle Road with its blade up and its chains going. "What you paid's more than it'll appraise, and the only way I get there is to put the store up behind it. My father put the store up once, in 'seventy-four, and I watched it nearly go. I was nine, and he sat out in the truck in the dooryard every night that winter with the engine off." He let that go by. "Credit union sits tomorrow at five. I'm asking it once."

She didn't answer the offer. She answered with the bank.

"Granite Lakes writes me the loan the day this winter clears thirty thousand, with every dollar it makes still sitting in the account."

"So it's got to come in," he said, "and then it's got to stay put."

"Every dollar."

"Nobody's cleared thirty in this building since Arlene's Normand, and he's been gone since before Danny was born."

"Then it's been done."

He waited for the rest of it. She gave him something else.

"I bought it to hear all ten going at once." The light held on the plug. "Last night I heard nine."

He had nothing for that. He shook his head once, at the plug, and put his hands back on it.

Her phone went in her vest. She took it out left-handed and kept the light where it was.

"Kate. I'm at the alley with the locksmith."

Something long came down the line.

"The house."

Something short.

"Not the rest."

A pause, as long as it took him to set a pin.

"I swear."

"Go to bed, Kate. Sunday at seven."

Ray kept his eyes on the pin the whole time.

He seated the new cylinder and worked the new key twice, and it went over smooth both times. Then he stood and counted the new keys into her palm, two for the front and two for the back. "That's every one there is."

Her hand closed on them.

He picked up the bag. "Then I'll see you March first."

"You'll have your answer before your committee sits."

He took his ring out of his jacket pocket, found the orange tag gone soft with handling, and slid his old key into her new lock. It went in all the way and turned nothing. He worked it back out and let it drop among the others.

\* \* \*

At ten o'clock the lock he had pinned that morning turned from her side, and she stood in the back doorway in her vest with the storeroom light behind her. Her text had come to the store at four, three sentences and three periods and no question mark: *Lane seven. Ten o'clock. I will let you in at the back.* He had come because tomorrow was league night.

"You'll have to come down and let me in," he said, "every time one of them acts up."

"Then they had better not act up."

In the pit one caged bulb burned over the seven-and-eight pair. The spare clutch had already come down off its nail and lay on the bench under it, his tag still wired on, and beside the tag a strip of her tape that said LANE SEVEN in black marker. Next to that lay an envelope with *R. Gagnon* on it in the same marker. He left it where it was.

She didn't stay. She went back out through the steel door and up to seven's approach, and the lane lights came on over seven and nowhere else.

"How long?" Her voice came down sixty feet of maple to him whole, at the level she might use across a table.

"Depends on her," he called. He had to raise his. "Twenty minutes, if this bolt lets go. Longer if she wants to make a point of it."

"League is at half past six tomorrow."

"I know when league is."

"It's eleven minutes past ten."

"She can hear you, you know. She'll go slower out of spite."

"Then I'll tell her the time as well."

He put the wrench on the bolt and leaned. Nothing, and nothing, and then the first quarter-turn gave, and he felt it go in his back teeth. He held the wrench there a second longer than the bolt needed, and then gave it the rest. The old clutch came off in his hands with its facing worn down to the rivets, and the new one went onto the shaft the way a good part does, without any argument. He threw the switch, and seven's motor came up around him, and the warmth came off it into the cold of the pit.

"Throw one," he called.

Her ball came down the lane slow and a little sideways, like a cart with one bad wheel, and took three pins off the left side and left the other seven standing there to talk it over. Seven swept and set clean, with no knock under it. He had heard everything he needed.

"Again," he called.

She threw again. This one went straight and took one.

When she came back through the steel door he had the envelope open. Inside, folded around the bills, was a page from her pad in her square printing: a pinsetter man in Worcester, his rate by the hour, his hours, the part, and a total at the bottom underlined twice.

"I found someone who has a rate," she said. "That's his."

"Machine's fixed," Ray said. "As for the bowler, I'd send her to Worcester."

"He only does machines. I asked."

He took the page out and folded it once more and put it in his shirt pocket with the old pins, and held the envelope out to her with the money still in it. "I'll keep the paperwork."

"An invoice is paid in money, Mr. Gagnon. That is how an invoice works."

"It's how this one works."

She took the envelope back. She stood in the pit holding it, owing him, and she didn't put it down.

At the back door, with his bag in his hand and the cold coming in past them both, she spoke first.

"Your committee sits at five tomorrow."

"You'll know where to find me."

"At your counter. Under the clock."

\* \* \*

At two minutes to five the bell went over the door.

Ray had the guard screw on the key cutter run down as far as it would go and was giving it another eighth anyway, and he had not said a word since four. It had been dark since a little after. The Bench was on its stools, and Normie, who had bowled with Lucien the winter the store nearly went, hadn't called a thing all afternoon.

It was Ellen, and the Bench went quieter than it had been. The coffee pot ticked on its plate. Somebody by the stools set a cup down on the counter with great care. At the far register Danny had a bag of rock salt halfway up to the shelf, and he held it there in the air and left it.

She came down the center aisle, under the roof rakes, to the counter. She pulled the glove from her right hand to get at her vest pocket and laid one key on the counter. A new key, no tag, no ring. She said nothing. It lay between his left hand, flat on the credit union's form, and Danny's name signed at the bottom of the form. He didn't need to pick it up. He knew the cut: her front door, cut on his own machine yesterday before seven.

She looked up at the clock over the key cutter, and he looked where she looked.

The minute hand sat one mark short of the twelve. All afternoon he had heard it jump from one minute to the next with a small hard click he could pick out over the coffee pot, and now he could not hear the coffee pot at all.

The key opened her front door. It opened it at four in the morning, or at noon on a Sunday, or at ten on a Thursday night with seven grinding, without his knocking and without her coming down through the dark building to turn the lock from her side, which was the thing they had stood at her back door about last night, and she had answered it here on his counter without asking him for one thing.

She was keeping the building. She had promised him an answer before his committee sat, and this was the answer, given by standing at his counter and letting his one ask run out on his own clock, in his own store, with the Bench watching. It was a no to selling. He could read that as plain as a tag.

What he couldn't read was whether it said anything past the door, and he turned that over and got nowhere with it.

Under his hand the form lay flat. He had left it in the drawer unsigned, and at noon Wednesday he had opened the drawer for a roll of register tape and found Danny's name on the line in Danny's careful hand, the store put up behind his offer by the man who would have to carry it if it went. He had never asked him. Danny had never said a word about it, and neither had he. The slip with the loan officer's number lay by the phone. One call before five, and the committee would take it up tonight. The phone was a foot from his hand.

It was the most anybody had laid on this counter without his asking since Linda.

He could ask her again. The phone was right there, and the slip beside it, and there was most of a minute left, and she stood close enough to hear him if he said it low. He had told her he would ask once. He had told himself the same a long time before that, the winter his father sat out in the truck, that he would ask the store once and never twice. He kept his hand flat on the form.

He watched her watch the clock. Her face was as calm as any face he had seen across this counter in forty years, and her breathing was wrong for it: short, then held, then let go, then held again. He heard it the way he could hear a machine that was about to go from clear out in the parking lot, and he listened to every bit of it. Her bare hand lay on the counter an inch from the key.

The minute hand jumped to the twelve.

Danny lowered the rock salt. Ellen turned and went back down the aisle without a word, and the bell rang over the door behind her.

"That's the bell," Normie said.
