# Chapter Two: Five O'Clock

Ray was counting a drawer Danny had already counted at six, twenties first. Down at the end of the counter by the coffee pot sat the Bench, three retired men on three stools who called whatever happened in the store the way the Saturday show out of Boston called a big string, with Normie, on the end, calling loudest.

At half past seven the bell over the door went, and the cold came down the center aisle ahead of her and lifted the smell of birdseed and cut brass off the shelves. She came straight to the counter in her navy vest with her color up from the lot, pulled her right glove off with her teeth, which he wouldn't have bet she'd do in front of anybody, and laid her phone face up between his drawer and the pen cup. She didn't say good morning. She touched the screen and looked at him.

Out of the little speaker came a sound like a chair dragged across an upstairs floor, and then a knock, and then the same wait, and the chair again.

Ray watched her hand. It lay flat on the counter by the phone, the knuckles red from her steering wheel and the nails cut short and square, and it stayed still the whole time the noise ran. Last night that hand had finished the tape before she came down the ladder.

"And here's the owner," Normie said, low, to the other two. "Brought her own sound."

Ray shut his eyes. He had it before the chair came round a second time: seven's clutch, slipping and catching, and the knock was the sweep coming down on her when it caught.

"Seven's clutch," he said, with his eyes still shut. "She's sulking, is all. There's a spare on a nail behind her with my writing on the tag."

In six years he hadn't much wanted to watch anybody do anything. When he opened his eyes she had the reading glasses up off their cord and on, a pen out of his cup that she hadn't asked for, and a little pad out of her vest pocket, and she was writing his answer down with her head bent and her mouth set, taking more care over it than he'd taken saying it. He lost his count of the twenties.

She capped his pen and put it back in the cup and looked at him over the glasses.

"I'd like the name of the part, and your rate."

"Name's a clutch. You wrote it down."

"Your rate."

"Haven't got one. Arlene never asked me for one."

"Then I'll find someone who has one."

"Go ahead and look," Ray said. "It's a short list, and I'm the top of it and most of the bottom." He squared the twenties against the drawer and started them over from the top. Her eyes went to his hands doing it, then up past his shoulder to the clock over the key cutter, and came back to his face. Danny, at the far register, watched his father start over and went back to his invoices.

"And the veteran declines," Normie said.

She let the glasses drop on their cord and turned her head along the three stools, the way she'd looked over the league last night.

"For the broadcast," she said. "He gave me the diagnosis for nothing and declined the money. Score it however you like."

Nobody on the Bench had a thing to say to that.

She put the pad away and pulled her glove on and went down the aisle, and she had the door open and the bell going over her head before she turned.

"Eight o'clock, Mr. Gagnon. I'll hold the light."

* * *

The cylinder was half out of her front door when Ray reached into his bag for the flashlight and came up empty. She had it on already, in her hand, aimed at his work.

The back door was done. The front stood open to the lot, and the wind off the lake found the gap at his collar. On the sill by his knee sat a cup of black coffee, where he'd see it. He'd said how he took it once, to a shoelace.

She held it steady, which most people can't, and close, which most people won't, down on one knee on the mat beside him, and her breath was on the back of his wrist, warm, and then the wind took it, and then it was there again. His hands stopped on the plug. He let them stay stopped, and she didn't move the light or say a word, and there was nothing in the doorway but the two of them and the cylinder half out of the door and the coffee going cold on the sill. When he went on, she moved the light ahead of his hands, to where they were going, and had it there before they arrived.

He pushed the plug out, and the old pins came down into his palm, small and brass and worn bright at the tips. The trash bag was two feet from his knee. He tipped them into his shirt pocket and buttoned the flap, and her light stayed on the pocket a second longer than it had any call to.

He had meant to ask it at his own counter that morning, and hadn't, and he said it to the lock.

"Thing is," he said, "I'd take the whole of this off your hands tomorrow, and you'd walk away square."

"You've said that to the lock."

"Lock's the one I'm working on."

The corner of her mouth went, and she put it back.

He sat back on his heels and said the rest to her face.

"Arlene had this place listed two years," he said, "and the only other one who came up the drive in all that time was a fella from Concord who wanted the lot at land value, for condos, and never got out of his car. So she sold it to you over me. Then she came down to the store and asked me to sign behind you, in case you fell, and I signed, because Arlene asked."

Ellen kept the light on the cylinder. Her mouth went flat at *in case you fell* and stayed that way, and she didn't look off him.

"Now I'm asking you," Ray said. "Sell it to me now, at what you paid, every dollar back, the house and all."

Nothing on her face moved but her eyes, which came to his and stayed.

"What you paid is more than it'll appraise, and the only way I get there is to put the store up behind it. My father put the store up once, in 'seventy-four, and I watched it about go. That winter he'd sit out in the truck in the dooryard with the engine off till it got dark." He looked down at the plug and back up at her. "Credit union sits tomorrow at five. I'm asking it once."

She didn't answer it. She spoke to him across the beam, level, in the same voice she'd used on the Bench.

"Granite Lakes writes me the loan the day this winter clears thirty thousand, with every dollar it makes still sitting in the account."

"So it's got to come in," Ray said, "and then it's got to stay put."

"Every dollar."

"Nobody's cleared thirty in this building since Arlene's Normand, and he's been gone since before Danny was born."

"Then it's been done."

She said it looking straight at him, the way she'd have told him the time, and he looked back at her longer than the remark called for.

"I bought it to hear all ten going at once," she said, and let that sit. "Last night I heard nine."

Ray had nothing for that. He shook his head once, slowly, at the plug, and picked up the first of the new pins and set it in its hole.

Her phone rang in her vest. She took it out left-handed and kept the light where it was, on his hands, and didn't step away from the job to answer it.

"Kate. I'm at the alley with the locksmith."

Something long came down the line in a young woman's voice, going fast. Ray kept his eyes on the pin.

"The house."

Something short.

"Not the rest."

Then nothing, for as long as it took him to set the next pin and press it home with his thumb. The light dipped a quarter inch and came back.

"I swear."

"Go to bed, Kate. Sunday at seven."

He seated the new cylinder and tried the new key in it twice, and it went over smooth both times. Then he counted the keys out of his palm into hers, two for the front and two for the back.

"That's every one there is."

She closed her hand on them.

He stood up with the bag in his hand. "Then I'll see you March first."

She stood too, with the keys in one hand and his flashlight in the other. "You'll have your answer before your committee sits."

He took out his ring and found the soft orange tag by feel and slid his old key into her new lock while she watched. It went in all the way and turned nothing. He worked it back out and let it drop among the others.

* * *

Her text came at four, while he was at the key cutter, and he read it once.

*Lane seven. Ten o'clock. I will let you in at the back.*

Three periods, and not a question mark anywhere in it. Tomorrow was league night, and he went.

At ten he came up Kettle Road under the sign with its first letter dark. He knocked at the back. The lock he'd pinned that morning turned from the inside with her key, and she stood in the doorway in her vest.

"You'll have to come down and let me in," he said, "every time one of them acts up."

"Then they had better not act up." She said it straight up at him and stood back to let him by.

The caged bulb was on over the seven-and-eight pair. The spare clutch was already down off its nail and lying on the bench under it, with his tag still wired on and, beside his tag, a strip of her tape that said LANE SEVEN in black marker. Next to it lay an envelope with *R. Gagnon* on the front in the same marker. He left the envelope where it was.

She didn't stay in the pit. She went out through the steel door, and he heard her come out onto seven's approach, up at the far end, and stop under the one lane light she'd left on.

"Tell me what you're doing."

She hadn't raised her voice. It came down sixty feet of maple to him whole, every word in its place, and in under the curtain to where he knelt with a wrench, and to answer it he had to raise his own, which he hadn't done in this building in a good many years. She asked what the part was called that he'd just set on the floor, and how he could tell from the lot, and he called every answer up the lane louder than it needed. Between questions he could hear her up there, the scuff of a shoe on the approach, and he started saving things up to tell her.

The bolt on the clutch housing had been in since the winter seven's belt went the first time, and it held against him, and held, and then gave its first quarter-turn, and he felt it in his back teeth. He sat a second with the wrench on it and let it be good.

When the new clutch was on he ran her, and the warmth started coming off the motor at his knees. "Throw one," he called.

Her ball came down the lane at about the speed of a man looking for a parking space. It wandered right, thought better of it, and took the one pin on the right-hand corner and left the other nine standing there to watch it go. Seven swept and set clean, with no knock under it anywhere. He had heard everything he needed.

"Again," Ray called.

She threw again.

She came back through the steel door into the pit. He picked up the envelope and opened it in front of her. Folded inside a page from her pad were bills, and on the page in her printing was a pinsetter man in Worcester, his rate by the hour, his hours, the part, all of it totaled at the bottom and the total underlined twice.

"I found someone who has a rate," she said. "That's his rate."

Ray read it through and looked at her over the top of it. "Machine's fixed," he said. "As for the bowler, I'd send her to Worcester."

"He doesn't take bowlers. I asked."

He took the page out from around the bills and folded it and put it in his shirt pocket, in with the old pins. He held the envelope out to her with the money still in it. "I'll keep the paperwork."

"That isn't how an invoice works."

"It's how this one works."

She took the envelope back and stood there holding it in both hands, owing him, which he could see she couldn't stand, and she didn't put it down.

He had his hand on the back door when she spoke.

"Your committee sits at five tomorrow."

"You'll know where to find me."

"At your counter," Ellen said. "Under the clock."

* * *

At ten to five the credit union's form lay flat under Ray's left hand, and the loan officer's number was on a slip by the phone. One call before five and the committee would take it up tonight. Danny's signature was on the line under his own. Ray had never asked for it. Danny had signed it anyway and put it in the drawer and never said a word, and he was out on the floor by the rock salt.

It had been dark since a little after four. The guard screw on the key cutter was loose, and Ray took a screwdriver to it and gave it a quarter-turn without a word, and another. The Bench was on its stools, and Normie hadn't called a thing all afternoon.

Ray looked at the door. The phone sat where it was.

At two minutes to five the bell went, and it was Ellen.

The Bench went quiet. The coffee pot ticked on its plate. Somebody set a cup down with care. Danny had a bag of rock salt halfway to his shoulder, and he held it there.

She came down the center aisle and stopped across the counter from him. She took her right glove off to get at her vest pocket, and her hand came out with one key in it, no tag, no ring, and she laid it on the wood between his hand and Danny's name without a word. Ray knew the cut without picking it up. Her front door. He had cut it yesterday on the machine at his elbow.

She looked up at the clock over the key cutter, and he looked up at it with her.

The minute hand stood a hair short of the twelve, the way it always stood before it jumped.

The key would let him in at her front door at any hour without knocking, without her having to come down through her own dark building and turn the lock from the inside, which was the very thing they had stood at her back door about last night. She had answered that without asking him for a single thing, with a key laid on a counter and her mouth shut.

It would not open the building. She was letting his one ask run out on his own clock, in his own store, with his son's name under his hand, and she had come down Main Street in the dark to stand here while it did, because she had told him he would have his answer before the committee sat. This was the answer. It was no to selling.

What it said past the door he couldn't read. He turned it over the way he'd turn a key somebody brought in off the street with no lock to go with it, and he couldn't find what it went to.

Since Linda, nobody had laid a thing on this counter for him that he hadn't asked for first.

He could ask her again. The phone was a foot from his hand with the slip beside it, and there was most of a minute left, and she would stand there and hear him dial. He had told her he'd ask once. He had sworn the store got asked once, the winter his father sat in the truck, and he kept his hand flat on the form.

He watched her watch the clock. Her face was as calm as any he had seen across this counter, and her breathing was wrong for it, short, and held, and let go, and held again, and he heard it the way he'd hear a machine from the lot that was about to give, and there was nothing on his side of the counter that would fix it. Her hand lay on the wood an inch from the key, bare, the knuckles still red from the cold, and her fingers did not move, and neither did his.

The minute hand jumped to the twelve.

Danny lowered the rock salt. Ellen turned and went back down the aisle without a word, and the bell rang behind her.

"That's the bell," Normie said.
