# Cold Read: Chapter 2, "Five O'Clock"

**Draft read:** `chapters/chapter-02/final.md` v2, Oct 28 (after `chapters/chapter-01/final.md`). No other files opened.

## 1. Pull score: 5 / 10

The two leads are now worth watching. The plot question, though, is a loan threshold, and the chapter ends on a gesture I can't fully decode. I'd open Ch 3 tonight. I would not stay up for it. A reader already let down twice by this kind of book closes the app here as often as she keeps going.

## 2. Where I got bored

- **The money speech on the mat, lines 61 to 69.** It starts at "Way I've got it," he said to the cylinder, "you own the building, and Arlene's carrying the note..." and runs through "the credit union lends me the rest against the store, if Danny signs, it being his name on the deed beside mine, and their committee sits Thursdays at five." It reads like a term sheet: Concord bank, appraisal, credit union, Danny's name on the deed, a committee, December 31, March 1. I stopped seeing the doorway and the cold and started doing sums. Ch 1 already gave me "appraised value" and "March first," so half of this is review. The flashlight moment just above it was the best thing in the chapter, and this paragraph flattens it.
- **Line 81, "Granite Lakes writes me the loan the day this winter clears thirty thousand... with every dollar it makes still sitting in the account."** It's another number on top of the previous ones. I understood it, but I didn't feel anything about it. Thirty thousand of what: gross, net, over how many weeks? I don't know if that's hard, and line 83 ("he could not think of one that had come near that") has to tell me it is.
- **Opening of the last scene, lines 143 to 147.** The form, Danny's signature, the loan officer's number, Ray squaring the form "for the third time since four," the key-cutter screw, and the Bench backstory about Normie and Lucien in 'seventy-four. It's setup while I wait for the bell. It picks up once the clock starts (line 149).
- **Line 135, the catwalk paragraph.** It's a nice idea, but it's long, and it re-narrates Ch 1 ("that had said *cleared to bowl* to forty-some people last night"). I skimmed it.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **What does the key mean?** (line 153). She lays a front-door key on his counter at two minutes to five, says nothing, waits out the clock and leaves. Is it a no to the offer, with the key as a consolation? An invitation to keep fixing her machines? A truce? Is "you can come in, but you can't buy it" what she means? I think that's it, but I'm guessing. Ray's interior paragraph (line 157) explains why *he* won't speak and never says what *he* thinks the key means. The chapter's climax is a gesture I can only half read.
- **Why is Ray in Ellen's contract at all?** Ch 1 says "he lost this place to you by eight days," and Ch 2 says her contract "sells the building to me that afternoon" if her bank doesn't pay. Why would a seller's note name a rival bidder as the backstop buyer? Was this Arlene's doing? It's never said. The contract is now mostly clear to me, but its oddest clause has no reason behind it.
- **"Nobody's cleared thirty in this building since Normand."** (line 89). Who is Normand? Is it Normie on the Bench, Arlene's late husband, or a former owner? Normie was introduced fourteen lines earlier, so I assumed they were the same man and then doubted it.
- **"Through the gap under the masking"** (line 135). Masking of what? I couldn't picture the catwalk.
- **Did Ray agree to fix lane seven?** In the morning he says he has no rate and she says she'll find someone else. Then she texts an order and he just goes. I bought it, but the morning scene ends with them at odds and the text acts as if it didn't.
- **I still don't know why Ellen bought a bowling alley.** I'm two chapters in. She's a retired air traffic controller who sold her house in Merrimack and swore to her daughter she wouldn't put in "the rest." What does she want this place *for*? The page hasn't said, so her side of the clock has no heat for me.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **The back door propped open on cardboard, with Ellen gone** (lines 127 to 131). She paid that morning to rekey both doors specifically to lock this man out. That night she leaves the back door unlatched on a bowling alley in a snowy lot with nobody there, so he can get in. The safer option is obvious and the page never closes it: be there, or hand him a key. (She hands him a key the next day anyway.) I can see the point she's making, that she won't stand and watch him, but her leaving a building with the till and the machines open to anyone runs against the woman who taped every toggle and labeled every shelf.
- **Ellen's silence at the clock** (lines 155 to 161). Under pressure, she lets the offer die without a word rather than say "no." That fits "I don't ask," so I believed it. But the obvious move is to say "no" on the mat that morning, and the page doesn't say why she waits for the deadline and comes in person.
- **Ray's vow** (line 157), "he would never ask the store twice." It arrives in the same paragraph it's needed, as the reason he can't speak. It feels convenient. One line in the mat scene ("I'm asking it once. This is the once") plants it, so it's half earned.
- **"Three full stops"** (line 127). A New Hampshire hardware man says "periods." That one's the writer talking, not Ray.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **Every scene ends on a button line.** "Eight o'clock, Mr. Gagnon... I'll hold the light." / "After that, I'll see you March first." / "he was on the outside of it with his hand on the cold handle." / "That's the bell," said Normie. Ch 1 did the same thing ("Not my alley." / "Ray won't."). Four for four. I could feel each ending being placed.
- **Talking to objects is now a tic.** "he said to the cylinder," "he told the lock," "he told the cylinder," "'Lock's the one I'm working on,'" and then the key-cutter screw: "'You've been backing out on me since Thanksgiving,' he told it." Ch 1 had "spoke to the laces." That's six uses across two chapters. It was charming the first time and is a device by the third.
- **Aphorisms.** "a drawer counted once was a drawer somebody was only hoping about." "Danny... didn't look over, which was how Danny said most things." These are neat little sayings, built to be neat.
- **The chapter recycles Ch 1 verbatim.** "run the tape over the eighth toggle and the ninth and the tenth, press it flat along its whole length with the thumb, and tear it clean on the edge of the panel" (line 11) is the Ch 1 sentence played back. "Ray thought of lane three last night, deciding whether it had been insulted" (line 41) is the Ch 1 line again. "*cleared to bowl* to forty-some people last night and had them listening by the last word" (line 135) is Ch 1 again. Each recap made me feel the book wasn't sure I had read Ch 1.
- **Feelings explained after the moment.**
  - "That was the first thing he ever knew about her, and he hadn't got past it yet." (line 11)
  - "He hadn't wanted to watch anybody do anything in six years." (line 21)
  - "She had told her daughter he was the locksmith, which he was. She had put her house into this and sworn the rest of what she had to her daughter, and he had just knelt on her mat and offered her the house back." (line 113) I had just heard the phone call. This paragraph tells me what I heard.
  - "He had been in here alone a hundred nights and never once listened for anybody." (line 135)
- **The glove.** The right glove comes off with her teeth (line 7), she leaves with "the one hand bare" (line 43), she's "bare-handed on the cold barrel" (line 57), the keys go into "her bare palm" (line 115), the glove comes off again and "he watched the bare hand come out of it" (line 153), and her "bare hand lay on the counter" (line 159). That's six bare hands. The motif is being hammered.
- **"like weather coming in off the lake"** (line 57). It's fine, but it's the narrator's poetry, not Ray's.

## 6. What I think is going on

Ray's wife Linda is dead, about six years ago ("hadn't wanted to watch anybody do anything in six years," "since Linda"). Locker fourteen with the brass padlock is hers, Ray has the only key, and opening it will be a late-book beat. Ellen is running from something too, probably a dead husband or a divorce. The house sale and the oath to Kate point that way, and the book is holding back why she bought this place. The thirty-thousand target will look doomed by midwinter. I think the crisis is a big machine failure that costs money she can't spend without breaking the "every dollar still sitting in the account" condition. Ray will fix it for nothing, which helps her and wrecks his own March first. The Concord condo man will come back as the real threat, maybe through Ray's lapsed financing or an appraisal. The unopened envelope is her payment, and he'll never cash it. The ending: March first, the blank square on the pool sheet, which I think Ray buys or fills. She makes the number, or nearly does and Ray declines to exercise his right. The two of them end up running it together, and someone finally fixes the K on the sign. I'm fairly confident of all of this, and that confidence is part of why my pull is middling: the shape is visible from here.

## 7. Listener pass

- **The money paragraph, lines 61 to 69**, is very hard aloud. "appraised value... Arlene's carrying the note... March first... the bank in Concord... the credit union... Danny signs... deed beside mine... committee sits Thursdays at five... December thirty-first... March first... two months." It's a dozen financial facts in a row in one voice. With TTS reading it there's no way to glance back, and I lost the thread at the credit union.
- **The notepad entry, line 21**: "*Clutch, slipping and catching. Sweep comes down on it. Spare on a nail behind seven, his tag.*" Aloud this repeats Ray's speech from two lines earlier. The voice reads the same information twice in a row.
- **"he said to the cylinder" / "he told the lock" / "he told the cylinder"**: three tags of the same shape in about eight lines. Aloud it sounds like a stuck record.
- **Line 157** is one paragraph of six long sentences inside Ray's head, with "she had never answered him," "an offer nobody had answered stood until the committee sat," and "he had sworn after 'seventy-four." Aloud, the 'seventy-four reference and the vow get lost. This is the climax, and it's the hardest passage in the chapter to follow by ear.
- **The phone call, lines 99 to 111.** We hear only Ellen's side, with "Then a short one," "Then she didn't answer." It works aloud, which surprised me. It's good.
- **Normie's first line, line 13**, comes before Normie is identified as one of the Bench. Aloud, a new name speaking in "the voice of the Saturday show out of Boston" needs a beat to place. Who is this?
- **"Normand" and "Normie"** sound nearly alike aloud. It's confusing.
- **"'seventy-four"** comes up three times (lines 65, 147, 157). Fine aloud, but repetitive.
- **The "and... and... and" runs**, for example line 133 ("and he leaned on it, and on the third try it gave its first quarter-turn, and he felt it in his back teeth"). A run is fine once, but there are several in a row across the chapter and they flatten under a TTS voice.
- **The text message, "Lane seven. Ten o'clock. The back door is open."**, works aloud.

## 8. The romance

Yes. This is the chapter's strength, and it's real.

- The flashlight (line 57): "She held the light steady, which most people can't, and close, which most people won't... His hands stopped. He let them stop, a second and then longer... When he went on, she moved the light ahead of his hands, to where they were going." This made me want them together. It's the best thing in two chapters.
- "On the counter behind him sat a cup of coffee, black, set where he would see it. He had said how he took it once, last night, to a shoelace." It made me smile.
- "'You've said all of that to the lock.' / 'Lock's the one I'm working on.'" It got a laugh out loud, and it's the only real laugh.
- "He watched her write his answer down, and he stood there with a twenty in his hand and lost his count." This is good, though the six-years line right before it explains it.
- The old key going into her new lock and turning nothing, which he does in front of her. It's a quiet, good beat.
- Normie as the Boston radio voice is mildly funny ("And the veteran declines"). "That's the bell" isn't funny. It's a button.

What's missing: we get nothing from inside Ellen this chapter. The whole chapter is Ray, so I know he's falling. I only know she is from her breathing at the clock.

## 9. What I want to know next

- What did the key mean, and what does Ray do with it?
- What's in the envelope? (Mild interest. I'm fairly sure it's money.)
- Why did Ellen buy a bowling alley? What is she after? This is the biggest gap, and it's what would make me care about March first.
- How far under was the till on night one? Denise's "ask me in the morning" was never paid off, and that number would tell me whether thirty thousand is possible.
- Linda and locker fourteen. (Low urgency. It's backstory I can see coming.)

Honest summary: the questions pulling me forward are about the two of them, not about the plot. The plot question, whether she makes thirty thousand, is a number I haven't been made to feel. Nothing in this chapter made me *need* the next one.
