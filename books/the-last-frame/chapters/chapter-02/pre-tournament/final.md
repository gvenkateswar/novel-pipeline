# Chapter 2: Five O'Clock

Danny had counted the drawer at six and written the total on the tape, and at half past seven Ray was counting it again, twenties first, because a drawer counted once was a drawer somebody was only hoping about. Danny was at the far end stocking ice melt and didn't look over, which was how Danny said most things.

The bell on the door went, and the cold came down the center aisle ahead of her and lifted the smell of birdseed and cut brass off the shelves, and the Bench, three retired men on three stools by the coffee pot, turned to see who had let it in.

Ellen Daly came to the counter with her collar up. She pulled her right glove off with her teeth, laid it by the register, and set her phone face up on the counter between his hands and the drawer. She didn't say good morning. She touched the screen with one finger.

Out of the little speaker came a sound like a chair dragged across an upstairs floor, then a knock, then the same drag again after the same wait.

Ray looked at her hand. The knuckles were red from the lot, and there was a plain steel watch on the wrist. Last night through the door glass he had watched the same hand run the tape over the eighth toggle and the ninth and the tenth, press it flat along its whole length with the thumb, and tear it clean on the edge of the panel while he stood out in the snow and knocked. She had finished the tape first. That was the first thing he ever knew about her, and he hadn't got past it yet.

"Gagnon's at the counter, folks," Normie said, low, in the voice of the Saturday show out of Boston, "and it's awful quiet in the house."

"That'd be seven," Ray said, "would it?"

"You know it is," Ellen said.

Ray shut his eyes. He let it come round once, the drag and the knock, and before it came round again he said, "That's seven's clutch, slipping and catching, and the knock's the sweep coming down on her when she catches. She's sulking, is all." He opened his eyes. "There's a spare on a nail behind seven with my writing on the tag, been there since the winter four's motor went, so I'd guess she's got some dust on her."

She put on the reading glasses that hung from a cord at her neck, took a pen out of the cup by his register without asking for it, and wrote it down on a little pad from her vest pocket, in small square printing, his own words down to the tag. *Clutch, slipping and catching. Sweep comes down on it. Spare on a nail behind seven, his tag.* He hadn't wanted to watch anybody do anything in six years. He watched her write his answer down, and he stood there with a twenty in his hand and lost his count.

"I'd like the name of the part," she said, "and your rate."

"Name's a clutch. You wrote it down."

"Your rate."

"Haven't got one. Arlene never asked me for one in twenty years."

"Then I'll find someone who has one."

"Go ahead and look," Ray said, "and take your time over it, it's a short list," and he went back to the drawer and started the twenties over from the top.

"And the veteran declines," said Normie.

Ellen turned her head and looked along the three stools, one man at a time.

"Noted," she said.

Normie opened his mouth and closed it again. The man on the end set his cup down on his knee and studied it, and Ray thought of lane three last night, deciding whether it had been insulted.

She put the glove in her pocket and picked up her phone and went down the aisle. At the door she turned, with the bell already going over her head.

"Eight o'clock, Mr. Gagnon," she said. "I'll hold the light."

The door shut on the bell before he could answer.

\* \* \*

The back door was done by twenty past eight, a storeroom latch that locked itself whenever it swung shut, and Ray was down on one knee at the front with his bag open on the mat and the door propped wide. The wind came across the lot and found the gap between his collar and his neck, and the building's warm air met it at the sill where he knelt.

On the counter behind him sat a cup of coffee, black, set where he would see it. He had said how he took it once, last night, to a shoelace.

He reached into the bag for the flashlight, and it wasn't there, and she was crouched on the mat beside him with it already on.

He started the cylinder out. She held the light steady, which most people can't, and close, which most people won't, down where his hands were working, and he could feel her breath on the back of his wrist like weather coming in off the lake. She was bare-handed on the cold barrel of it, and it didn't shake. His hands stopped. He let them stop, a second and then longer, with the cylinder half out of the door, and she didn't move the light or say a word. When he went on, she moved the light ahead of his hands, to where they were going.

He pushed the plug out, and the old pins came with it into his palm, small and brass and worn bright at the tips by sixty-some years of keys going in and out. The trash was two feet from his knee. He tipped them into his shirt pocket and buttoned the flap.

"Way I've got it," he said to the cylinder, "you own the building, and Arlene's carrying the note, and if your bank hasn't paid Arlene by March first, that paper you signed sells it to me that afternoon at appraised value, so Arlene gets paid either way."

"Correct," she said.

"Arlene wouldn't carry the note without a buyer signed in behind it," he said, "and I was the only one around who'd sign at appraised, so that's how my name got on your paper." He turned the old cylinder over in his fingers. "Or you sell it to me now, at what you paid, and you get every dollar back, house and all." He set the old cylinder down on the mat. "Committee's Thursday at five. This is the once."

She kept the light on the empty hole in the door. "You've said all of that to the lock."

"Lock's the one I'm working on."

"Who else has come up the drive?"

"Nobody. Arlene had it listed two years, and the only other one who came up it was a fella up from Concord in a car too clean for the roads, wanting the lot for condos at land value. He never came inside."

Ellen looked out the open door at the drive, and the light stayed where it was.

"Granite Lakes writes me the loan the day this winter clears thirty thousand," she said, at the level she'd used on the PA, and it went out the open door and across the lot, "with every dollar it makes still sitting in the account."

It was an answer, and he had asked her something else. He had known this building's winters since he was a boy, by what had broken in each of them, and he could not think of one that had come near that.

"So it's got to come in," he said, "and then it's got to stay put."

"Every dollar."

Ray sorted the new pins in his palm. "Nobody's cleared thirty in this building since Normand."

"Then it's been done."

He closed his hand on the pins. "Mrs. Daly."

"Ms."

Her phone went off in her vest. She took it out with her left hand and kept the light where it was with her right, and she didn't stand or step off the mat or turn her back on the job, so he heard every word.

"Kate," she said. "I'm at the alley with the locksmith."

He set the first pin and kept his eyes on it, because it was the only place in the doorway he could decently put them. Something long came down the line, and he could hear the shape of it and none of the words.

"The house," Ellen said.

Then a short one.

"Not the rest."

Then she didn't answer, for as long as it took him to set a pin in the new plug and reach for the next one, and her light didn't move off his hands.

"I swear," she said. "Go to bed, Kate. Sunday at seven." She ended it there and put the phone away.

She had told her daughter he was the locksmith, which he was.

He seated the new cylinder and worked the new key twice, and it went over smooth both times. Then he counted the new keys into her palm, two for the front and two for the back, and she closed her hand on them.

"That's every one there is," he said.

Then he took out his key ring and found the orange tag, gone soft at the corners, and slid the old key into her new lock while she watched. It went in all the way and turned nothing. He worked it out again and let it drop among the others on the ring.

He stood with the bag in his hand.

"Offer stands till they sit," he said. "After that, I'll see you March first."

\* \* \*

Her text came at four, while he was cutting a house key for the Pelletier girl, and he read it once standing at the machine. *Lane seven. Ten o'clock. The back door is open.* Three periods, and not a question mark anywhere in it.

At ten to ten he came along Kettle Road with the heater ticking, and the sign came up out of the dark on its pole with the first letter out. The K had gone out the winter Danny got his license, and nobody fixed it, because Arlene said the sign still said what it meant. He turned in under the dead K. Her car wasn't in the lot.

The back door gave when he pulled it. She had propped it shut on a folded square of cardboard so the latch couldn't catch, and he saw that for what it was: she'd left it that way so he could come in and fix seven without either of them having to ask. In the pit the one caged bulb was on over the seven-and-eight pair, and the spare clutch had come down off its nail and lay on the workbench under it, with his old tag still wired to it and a strip of her tape stuck beside the tag, LANE SEVEN in black marker. A flashlight lay on the bench, switched on and aimed at seven's drive. Under it was an envelope with *R. Gagnon* on it in the same marker.

He worked. The first bolt on the old clutch had been on since before Danny was born and didn't want to come, and he leaned on it, and on the third try it gave its first quarter-turn, and he felt it in his back teeth. He stood a second with his hand on the wrench and enjoyed it. He swapped the clutch bolt by bolt in the light she'd aimed for him. Then he ran her from the pit. Seven set clean, and the sweep came down and lifted with no knock under it.

He ran her twice more and let her stop, and the motors ticked down, and he stood on the catwalk in the warm that came off them. Through the gap under the masking the lanes ran backward and bright under the lights she had left on, away from him to a counter with nobody at it. He listened for a voice that carried to lane ten without being raised, that had said *cleared to bowl* to forty-some people last night and had them listening by the last word, and the building gave him the boiler running down cellar and a ticking motor and nothing else. He had been in here alone a hundred nights and never once listened for anybody.

He left the envelope on the bench unopened. He set the old clutch on top of it so it wouldn't blow off, and switched off her flashlight, and went out the back.

The cardboard dropped to the step when he pulled the door to, and he heard the lock he had pinned that morning take, and he was on the outside of it with his hand on the cold handle.

\* \* \*

The credit union's form lay on the counter under Ray's left hand, with Danny's signature on the second page, since it was Danny's name on the deed beside his. Concord would lend him what the appraiser said, through the end of the year, and the credit union would lend the rest against the store if its committee said so at five. His father had put the store up once, in 'seventy-four, and near lost it. Danny was at the far register ringing up rock salt and hadn't said a word about it since he signed. Ray squared the form to the edge of the counter and looked at the door.

The guard on the key cutter had a screw that had worked loose sometime in the fall, and he took a screwdriver out of the cup and went after it. "You've been backing out on me since Thanksgiving," he told it, "and I've let you." He gave it another eighth of a turn.

The Bench was on its stools. Normie had bowled with Lucien that winter and knew what it had nearly cost, and he hadn't called a thing all afternoon.

The clock over the key cutter moved in small jumps, the minute hand sitting and sitting and then going all at once. It said two minutes to five when the bell on the door went.

Ellen came in, and the Bench stopped calling anything at all. The coffee pot ticked on its plate. Somebody set a cup down beside it with great care, and at the far register Danny held a bag of rock salt in the air and left it there.

She came down the center aisle and stopped across the counter from him. She took her right glove off to get at her vest pocket, and he watched her hand come out of it. She laid one new key on his counter, no tag, no ring, no word. It lay between his hand and Danny's signature, bright from his own cutter, and he knew the cut without picking it up: one of the two for the front door.

She looked up at the clock over the key cutter, and so did he.

The minute hand sat on the last line before the twelve. Ray kept his hand flat on the form. People had been setting things down on this counter for him since he was tall enough to see over it, money and returns and complaints and every broken thing in Ambrose, and he could not think of one that anybody had put there without his asking since Linda. A front-door key, from the woman who'd had the locks changed against him yesterday morning: she was handing him the way into her building without asking him in, and without selling it to him either. That was her answer to the offer, he knew that much. He didn't know yet which answer. He could ask her now. The offer stood until the committee sat, and there was a minute left. He could say it again, and she would have to answer him out loud in front of the Bench. He had sworn after that winter, at his father's kitchen table, that he would never ask the store twice, and he had asked it once, yesterday, on one knee on her mat, and so he wouldn't.

He watched her watch the clock. Her face was as calm as any he'd ever seen across this counter, and her breathing was all wrong for it, short and held and let go and held again, the breath of somebody keeping a count to herself. Her bare hand lay on the counter an inch from the key. The minute hand sat, and sat. Down the counter Danny lowered the bag of rock salt at last, and the minute hand jumped and stood on the twelve.

Ellen turned and went back down the aisle without a word, and the bell on the door rang behind her.

"That's the bell," said Normie.
