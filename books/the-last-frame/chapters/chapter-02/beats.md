## Tournament lead instructions

All three variants write the **same beats, facts, lines, scene order, ending types and word targets** set out below. They differ only in what each one leads with, meaning where the words and the attention go. Do not add or drop a beat to suit a lead.

**V1: Lead with the couple.** Every paragraph points at the other person. The longing beats get the most room in their scenes: the flashlight in S2 is allowed to run longest, S3 belongs to her voice coming up sixty feet of maple to the pit, and S4 belongs to her breathing. Stay in Ray's POV, but read Ellen hard from the outside: her hands, where she looks, what her voice does and what she doesn't say. The cold reader got nothing from her side last time, so this variant gives it through what he sees. Each business line (the offer, the bank's number, the envelope) is said looking at the other person, and Ray's eyes go back to her after every one. The Bench, Danny and Kate get their assigned lines and nothing more. The humor is kept, but it is quiet, said between two people rather than played to a room.

**V2: Lead with pace and the clock.** Enter every scene late and leave on the assigned last beat with nothing after it: S1 opens on the recording, S2 with the cylinder half out, S3 with the lock turning from her side, and S4 at two minutes to five, with the form and Danny's signature folded into the minute. Every scene carries the time and the deadline (half past seven, eight, ten, two minutes to five), so the chapter reads as a countdown to Ray's five o'clock, and the reader has it in their body before S4. Dialogue carries the weight. Interior shrinks to one clause per beat everywhere except the minute, which is the one place the chapter slows, and it slows hard. Cut all logistics (the drive, packing the bag, the text arriving). Keep every joke. Write to the low end of the S1 to S3 targets and spend what's saved on S4.

**V3: Lead with Ray's voice and humor.** Let the way Ray talks and thinks carry the chapter: *and*-chains with the weight on the last word, machines as *she* with tempers, time dated by what broke, rounded numbers, mock-solemnity about his own habits, and teasing aimed at her exactness and her bowling. One "fella I knew" story is allowed, in S1 or S3, under 60 words and ending on a turn (book cap 3; this is the first). Three interior passages run longer than the dialogue around them: S2 after the pins, S3 while her voice comes down the lane, and S4 in the minute. The comedy carries the tension right up to the minute and then stops dead. Ray makes no joke and no wry thought anywhere in S4, so the drop lands. Avoid aphorisms: his humor lands on the last word of a long, ordinary sentence and never in a neat saying.

### Scene 1

**Setting:** Gagnon & Son Hardware, bottom of Main Street. Wednesday, December 2, half past seven, cold and clear; the store has been open since six. Sensory anchor: the bell on the door, and the cold coming in with her and lifting the smell of birdseed and cut brass off the aisles.

**Characters:** Ray, Ellen, Normie and the two other men of the Bench, Danny (one action, no line).

**Want:** Ellen wants the name of lane seven's part and Ray's rate, so she can hire it fixed before Thursday's league without owing him. The reader learns it at "I'd like the name of the part, and your rate." Ray wants her to stay at his counter, and he wants to be *asked*: he'll give the name for nothing and won't be hired. In the way: each refuses the other's terms in front of the Bench.

**Surface action:**
1. Ray behind the counter, counting a drawer Danny has already counted, twenties first (one clause; it's his want apart from her, and needs no saying about it). The Bench introduced **before anyone speaks**, in one sentence: three retired men on three stools by the coffee pot, and Normie, on the end, who calls everything in the store like the Saturday show out of Boston.
2. The bell. Ellen comes in with the cold. At the counter she pulls a glove off **with her teeth**, lays her phone down and says nothing first. She plays the recording: a sound like a **chair dragged across an upstairs floor**, then the knock, and the same wait again.
3. Ray watches her hand on the phone instead of the phone. One clause of memory, no more than fifteen words: she finished the tape before she came down the ladder. No replay of the strip.
4. Normie, low, play-by-play (his own words; no real catchphrase).
5. Ray shuts his eyes and names it before it comes round twice: seven's clutch, slipping and catching, and the knock is the sweep coming down on her. "She's sulking, is all." There's a spare on a nail behind seven with his writing on the tag.
6. She puts on the reading glasses from her cord, takes a pen from the cup on his counter without asking, and writes his answer on the pad from her vest pocket. **Do not quote what she writes.** He loses his count of the twenties. (The six-years sentence is optional. If used, it comes *before* he watches.)
7. "I'd like the name of the part, and your rate." / "Name's a clutch. You wrote it down." / "Your rate." / "Haven't got one. Arlene never asked me for one." / "Then I'll find someone who has one." / Ray: go ahead and look, it's a short list. He starts the twenties over.
8. Normie: **"And the veteran declines."**
9. Ellen turns her head along the three stools and answers the Bench literally, deadpan, in a way that takes the joke off them. **Not "Noted."** The dialogue doctor owns the line. The Bench takes it in one beat of silence.
10. At the door, with the bell already going over her head: **"Eight o'clock, Mr. Gagnon. I'll hold the light."** Gone before he can answer.

**Subtext:** She has come to the one man she won't ask, to get his answer without asking, and booked herself to kneel beside him in public. He wanted to be asked and got offered money instead.

**Tension source:** two refusals in front of an audience that is keeping score.

**Texture budget:** (1) **Diagnosis by sound**, with his eyes shut, from a phone speaker. It is the one thing in town she can't get without him.

**Point of view:** Ray.

**Ends on:** **Reversal.** She takes his Ch 01 gibe ("he'll want somebody to hold the light") and books herself into it. Last beat: her line and the bell, and nothing from Ray after it.

**Word target:** 600

### Scene 2

**Setting:** Kettle Lanes, the front door, Ray kneeling on the vestibule mat. Wednesday, December 2, about eight. The door stands open to the lot, the wind off the lake finds the gap at his collar, and the warm fryer air from inside meets it at the sill. Optional, one line: a cup of black coffee set where he'd see it.

**Characters:** Ray, Ellen, Kate (by phone; only Ellen's half is heard).

**Want:** Ray wants her to sell to him **now**, at what she paid, before his committee sits tomorrow at five, so the building comes to him clean and nobody's mistake is bought cheap. The reader learns it at **"Now I'm asking you."** Ellen wants every key to her building in her own hand, cut by the man who held the old one, and she wants to keep the building. The reader learns the first when she has his flashlight on before he reaches for it. In the way: he will only ask once, and she has sworn the rest of what she has to her daughter.

**Surface action:**
1. Open in motion: the cylinder is half out. He reaches into the bag for the flashlight, and it is already on, in her hand, aimed at his work. (The back door is already done: one clause, or leave it out.)
2. **The longing beat, given room, with no joke.** She holds the light steady, which most people can't, and close, which most people won't. Her breath is on the back of his wrist. His hands stop, and he lets them stay stopped, and she doesn't move the light or say a word. When he goes on, she moves the light ahead of his hands, to where they're going. Write it in three to five sentences and add no gloss after.
3. He pushes the plug out and the old pins come into his palm, small and brass and worn bright at the tips. The trash is two feet from his knee. He tips them into his shirt pocket and buttons the flap.
4. **The money, once, in one plain exchange that is a move between them.**
   - He says the start of the offer **to the cylinder**, in one sentence. She: **"You've said that to the lock."** Ray: **"Lock's the one I'm working on."**
   - Then he sits back on his heels and says it **to her face**, in his own words and in this order:
     - *Arlene's doing, said so it costs him:* Arlene had the place listed two years, and the only other one up the drive was a fella from Concord who wanted the lot at land value, for condos. So she sold it to you over me, and then she came down to the store and asked me to sign behind you in case you fell, and I signed because she asked. (This costs Ray: he says to the winner's face that he was passed over and kept as the net. It costs Ellen too: Arlene hedged her. Do not say why Arlene chose her.)
     - **"Now I'm asking you."** Sell it to me now, at what you paid, every dollar back, the house and all.
     - What she paid is more than it'll appraise, and the only way he gets there is to put the store up behind it. His father put the store up once, in **'seventy-four**, and Ray watched it nearly go. Give one clause of what the boy saw, physical (for example, his father sitting in the truck in the dooryard with the engine off). No more.
     - The credit union sits **tomorrow at five**. **"I'm asking it once."**
   - Nothing else about the contract. No lender. No terms.
5. Ellen does not answer the offer. She answers with the bank: **"Granite Lakes writes me the loan the day this winter clears thirty thousand, with every dollar it makes still sitting in the account."** Ray reads it back in his words, so the rule is held: "So it's got to come in, and then it's got to stay put." / **"Every dollar."**
6. Ray makes the number felt: **"Nobody's cleared thirty in this building since Arlene's Normand,"** dated his way (he's been gone since before Danny was born). Always "Arlene's Normand," never bare "Normand," so a listener doesn't hear "Normie." Ellen: **"Then it's been done."**
7. **Her want, in one line that is hers:** **"I bought it to hear all ten going at once."** Beat. **"Last night I heard nine."** (Lane seven, and his "Not my alley.") Ray has no answer. His hands go back to the plug. One physical beat shows he doesn't take it as a reason to spend a house (it hands off to Ch 03), and nothing interprets it.
8. **Kate.** Her phone goes in her vest. She takes it left-handed and keeps the light where it is, because she doesn't leave a position mid-job, so he hears every word. "Kate. I'm at the alley with the locksmith." / something long comes down the line / **"The house."** / something short / **"Not the rest."** / a pause as long as it takes him to set a pin / **"I swear."** / "Go to bed, Kate. Sunday at seven." Ray keeps his eyes on the pin the whole time. **No interior gloss after the call.**
9. He seats the new cylinder and works the new key twice; it goes over smooth both times. He counts the new keys into her palm, two for the front and two for the back: "That's every one there is." She closes her hand on them.
10. Ray, standing, bag in hand: **"Then I'll see you March first."** Ellen: **"You'll have your answer before your committee sits."** This is her promise, and it is why she comes at two minutes to five.
11. In front of her, he takes out his ring, finds the soft orange tag and slides his old key into her new lock. It goes in all the way and turns nothing. He works it out and lets it drop back among the others.

**Subtext:** He is offering her house back to a woman who has just sworn the rest of what she has to her daughter, in front of him. She won't take it, and she won't say no either, because a no would end him at her door, and she wants him at her door.

**Tension source:** one ask, which costs him a line at a time (passed over, kept as the net, his father's store), against her oath, which he hears her make.

**Texture budget:** (1) **The old pins into his shirt pocket**: a man who keeps things. (2) **The dead key in the new lock**: the cost of his own work, done in front of her. Use no locksmith vocabulary beyond "cylinder," "plug" and "pins," and no licensing line.

**Point of view:** Ray.

**Ends on:** **Consequence.** His own key, cut out of her door by his own hands, turns nothing. He puts it back on the ring. This is the chapter's one prop ending (1 of the 2 allowed). Her promise is the last spoken line, and the dead key is the last beat.

**Word target:** 950. Do not compress step 2 (the light), steps 4 and 5 (the ask and the bank) or step 8 (the oath).

### Scene 3

**Setting:** Kettle Lanes after closing. Wednesday, December 2, ten o'clock, hard cold and clear. The back door first, then the pit behind lane seven, with seven's approach sixty feet up the lane. Sensory anchor: one caged bulb on over the seven-and-eight pair, the lane lights on over seven only, and the warmth that comes off the motors when he runs her.

**Characters:** Ray, Ellen.

**Want:** Ray wants seven running before tomorrow's league (he has never left a machine down on a league night except Tuesday's), and under that he wants her to stay in the building while he works. The reader learns the second when he calls **"Again"** for a test ball he doesn't need, because he heard the machine set clean the first time. Ellen wants the job done and paid at the going rate, so she owes him nothing, and she wants to watch him do the thing she can't. The reader learns the first when she says what's in the envelope.

**Surface action:**
1. One or two sentences only: her text, read once at the store at four, **"Lane seven. Ten o'clock. I will let you in at the back."** Three periods and no question mark ("periods," never "full stops"). He goes because tomorrow is league night (one clause). The S3 opening may run straight from the text to the door.
2. Optional, one sentence: Kettle Road, the sign with the first letter dark. The K went out the winter Danny got his license, and nobody fixed it, because Arlene said the sign still said what it meant. "Dead K" at most once.
3. **The honest way in.** He knocks at the back. The lock he pinned that morning turns from the inside with her key, and she's in the doorway in her vest. They plant the problem the key will answer: Ray, something close to "You'll have to come down and let me in every time one of them acts up." / Ellen, a literal reply (for example, "Then they had better not act up."). The doctor owns the lines. Avoid "sulk" (it was used in S1).
4. The pit. The spare clutch has already come down off its nail and lies on the bench under the caged bulb, with his tag still wired on and her strip of tape beside it reading LANE SEVEN in marker. Beside it is an envelope marked *R. Gagnon*. He leaves the envelope for now.
5. She doesn't stay in the pit. She goes back out and up to seven's approach. **They talk the length of the lane.** She doesn't raise her voice, and it comes down sixty feet of maple to him whole. He has to raise his. (This is the longing beat. Do not recap the Ch 01 PA.) He works: give one or two sentences of hands, and the seized bolt's first quarter-turn giving, which he feels in his back teeth. That is the one ordinary pleasure, given room for two sentences and enjoyed without comment.
6. "Throw one." Her ball, badly: one comic image, and not into another lane (Ch 01 owns that). Seven sweeps and sets clean, with no knock under it. Ray calls **"Again."** She throws again.
7. She comes back through the steel door into the pit. He opens the envelope in front of her: folded bills inside a page from her pad, in her printing, giving a pinsetter man in Worcester, his rate by the hour, his hours and the part, totaled and underlined twice. **No figures on the page.** She says what it is, plainly: she found someone who has a rate, and that is it.
8. **The laugh**, Ray at her bowling, landing on the last word and tied to what he's just read (for example, the machine's fixed, and as for the bowler, he'd send her to Worcester). Ellen answers it literally. The doctor owns the exchange.
9. **The move.** He takes the page out and folds it into his shirt pocket with the old pins, and holds the envelope out to her with the money still in it: "I'll keep the paperwork." She tells him that isn't how an invoice works (avoid any "X, not Y" form). He tells her it's how this one works. She takes the envelope back and stands there holding it, owing him, which she can't stand, and she doesn't put it down.
10. At the back door, going out. Ellen: **"Your committee sits at five tomorrow."** Ray: something close to "You'll know where to find me." Ellen: **"At your counter. Under the clock."** She has looked at his store (S1), and he hears that she has.

**Subtext:** She paid so she wouldn't owe him, and he kept her handwriting and gave back her money so she would. She lets him in by hand, as owners do, and they both see that she'll have to do it every time. Her last line tells him she has been watching him as closely as he has watched her.

**Tension source:** the envelope (who owes whom), and the deadline she raises herself as he leaves.

**Texture budget:** (1) **The clutch swap and the bolt giving**, one or two sentences of hands. That is all the trade in the scene.

**Point of view:** Ray.

**Ends on:** **Dialogue.** "At your counter. Under the clock." It points the reader at S4's clock and leaves the question open: what will she do at five?

**Word target:** 650

### Scene 4

**Setting:** Gagnon & Son Hardware. Thursday, December 3, from about ten to five to five o'clock. It has been dark since a little after four, and the store lights are on. Sensory anchor: the clock over the key-cutting machine at the end of the counter, its minute hand sitting and sitting and then jumping.

**Characters:** Ray, Ellen, Normie and the Bench, Danny (silent).

**Want:** Ray wants her through the door before five. He'd tell himself he wants the yes; what he wants is her at the counter. The reader learns it when, with the loan officer's slip by the phone, he looks at the door and not the phone. Ellen wants to keep her word (an answer before the committee sits), to give him the way into her building without giving him the building, and to ask him for nothing. The reader learns it when she lays the key down and looks at the clock, not at him.

**Surface action:**
1. **The deadline, made physical, in one short paragraph.** The credit union's form lies flat under Ray's left hand, and the loan officer's number is on a slip by the phone: one call before five and the committee takes it up tonight. Danny's signature is on the form. Ray never asked him; Danny signed it anyway, put it in the drawer and never said a word. Danny is at the far register. Give it no mechanism beyond this.
2. Fear register: Ray tightens the loose guard screw on the key cutter **in silence**. The Bench is on its stools, and Normie hasn't called a thing all afternoon. (Optional, one clause: Normie bowled with Lucien that winter.)
3. **Two minutes to five.** The bell. Ellen. **The Bench goes quiet.** Write the quiet: the coffee pot ticking on its plate, somebody setting a cup down with care, Danny holding a bag of rock salt in the air and leaving it there.
4. She comes down the center aisle to the counter. She takes her glove off to get at her vest pocket (bare hand, the second of two). **One new key, no tag, no ring, no word.** It lies between his hand and Danny's name. He knows the cut without picking it up: her front door, cut on his own machine yesterday.
5. **The minute, in real time, at least 250 words from the key touching the counter to the hand jumping to twelve.** Both of them look up at the clock. Ray's interior, plain and in his own terms, with **no joke** and no wry thought:
   - **What the key opens:** her front door, at any hour, without knocking, and without her having to come down and let him in. That is the thing they stood at her back door about last night, and she has answered it without asking him for anything.
   - **What it doesn't:** the building. She is letting his one ask run out on his own clock, in his own store, in front of him. That is her answer, the one she promised before the committee sat, and it is a no to selling.
   - **What he can't read:** whether it says anything past the door. Leave it open (handoff to Ch 03).
   - The most anyone has put on this counter without his asking, since Linda.
   - He could ask her again. The phone and the slip are right there, and there is a minute left. He told her he'd ask once, and he won't ask the store twice. Hold it on his hand flat on the form.
   - He watches her watch the clock. Her face is as calm as any he's seen across this counter, and her breathing is wrong for it: short, held, let go, held again. He hears it the way he hears a machine that's about to go. Her bare hand lies an inch from the key.
   - The sentences get longer and slower here. This is the chapter's high point.
6. The minute hand jumps to the twelve. Danny lowers the rock salt. Ellen turns and goes back down the aisle without a word, and the bell rings behind her.
7. Normie: **"That's the bell."** Cut. Nothing after it: no reaction beat, no hand reaching for the key.

**Subtext:** She has refused him and given him a key in the same gesture. He has kept his word to his father and lost the building he wanted in the same minute. The Bench has watched both, and neither of them said a word.

**Tension source:** the clock, the phone a foot from his hand, and two people who will not ask.

**Texture budget:** (1) **The credit union form with Danny's signature**, given one look, to show what the store would have carried. Nothing else technical; the screw is an action, not texture.

**Point of view:** Ray.

**Ends on:** **Cut.** At five the bell rings as she goes, and Normie says "That's the bell." End of chapter and of Act 1. The chapter promise is left open: *What will he do with a key to the building he's contracted to buy?*

**Word target:** 600, with at least 250 of it in the minute.
