# Chapter 2: Five O'Clock

The store had been open since six, and Ray was behind the counter counting a drawer Danny had already counted, twenties first. On three stools by the coffee pot sat the Bench, three retired men who came for the free coffee, which was terrible, and stayed because something in a hardware store was always about to happen, and the one on the end, Normie, called whatever it was like the Saturday show out of Boston.

At half past seven the bell went, and the cold came in with Ellen Daly and lifted the smell of birdseed and cut brass off the aisles. She came down the center aisle without a glance at a shelf, pulled her right glove off with her teeth, laid her phone on his mat, and said nothing. She touched the screen.

Out of the little speaker came a sound like a chair dragged across an upstairs floor, then the knock, then the same wait, and the chair again.

Ray watched her hand. It was square and plain and red across the knuckles from the cold, and it lay flat beside the phone and waited. Last night she had finished her strip of tape before she came down the ladder.

"And here's the new owner up the middle, boys," Normie said into his cup, "and she's brought him something."

Ray listened. He had it before it came round a second time. That was seven's clutch letting go and catching and letting go, and the knock was the sweep coming down on the machine before she was ready. Seven had done that since the winter the Legion broke the ball return on four.

"Seven's clutch," he said. "She's slipping and catching, and the sweep comes down on her late, and that's your knock. Sulking, is all. There's a spare on a nail behind seven with my writing on the tag."

She put on the reading glasses from the cord at her neck, took a pen from the cup on his counter without asking, and drew a small pad from her vest pocket. He had not wanted to watch anybody do anything in six years. He watched her write his answer down, the pen stopping hard at the end of each line, and he lost his count of the twenties. Her eyes went once past his shoulder to the clock over the key cutter, and came back.

"I'd like the name of the part," she said, "and your rate."

"Name's a clutch. You wrote it down."

"Your rate."

"Haven't got one. Arlene never asked me for one."

"Then I'll find someone who has one."

"Go ahead and look. It's a short list. Fella I knew in Tilton had an alley over there and wouldn't call me on principle, and drove all over three states looking for a pinsetter man, and found one, too, down in Lowell, eighty-some years old, and the old man listened to the machine a minute and asked him for my number."

She heard him out and wrote none of it down. He started the twenties over.

"And the veteran declines," Normie said.

She turned her head and went along the three stools, one man at a time.

"He's the youngest of the four of you," she said.

Nobody on the Bench said a word. Down at the far register Danny bent over a carton of sash cord and kept his face in it.

She put the pad in her vest and the glove back on and went up the aisle, and the bell was already going over her head when she turned in the open door.

"Eight o'clock, Mr. Gagnon. I'll hold the light."

She was gone before the bell stopped.

\* \* \*

The cylinder was half out of her front door, and Ray was on his knees on the vestibule mat with the door open to the lot. The wind off the lake found the gap at the back of his collar, and the building's heat met it at the sill, so he was cold behind and warm in the face. A cup of black coffee sat on the sill where he would see it, and it was hot, which was more than the Bench's ever managed. He reached into the bag for the flashlight.

It was already on, in her hand, aimed at his work.

She held the light steady, which most people can't, and close, which most people won't, down beside him with her shoulder an inch off his, and her breath on the back of his wrist came warm and even. His hands stopped. He let them stay stopped, and she didn't move the light or say a word, and the only sound in the vestibule was the door glass ticking in its frame. When he went on, the light went ahead of his hands to the place they were going next, and was waiting there when they arrived.

He pushed the plug out, and the old pins came into his palm, small and brass and worn bright at the tips by every key that had gone in and out of that door since before he could drive. The trash can was two feet from his knee. He tipped them into his shirt pocket and buttoned the flap.

He had a coffee can down cellar at home with his father's pins and his own in it, and springs and the insides of locks off houses that had been sold or torn down, and Linda used to call it his retirement. On the drive out Kettle Road he had been over what he meant to say twice, and both times it had come out fine, the way things do in a truck when nobody is holding a light on you. And here he was on his knees on her mat with her old pins in his pocket, and the only thing in front of him that wasn't looking at him was the lock.

So he told the lock. "Thing is, I'd buy this door off her, and the frame, and the building it's hung on, if she'd sell it to me."

"You've said that to the lock."

"Lock's the one I'm working on."

The light didn't move. He sat back on his heels and looked up past it at her.

"Arlene had this place listed two years," he said, "and the only other one who came up the drive was a fella from Concord who wanted the lot at land value, for condos. So she sold it to you over me. Then she came down to the store and asked me to sign behind you, in case you fell, and I signed, because she asked." He let that sit. "Now I'm asking you. Sell it to me now, every dollar back, closing costs and all. That's more than it'll appraise at, so the store goes up behind it. My father put it up once, in 'seventy-four, and I watched it nearly go, him out in the truck in the dooryard after dark with the engine off. I'll do it anyway, because I'd sooner have this place clean, with you saying yes, than cheap off your mistake. The credit union sits tomorrow at five. I'm asking it once."

She didn't answer it. She kept the light on the open lock.

"Granite Lakes writes me the loan the day this winter clears thirty thousand, with every dollar it makes still sitting in the account."

"So it's got to come in," Ray said, "and then it's got to stay put."

"Every dollar."

"Nobody's cleared thirty in this building since Arlene's Normand. It's in her ledgers, if you want to check my arithmetic."

"Then it's been done."

He waited for the rest of it.

"I bought it to hear all ten going at once." The light stayed where it was. "Last night I heard nine."

He put his hands back on the plug, took the next pin from the tray, and set it.

Her phone went in her vest. She took it out left-handed and kept the light where it was, on his work.

"Kate. I'm at the alley with the locksmith."

Something long came down the line, a young woman's voice with no room for breathing in it, and he didn't try to make out a word.

"The house," she said.

Something short.

"Not the rest."

The line went quiet for as long as it took him to set a pin and seat it.

"I swear."

Another while. "Go to bed, Kate. Sunday at seven."

Ray kept his eyes on the pin the whole time.

He seated the cylinder and ran the new key in and turned it, and it went over smooth, and he did it again, and it went over smooth again. He counted the new keys into her palm, two for the front and two for the back.

"That's every one there is."

She closed her hand on them, opened it, and counted with one finger. "Four."

"Could have told you four."

"You did. Now I have it twice."

He got up off the mat, and the right knee clicked twice. He picked up the bag. "Then I'll see you March first."

"You'll have your answer before your committee sits."

He took out his ring and found the soft orange tag by feel, and in front of her he slid his old key into her new lock. It went in all the way and turned nothing. He worked it back out and let it drop among the others.

\* \* \*

Her text had come at four, while he was cutting a house key for the Pelletier girl: *Lane seven. Ten o'clock. I will let you in at the back.* Three periods and no question mark. He was in bed by half past nine most nights, and he went anyway, because tomorrow was league night. The K on the sign out at the road had gone dark the winter Danny got his license, and nobody fixed it, because Arlene said the sign still said what it meant.

He knocked at the back. The lock he had pinned that morning turned over from the inside, and she was in the doorway.

"You'll be coming down to let me in every time one of the machines gets a notion, then."

"Then they had better not get notions."

The pit was dark but for one caged bulb over the seven-and-eight pair, and on the bench under it lay the spare clutch, already down off its nail. His tag was still wired to it with *seven* on it in his own hand, and beside the tag she had laid a strip of tape that said LANE SEVEN in black marker, in case the part had any doubts. Next to it was an envelope marked *R. Gagnon*. He left that where it was.

She didn't stay. She went out through the steel door, and the lights came on over seven, and she stood on seven's approach, small and lit at the far end of the lane.

"What's it doing?" she said.

"Thinking it over," he called.

"For how long?"

"Depends who's asking."

She hadn't raised her voice. It came down sixty feet of maple and under the masking and got to him whole, every word where she'd put it, and he had to holler to send anything back, and he hollered. A good many voices had come down that lane at him, Linda's among them, and none of them had come this low or got here in one piece.

"It's eleven minutes past ten."

"Seven can hear you, you know, and she'll go slower out of spite."

"Then I'll tell it the time as well."

The bolt on the old clutch had been in since the winter nine's belt let go the first time, and he set the wrench on it and leaned.

"You still up there?"

"You can see me."

"Wanted to hear how you'd put it."

"I'm on the approach to lane seven."

The bolt gave its first quarter-turn. He felt that in his back teeth, and he stayed on his heels a second and let himself have it.

He had the new clutch on and the belt back over it, and he ran her, and the warmth came off the motor into his face.

"Throw a box," he called.

Her first ball came down slow enough that he could have read the maker's name on it. Two-thirds of the way down it wobbled, like it had changed its mind, then went on and took the ten pin out of the far corner and nothing else. After her third the sweep came down on time and seven set clean, with no knock under it, and it sounded right to him.

"Again," he called.

She threw another box. It was worse.

She came back through the steel door, and he opened the envelope in front of her. Folded bills were inside a page off her pad, and on it, in her printing, were a pinsetter man in Worcester, his rate by the hour, his hours, the part, and a total underlined twice.

"I found someone who has a rate," she said. "That is what he would have charged."

"Machine's fixed, then," Ray said. "And as for the bowler, I'd send her down to Worcester."

"I asked him. He only does machines."

He took the page out, folded it twice, and buttoned it into his shirt pocket with the old pins. He held the envelope out to her with the money still in it. "I'll keep the paperwork."

"An invoice goes with the money, Mr. Gagnon. That is the entire point of one."

"That's how this one works."

She took the envelope back and stood holding it in both hands, owing him, which sat on her about as well as a wet coat, and she didn't put it down.

At the back door she said, "Your committee sits at five tomorrow."

"You'll know where to find me."

"At your counter," she said. "Under the clock."

\* \* \*

The credit union's form lay flat under Ray's left hand, and the loan officer's number was on a slip by the phone: one call before five, and the committee would take it up tonight. Danny's signature was on the last line. Ray had never asked him for it. Danny had signed it anyway, put it in the drawer under the register, and never said a word.

The guard on the key cutter had a loose screw. Ray took the driver from the cup and gave it a quarter-turn, and another, and it had been tight after the first, and he gave it a third. Normie hadn't called a thing all afternoon. He sat on the end stool with his cup on his knee and watched the door.

Over the key cutter the clock's minute hand sat on its mark, and sat there, and jumped.

At two minutes to five the bell went over the door.

The Bench went quiet. The coffee pot ticked on its plate. Somebody set a cup down with care. Down at the far register Danny had a bag of rock salt halfway to the shelf, and he held it there in the air and left it there.

She came down the center aisle and stopped across the counter from him. She took her right glove off to get at her vest pocket, and her hand came out with one key in it, new, with no tag and no ring, and she laid it on the counter between his hand and Danny's name and said nothing. He knew the cut without picking it up. It was her front door, and he had cut it on his own machine yesterday morning.

She looked up at the clock, and he looked up with her.

The minute hand stood on the fifty-nine with a minute still to run, and under the store lights the key lay bright as a new washer, the brass still raw from his wheel, an inch from Danny's name on the last line of the form. He did not pick it up. He read the loan officer's number on the slip beside the phone, all ten digits, and read them again. The phone sat a foot from his right hand, and his right hand stayed on the edge of the counter. Under his left the corner of the form had gone damp and begun to curl against his palm, and he pressed it flat.

He tried to keep the seconds the way he kept the twenties, in tens, and lost it somewhere past forty and began again, and lost it again. The coffee pot ticked on its plate. Normie's cup stayed on his knee. Over by the register the plastic of the rock salt bag gave one small crackle in Danny's arms and went still.

He could ask her again. Yesterday on her mat he had told her he would ask once. He had sworn, the winter his father sat out in the truck, to put the store up once in his life and never twice. His left hand stayed flat on the form.

Ray watched her watch the clock. Her face was as calm as any face he had seen across this counter, and her breathing was wrong for it. It came short, and held, and let go, and held again, and he heard it the way he heard a pinsetter from the lot when a clutch was about to go, one small wrong beat under a sound that was otherwise fine. Her bare hand lay on the counter an inch from the key. It did not move, and neither did his.

The minute hand jumped to the twelve.

Danny let the rock salt down onto the shelf. She turned and went back down the aisle without a word, and the bell rang behind her.

"That's the bell," Normie said.
