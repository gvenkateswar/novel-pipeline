# Reader Notes: Chapter 02, "Right of First Refusal"

I read chapter 1 for context. These notes are about chapter 2 only.

## Where I got bored

- **The rekey paragraph.** "Cylinder out, plug pushed through onto the follower so the springs stayed in their chambers, old pins tipped into the tray. New pins in from Danny's twists, one chamber at a time, until the new key lifted every stack flush with the plug." This is locksmith procedure and I skimmed it. I believe Ray knows his job. I didn't need to watch all of it. Then the Corbin paragraph adds more of the same. My attention came back only when he knelt at the front door and could see the trophy case.
- **The Tilton furnace story.** "Fella I knew in Tilton..." I saw the point coming by the second sentence. It's a parable with the moral sticking out. Danny calling it "a story. About pipes" saved it a little, but I still had to read the whole parable before the joke landed.
- **The middle third is quiet.** From the rekey through the 9 p.m. repair, it's mostly Ray alone with tools and his thoughts. I picked this up because the book was billed as a romantic comedy, and this stretch is a sad, slow procedural. Chapter 1 had the "Roger, tower" heckling and the ball jumping into Ray's lane. Chapter 2's only real laughs come from Normie.

## Where I got confused

- **"The Bench."** It's capitalized as if I should already know it. I worked out that it means the men on stools by the coffee pot. Only Normie has a name. "The one beside him," "the second man," and "the third" are interchangeable.
- **"He still knew Linda's average, too."** It's dropped into the middle of the paragraph about the bank letter. Chapter 1 showed me a memorial trophy, so I'm guessing Linda is his dead wife, but nobody has said so. The sentence interrupts the money thought without explaining anything.
- **The right of first refusal.** The chapter is named after it and I still don't understand how it works. Ray had a bank loan approved eight days after Arlene shook Ellen's hand. So did he miss his chance? Did Arlene skip him? Chapter 1 says that if Ellen misses the note payment, Ray "gets first crack." I can't tell whether Ray feels cheated, was too late, or never asked. That matters for how I'm supposed to feel about him.
- **"Scale of one to ten, I'm a three."** A three of what? Worried? Tempted to sell? Angry? I read it twice and still don't know what Kate asked.
- **Whose head am I in?** The chapter switches to Ray's point of view with no signal. That was clear enough within a page, but my first reaction was "wait, is Ellen gone?" She's mostly offstage in a chapter I thought would be about her fighting to keep the place.

## Where I stopped believing

- **Ellen's "Unable" speech** retells what chapter 1 already gave me almost word for word: thirty-one years, she didn't choose when to stop, the first thing she has chosen. Chapter 1's version was interior and it moved me. Said out loud to Ray, it sounds like a statement she prepared, and the narration covers for it ("It made each word sound like it had been checked"). Twice in two chapters makes it feel like a thesis.
- **Ray deleting his text drafts.** He types two versions, deletes both, and sends the third. It's a stock rom-com move, and it doesn't fit a man who writes a 7 so hard it goes through the paper and won't buy a five-dollar square. I don't see him agonizing over phrasing.
- **The salt echo.** The plow driver buys two bags of salt, and then Ellen walks in and buys two bags of salt. It reads as staged, not funny.
- Mostly, though, I believed these people. Ellen reading the safety instructions back and handing Ray the index card before he asked is exactly who she was in chapter 1.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **Paragraphs that end on a wise little image, one after another:**
  - "the way a man keeps a part for a machine that's already been sold."
  - "You did that with a good tool sometimes, left the edge alone because it was the last edge somebody else had put on it."
  - "She kept what still had a use and changed what she had a reason to change."
  - "He couldn't decide if that made the room better or worse."
  - "Arlene's key, which opened nothing."
  - "A man who meant to buy a building didn't put five dollars on the day it came up for sale."

  Any one of these is fine. Together they make Ray sound like a fortune-cookie version of a quiet man. The last one also spells out the pool-square subtext the chapter had already shown me with the pen in the cup. I'd figured it out, so the explanation took something away.
- **Everyone talks in the same clipped way.** Ray, Ellen, and Danny all speak in short declaratives with no wasted words. Danny's "Cut them or I will" and "Take the good flashlight. Not yours." could be Ray's lines. Normie is the only one with a different rhythm. Denise isn't in this chapter, and I missed her, because she was the only loose voice in chapter 1.
- **Repeated stage business.** Ray touching the trophy glass with one knuckle (again), the key ring jingling against the orange tag (again), trying the door handle twice (Ellen did that in chapter 1, and now Ray does it). I see that these are meant to be echoes. Repeated this often, they feel mechanical.
- Normie's "folks," sports-announcer commentary is fun the first time and starts to feel like a device by the third.

## What I want to know next

- How Ellen will make any money at all. Chapter 1 ended with ninety days and a night in the red. Chapter 2 moved the romance thread and did nothing on the business. I want to see her actually try something.
- Is "INVOICE TO E. DALY" her insisting on paying him, or a joke at his expense? I read it as her refusing to owe him anything. I want to see whether he bills her.
- What happened with Linda, and why the alley is a shrine to her for Ray.
- Whether Ray will keep fixing machines to "save the league" while still waiting for March 1. That contradiction is the most interesting thing in the book so far.
- Kate. One phone call, and she's apparently against the whole idea. I'd like to see more of her.

The pull is real but mild. I'm curious about Ray. I'm worried the book will stay this quiet.

## Best moment

> "Ray put his pin tray in order. Nothing in it had been out of order."

It's tied with the exchange of cards. He prints his repair in her five columns, "07 / 2340 / MX / 1 / CLUTCH — SLIPPING. REPLACED." and ties it to the old part, and the next morning gets a photo back: "REMARKS: INVOICE TO E. DALY." That's the courtship happening in their own shared language, with no one saying anything. It was the best thing in the chapter and the first time it felt like a romance.

## Would I keep reading?

Yes, on the strength of the card exchange and Ray's contradictions. But if chapter 3 is this quiet again, with Ellen offstage and no comedy, I'd probably put the book down. This chapter read like literary fiction about grief, not the comedy the premise promised.
