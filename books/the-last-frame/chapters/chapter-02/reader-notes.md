# Reader Notes: Chapter 02, "Five O'Clock"

**Draft read:** final.md, tournament winner, Oct 28 evening. (I read chapter-01/final.md first. I read no other file.)

---

## 1. Pull score: 5 / 10

**I'd keep reading, but only for the two of them.** The banter is the best thing in the book so far. The plot of this chapter is that he asks her to sell and she says no, and Chapter 1 already told me she would say no ("I signed so Arlene gets paid. By me."). Four scenes go: diagnose the machine, rekey the door, fix the machine, wait out the clock. Nothing gets worse for Ellen. She ends the chapter better off than she started (machine fixed, door rekeyed, Ray turned down) and nothing new is threatening her. If I'd already been bored by two quiet books like this one, I'd give it one more chapter to put her in trouble.

---

## 2. Where I got bored

- **Scene 1, about 12% in.** "Fella I knew in Tilton had an alley over there and wouldn't call me on principle, and drove all over three states looking for a pinsetter man, and found one, too, down in Lowell, eighty-some years old..." This is a tall tale about two men I'll never meet, and all it says is "nobody else can do it." I stopped listening halfway through. Ellen doesn't write any of it down either, and I was on her side.
- **Scene 2, about 33%, Ray's long ask.** "So she sold it to you over me. Then she came down to the store and asked me to sign behind you, in case you fell, and I signed, because she asked." I already knew the contract from Chapter 1. Then it's appraisal, closing costs, the store going up as collateral, his father in 1974, the credit union sitting tomorrow at five. It's a loan application read out loud. The one thing that matters (he's risking his family store) is buried in a pile of terms.
- **Scene 3, about 62%.** "The bolt on the old clutch had been in since the winter nine's belt let go the first time, and he set the wrench on it and leaned." Repair procedure. Every time we leave the shouted lines between them and go down into the pit, my attention went down with it. Chapter 1 broke the lane seven machine and Chapter 2 spent a whole scene fixing it. I don't care about lane seven any more.
- **Scene 4, about 85–92%, the biggest drift.** After she puts the key down, the narrator spends five paragraphs explaining what the key means: "The key opened her front door... That was the trouble they had stood at her back door over last night... The building stayed on her side of the counter... and it was a no to selling. Whether it said anything past the door he could not read." I understood the gesture the moment she laid it down. Then it got explained to me three times while the clock that was supposed to be the tension sat there.

---

## 3. Where I got confused

- **"He's the youngest of the four of you."** It took me a second read to work out that "the four" is the three Bench men plus Ray, and that she's saying he already belongs on a retirees' stool. It's a good burn, but it lands late.
- **"She" is the machine and "she" is Ellen.** "She's slipping and catching... She's sulking, is all." "She'd done that since the winter the Legion broke the ball return on four." In scene 3: "She can hear you, you know, and she'll go slower out of spite." Ellen is standing right there. Twice I thought "she" meant Ellen.
- **Normie and Normand.** Normie is on the Bench and runs the pool sheet. "Arlene's Normand" cleared thirty thousand. Are they the same man? I assume Normand is Arlene's late husband, but two Normands in one chapter made me stop.
- **"The store he would not ask twice; he had sworn that the winter his father sat out in the truck."** I can't parse this. Did he swear never to put the store up again? Then why is he putting it up now? Or did he swear to ask for things only once? I read it three times and still don't know what he promised.
- **Why does Ray offer more than appraisal now,** when the contract hands him the building at appraised value on March 1 if she fails? In Chapter 1 he said, "I'm not buying your mistake cheap." Now he's paying a premium and risking his father's store to get it early. The page never tells me why now and why that price. My guess is Linda, but the page doesn't say it.
- **The Kate call.** This is meant to be a mystery and that's fine. But I don't even know who Kate is yet. A daughter, I think, from "Go to bed, Kate."

---

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **Ray's ask has an obviously safer option the page never rules out: wait.** If she misses March 1, he gets the building at appraisal without mortgaging the store. He's a careful man who watched his father nearly lose that store in a truck after dark. Him choosing the risky, expensive path needs a reason on the page, and there isn't one.
- **"owing him, which sat on her about as well as a wet coat."** We're in Ray's head, and this line reads Ellen's insides with a confidence he hasn't earned. It's the narrator talking, not Ray.
- **The ending moves fast.** In Chapter 1 she rekeys the doors in front of the whole league to take his key away. About thirty-six hours later she walks a new key into his store in front of the Bench. I can follow the logic (it's her key, given on her terms), and I mostly believe it. But it's a big reversal, and it happens off the page.
- **Danny signed the loan form "and never said a word."** I'd believe it if I'd met Danny for more than one phone call and a bag of rock salt.

---

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **The gesture explained after the gesture** (scene 4): "and that was the answer she had promised him before his committee sat, and it was a no to selling." This is the worst one in the chapter.
- **Every scene ends on a neat closing line, four out of four:**
  - "She was gone before the bell stopped."
  - "It went in all the way and turned nothing. He worked it back out and let it drop among the others."
  - "'At your counter,' she said. 'Under the clock.'"
  - "'That's the bell,' Normie said."
  Chapter 1 did the same thing ("Not my alley." / "Nobody," Denise said. "Ray won't.").
- **Ray's comparisons all come from machines, and inside one chapter it's a tic:** "like the air off a motor that's been running a while"; "which on a motor he'd have called a bearing"; "the way he heard a machine from the lot when she was about to go, one small wrong beat"; "the small shake of the head he gave a man who wanted to rebuild a yard-sale snowblower"; "the way you hear a door shut true."
- **Writerly patterns:** "She held the light steady, which most people can't, and close, which most people won't." "It told him what a key tells anybody." Both are pretty, and both sound like someone writing.
- **The grief line set apart in its own paragraph:** "Nobody had put a thing on this counter for him without his asking since Linda." And earlier: "A good many voices had come down that lane at him, Linda's among them, and none of them had come this low or got here in one piece." Two Linda beats in one chapter, both staged for effect.
- **Ellen's voice is a credit, not a fault:** "Then they had better not get notions." "I'm on the approach to lane seven." The two leads do not talk alike. Normie's sports call is distinct as well.

## 6. What I think is going on

Ellen is a retired air traffic controller (mandatory retirement at 56) who has sold her house and put the money into Kettle Lanes. Kate is her daughter. Ellen has told Kate about the house but not about the alley, and "I swear" is a promise she's already breaking. Kate will find out, probably on a Sunday at seven, and that will be a crisis. Ellen needs $250k for Arlene on March 1, and the bank will only lend if the alley clears $30k this winter and the money stays in the account. Only Arlene's late husband Normand ever cleared that. Ray is a widower of about six years. Linda was his wife and bowled the house high single (187). Locker 14 is hers, and Ray has the only key to the padlock. He wants the alley because it's the last place Linda is. Danny wants his father to have it, or at least to stop being dead inside. I predict the machines and the oil bill eat her margin. Ray will keep refusing her money, and that will become a problem because unpaid help could look like his stake in the place. Someone opens locker 14 near the climax. On March 1 she makes the number, or comes close and Ray declines to exercise his option. I predict they end up together with Ray holding a key she gave him, and the K on the road sign gets fixed in the last chapter so it reads Kettle Lanes again. Nobody wins the pool. March 1 stays blank.

## 7. The listener pass

- **Who's speaking:** "Granite Lakes writes me the loan the day this winter clears thirty thousand..." comes straight after "She didn't answer it." It has no tag, so the voice has to guess, and a listener assumes it's still Ray.
- **"She" for the machine** (see section 3) is worse aloud than on the page, because there's no italic or capital to help.
- **Numbers and lane codes come thick:** "seven's clutch," "the ball return on four," "the seven-and-eight pair," "the winter nine's belt let go," "'seventy-four," "thirty thousand," "cleared thirty," "eleven minutes past ten," "two for the front and two for the back." Read aloud, "the winter nine's belt let go" sounds like a year.
- **"On three stools by the coffee pot sat the Bench."** Heard aloud, you can't hear the capital B, so it sounds like furniture.
- **Long "and... and... and" sentences** that run out of breath: the light-holding paragraph ("and close... and her breath... and the only sound... and was waiting there"), Ray's ask speech, and the Tilton anecdote. In the narrator's voice these blur together.
- **Repeated sentence shapes:** "and it went over smooth, and he did it again, and it went over smooth again." That works once. But the chapter keeps falling into "X, and Y, and Z" rhythm until it sounds like a lullaby.
- **The one-sided Kate call** works aloud ("Something short." / "Not the rest."). It's one of the better passages for the ear.
- **"The store he would not ask twice..."** won't survive being heard. I couldn't get it from the page either.
- **Normie / Normand:** with no spelling to look at, a listener will hear the same man.

## 8. The romance

Yes, this is the reason to keep going.
- "He's the youngest of the four of you." I laughed.
- "Could have told you four." / "You did. Now I have it twice."
- The exchange shouted down the lane: "You still up there?" / "You can see me." / "Wanted to hear how you'd put it." / "I'm on the approach to lane seven." That made me want them together.
- "I asked him. He only does machines." Funny.
- The light scene, where the light "went ahead of his hands to the place they were going next, and was waiting there when they arrived," has real heat for a clean book.
- At the end, her breathing is wrong and her bare hand is an inch from the key. That works without the paragraphs explaining it.

---

## 9. What I want to know next

- What has Ellen told Kate, what is "the rest," and what did she swear?
- What's in locker 14?
- How does a bowling alley with ten 1970s machines and a fryer clear $30k in one New Hampshire winter? The page hasn't shown me a single way she plans to make money. That's the question I want answered, and it's the one the chapter ignores.
- Why does Ray want it badly enough to put up the store his father nearly lost?
- What does Ray do with that key?

The romance questions are pulling me. The plot question (will she save the alley?) is not. It's a March deadline with no pressure on it this week. I can't name a single thing that could go wrong for her in Chapter 3.
