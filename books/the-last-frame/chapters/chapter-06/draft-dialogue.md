# Chapter 6: Walking Ice

"You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?"

Hollis said it to the thermos while he poured.

The bob-house was six by eight and Ray was six foot one, so he had his head up in the heater's warm and his boots down in the cold off the two holes in the floor. The propane heater hissed. Out the one window the tip-ups stood in a crooked line across the snow, and every flag was down.

"Fella I knew in Meredith," Ray said, "shoveled the same widow's roof four times one winter, and there wasn't a thing wrong with that roof, and come April she married the fella who sold him the rake."

Hollis drank some coffee.

"You courting her," he said, "or just fixing her machines a lot?"

Ray didn't answer the second time. Something small and dark was coming out from the public access, and he leaned to the glass.

A navy vest over a parka. She walked the way she stood, square, with her weight even on both feet like somebody braced for a long shift, and she didn't watch her boots the way people do their first time out. Near in, he could see her hands were bare, with the gloves stuffed in the vest pocket.

"Who told her it'd hold?"

"Eleven inches of black ice at the access," Hollis said. "I drilled it Thursday. She asked was it black or white."

"And you told her."

"Told her it's walking ice and sled ice, and a car wants twelve. Leave the car at the access." Hollis blew across his coffee. "She wrote it down."

"You knew she was coming."

"Said seven. Said she'd like you here."

Ray knew what *like* meant in her mouth. It meant seven sharp.

"You might've told me."

"You didn't ask."

She knocked on the orange door with H. PIKE and his address stenciled on it, and Hollis got up.

"Going to check my flags."

"They're all down."

"Then it won't take long." He ducked out past her, and the door shut on the cold.

She sat down on the upturned bait bucket by the cooler before she said a word. Ray knew by now what it meant when she sat. Something had been settled before she ever got to the door.

She took three printed pages out of the vest, folded the long way, and flattened them on the cooler lid with her bare hands.

"Thank you for being here. I wanted you to hear this before the committee and before the town."

The short words had gone out of her again. Ray heard them go, and put it down to the cold.

"Page one is the last Saturday in February," she said. "An Open on derby weekend. Any team of four that pays the entry may bowl, and it takes the whole Saturday. Its final is Linda's Memorial, on all ten lanes, open to anyone, and the prize is Linda's trophy. The league's teams bowl in it with everyone else." She turned the pages to face him. "Page two is the plan if the ice goes. Page three is the terms."

Ray picked them up, read page one through, and laid all three back on the cooler.

"Without the Open," she said, "I do not make the bank's number."

Without Linda, she meant. Without Linda's name on it there was nothing on page one to bring the lake in off the ice.

He didn't say anything for a while. The heater hissed. She waited, and didn't fill it.

"You gave me your word," Ray said.

"I did."

"I told the whole league so."

"I know. I was at the counter."

"It's in Verna's book."

"It is."

"Dale's crew paid on it."

Her hands were flat on the pages to keep them from lifting in the heater's draft, and her fingers had gone white past the second knuckle, white down onto the backs of her hands. She didn't seem to know it. Ray pulled off his gloves, the right and then the left, and leaned across the cooler and took her right hand up off the paper.

"The house sets the lanes and the hours of championship weekend," she said. "Verna read it to the room. You heard her read it."

"I heard her." He opened the cuff of the glove with his thumb and started it over her fingers.

"The date does not move. The lanes do not move. The cup is Linda's."

"Anybody can bowl it."

"Anyone who enters."

"Anybody off the ice." He worked the glove's first finger down over hers to the web and smoothed it flat and went on to the next. The leather was shovel-dark across the palm, and her fingers went into it cold. "Anybody with a fish on the scale."

"Anyone. Denise may bowl for it. The Highway crew may. You may."

"The league's bowled for it five Februaries."

"The league will bowl for it this February, on the same lanes, with the whole lake watching."

He did the last finger and the thumb, and pressed the cuff closed round her wrist.

"Other one."

She gave it to him, and left the pages where they lay. The left was colder than the right had been, and he held it a second between both of his before the glove went on, which he hadn't meant to do, and then he got on with the glove.

"Mrs. Daly. That's Linda's league."

"I am inside the bylaws. Verna will tell you so."

"Dale's crew pulls twelve-hour shifts. The Legion's on a fixed check. I stood up for them. I stood up for you. My name's under it."

"I know where your name is."

"Then you know what you said."

She straightened the pages under his gloves.

"I said, 'It stays. Unless the numbers say otherwise.'"

Ray had the pit behind five back all at once, the flashlights down the lane and Denise's ball coming out of the dark and the whole house going up when the ten went over. He could hear a clutch start to slip from the far end of the lot with the truck running. A woman had said five words a foot from his ear, and he hadn't heard one of them.

"I never heard that."

She said nothing to it.

"Did you know I didn't hear it?"

She didn't answer straight off. Somewhere out toward the far shore the ice let go a long boom that went under the shack and away. Her gloved hands closed on the pages and creased the top one. She looked down at his gloves on her hands, and her eyes stayed there.

"I said it," she said, level.

He had asked her once. He looked at his own hands, bare on his knees, the knuckles going red in the cold that came up off the floor.

Then he got up, stooped under the roof, and opened the orange door, and the cold came in at the ankles and kept coming. He went out bare-handed with his gloves still on her, across the snow to Hollis, who was standing over a flag that was down.

"She's got business for your committee."

\* \* \*

Ellen came in last, and before Hollis could open anything she took Ray's gloves out from inside her vest, folded one into the other, and held them out to him. They were warm when he took them. She'd had them a week and brought them back to the place she'd borrowed them.

"Thank you for the loan," she said.

He put them in his jacket pocket and left them there. She had her own on. With her in, there were five of them in six by eight, Ray on the bait bucket with his knees against the treasurer's, and their breath had the one window fogged solid.

"Committee'll hear the owner," Hollis said.

She briefed from page one, standing, and she gave it to them the way she had given it to Ray, with no figures and Linda's name in the middle of it. The treasurer nodded at the name. The other one, a woman from the Sportsmen's Club in a snowmobile suit, wrote nothing down.

She had nearly finished when two flags went up outside at once, orange through the fog on the glass.

"Stand by," Ellen said, and was out the door before anybody else had moved. Ray went for the other one.

Out on the ice the two tip-ups stood side by side over their holes, and on both of them the spool was turning, paying line down into the black water as fast as it would go, spinning free the way a pulley spins when the belt's jumped off it. Ellen got to hers first and went down on one knee and stripped off her gloves and stuffed them in the vest, and took the line in her bare fingers.

"Don't horse it," Ray said.

"I am not horsing it."

She brought it up hand over hand with the line laid in loops on the snow behind her, slow and even, and she didn't horse it, and a yellow perch came up out of the hole the length of Ray's hand and lay on the ice flipping. His own line had gone slack. He brought his up to an empty hook with the smelt gone off it, and stood there holding it.

Back inside, Hollis looked at the perch in the bucket and then at Ellen, the way he had looked at people on the ice for twenty-eight years.

"Got a license?"

"Yes."

Hollis waited. Ellen waited. The treasurer looked from one to the other.

"Let's see it."

She had it inside the vest, folded behind her copy of the terms, and he unfolded it and read the front and turned it over and read the back.

"Says you bought this at ten past four this morning."

"I was up."

He handed it back. "Plan if the ice goes?"

"Page two."

He read page two and wrote in the waterproof notebook, a page past where he'd written the championship-weekend bylaw on the fifth of January with Ray beside him.

Ray leaned in off the bucket. He had come out here to let Hollis say no for him, and all Hollis had to do was say it. "Your people can come in off the ice and watch Linda's Memorial for nothing, Hollis, same as every year."

Hollis went on writing. "Our people come in off the ice to bowl."

"They'll pay to enter a final," the treasurer said, "and they'll fill the lot doing it." He had page three by then, and he read it out. "Cash refunds if weather stops play." He looked at Ellen over the top of it. "Cash?"

"Cash."

"Motion to put the Open on the derby's Saturday, at Kettle Lanes," Hollis said. "All in favor."

Three hands went up in six by eight, and one of them was Hollis's. Ray had no vote on the derby committee and never had. He watched his oldest friend's hand go up with the golf pencil still in it, and Hollis kept his eyes on page two and didn't look over.

The hands weren't down yet when Ellen went out the door. Through the gap before it swung shut Ray saw her at his tip-up, the one that had lost his fish, down on the ice with her gloves stuffed in the vest again and her bare hand in the smelt bucket. He was up and out the door after her, his gloves in his pocket, crossing the ice.

\* \* \*

Ray was on six's settee lacing his shoes, and the smoke off the Highway crew's burgers came drifting down the lanes from the flattop, the way it had every Tuesday since anybody could remember.

The notice for the Open had gone up Monday on the board by the Bench's stools in his own store, with Linda's Memorial printed as its final and any team of four welcome. Ellen had brought it in herself so he'd see it first. By tonight the league had it.

Down at the counter she was ringing up the crew's burgers. He knew where she was without turning his head, and then he turned it.

Dale Hutchins came down the settees with a legal pad and stood at the end of six.

"Pine Ridge Lanes is open Tuesdays," Dale said. "Gil's place, over in Halsey Falls. He'll take us at the half, the crew and the Legion both."

Ray finished the knot on his left shoe before he said anything. Gil Arsenault had taken two of his teams before, and been decent about it, which was worse.

"Half ends Saturday the thirtieth," he said. "Go before it and you forfeit."

"Don't mean to forfeit anything. We'll go the first night after." Dale looked back toward the snack bar. "Crew eats at her counter Tuesdays. They can eat at Gil's."

He held the pad down where Ray could read it. The Highway Department was on it in Dale's square print, with the Legion under it. Ray had known that Legion team since the winter they broke the ball return on four. Above them both Dale had left the top line empty.

"Rest of them go where you go," Dale said, and held out the pen.

Ray took it. "She said—" he said, and stopped.

Dale waited.

"She's inside the bylaws, Dale."

"You put her word in Verna's book."

"I know what I put in it."

He handed back the pen and the pad, the top line still empty. She'd declined to answer him at the January meeting, and he'd turned that over and put it down to a woman who wouldn't haggle over a word with the league watching. To set the minutes straight he'd have had to stand up in front of Dale's crew and tell them he'd vouched for half a sentence because he hadn't heard the other half. And the minutes were the last thing holding Linda's Saturday where it was.

Dale went back up the settees. The pad went from hand to hand along the Tuesday teams, captain to captain, and Ray watched it go. Most of them read it and passed it on without writing anything. Down at the counter Ellen watched it too, with the register drawer open, and didn't come down.

Denise came down with six's score sheets and the cigar box of pencils, which was her job on a Tuesday, and set them on the scorer's table. She didn't go back.

"Ray. Honey."

She looked past him at the counter, at Ellen, and back.

"That night in the pit—"

Ray waited. Denise squared the score sheets, which didn't need it.

"You were a help to her that night, don't let it go to your head, that's all I was going to say, and you've got one shoe laced and one not and Hollis is already on the approach, so finish your shoes, you're holding up the pair."

Ray bent back down to his laces.

\* \* \*

Ray laid his own statement from the credit union, printed that morning, on the back-room desk and turned it round to face Danny. Out the one window the light was going off Main Street toward the dock. The half had ended Saturday, and tomorrow was a Tuesday, the first night Dale's two teams could go.

He had sat in her second chair after close most nights since the ninth, and in that chair neither of them had said Linda's name.

"Run that against her bank's number," Ray said, "the way a bank'd figure a paydown. You've read Concord's papers, I'd guess."

Danny looked at the statement, and at him, and pulled the adding machine over. "You'll want it to the dollar."

"Round's fine."

"Not at a bank it isn't."

Danny punched it in the way he did the Saturday deposit, and pulled the handle, and the machine went chunk and ratcheted and fed out tape, and he punched and pulled again. It made the sound a starter makes on a cold morning, grinding through to something and catching. Ray watched the tape come up off the roller and curl over the edge of the desk.

Danny tore it off and laid it on the statement and turned it so Ray could read the bottom line.

Ray didn't know how far under she was. She'd given him the bank's number and the seven hundred a week, and told him the old rate wouldn't make hers, and past that she'd never said a figure, and he'd never asked. The number on the tape was what his own money would take off the bank's, the way a bank would count it. He knew what Linda's name could bring in off the lake in a night, from five Memorials and thirty years of league nights before them, and the tape said more than that. She could give the league its Saturday back and still come out ahead.

Danny reached into the desk drawer and slid the store's long checkbook across the blotter, the one with the stubs. "Store's got a line at the credit union. It's what the line's for."

Ray put it back in the drawer and shut the drawer. He didn't stop halfway this time.

"I asked the store once."

He reached inside his jacket and took out the cashier's check. He'd drawn it at noon at the credit union window, out of his own savings, nearly all of them, and the teller had asked was he sure. He laid it on the tape.

Danny read the face of it and didn't say the number.

"You'd already done it."

"Wanted to hear the machine say it."

Danny looked at him a while longer. Then he got his coat off the hook and put it on, and stopped in the doorway with one arm still finding the sleeve.

"Dad. You're allowed to want something that isn't on a shelf."

The bell over the front door went once behind him.

Late, with the store shut and only the desk lamp on, Ray took a Gagnon & Son envelope off the stack and put the check in it and sealed it, and ran his thumb along the flap once to make it hold. He took the top off a pen to write her name on the front. He held it over the envelope a while, and then he put the top back on and laid the pen down.

He put the envelope inside his jacket, in the pocket where his own checkbook lived, and zipped the jacket.
