# Cold reader notes: Chapter 6, "Walking Ice"

## Warning, above the score

**I'd keep reading, but only because of the last two scenes.** The first half of this chapter is three scenes in a row of Ray handing a document across a table or counter: the release to Arlene, the store transfer to Danny, the release to Ellen. I did not quit. **I came closest at line 67**, "Ray laid the second envelope from the Laconia office on the counter in front of his son", and from there I skimmed to the shanty. A reader who has already been bored by this book's paperwork once puts it down here. If I had stopped, that line is where.

## 1. Pull score: 4 / 10

The back half (Hollis asking the same question twice, the gloves) is the warmest stretch since the New Year's kiss. But it comes after about 1,500 words of contract talk, and what it sets up at the end is still a fight about a document and a name on a cup.

Average: about 2.9 of 5. The chapter's ending is stronger than its opening, and the opening is where readers leave.

## 2. Where I got bored

- **Line 15**, "At his own counter, in front of his son, he had told Ellen his no was worth nothing, coming from the man who'd buy if she missed." I read that sentence half a page ago, at the end of Chapter 5. The book is summarizing itself to me.
- **Lines 9–45**, the release explained, then Arlene's three outcomes ("The Pines gives my place to the next woman... I get my money months from now... the man from Concord buys the lot"). This is the money mechanics for the third or fourth time. I already knew the stakes, and here they come as a list.
- **Line 67 onward, scene 2.** "Ray laid the second envelope." The word "second" is what lost me: I knew right away I was in for another round of signing. Then the credit-union form from December comes back out of the drawer. That makes three documents in about forty lines.
- **Lines 109–125**: "He took the release out of the envelope and laid it flat, with his name on the first line and the two lines under it blank." Then she reads it, "down the one clause, and back to the top, and down it again." So do I, for the third time this chapter.
- **Lines 227–229**: Hollis on Gil Arsenault "from Pine Ridge in Halsey Falls" and the 'ninety-four open. This is a stranger's name, a lane's name and a town's name delivered as a plot fact. My attention went.

Plainly: this chapter's engine is a release form. I don't care about a release form. I care about whether Ray can stand his house after supper, and the chapter gives that one paragraph, at the ice shanty.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **Line 3, where are we?** "By the clock over Arlene's stove." It took the wintergreen smell coming up a pharmacy stair and the boxes for The Pines to tell me we're in Arlene's apartment on Main Street. That was fine once I had it, but the first sentence drops me into a kitchen I've never been in.
- **The money is now six papers deep, and I can't hold it.** There's the backup contract, the release, Arlene's note, Judy's commitment, the credit-union form, the store transfer, and now an "option to buy it back." Ellen says "Without you behind me, a miss is a bank sale," but Arlene just said *she* forecloses. Which bank? I thought Arlene held the note. I stopped trying to work it out.
- **Danny, line 81**: "I signed that so you could buy it on her yes. You're standing in her paper waiting on her no." Her yes to *what*, her no to *what*? I worked out that "her no" means her failing, but by ear "her no" sounds like Ellen refusing something.
- **Arlene, lines 45–55**: "I won't hold a man to a paper he's asked out of," then "Bring me her signature... I sign last." For a second I didn't know whether she had agreed. She has handed the decision to Ellen, and the page leaves me to work that out.
- **"Voyons donc."** I gather it's French and that Arlene is Franco-American, but nothing on the page says so.
- **Derby days.** Chapter 5 put the ice-fishing derby on "the last Saturday in February." Now there's "derby Sunday" and "the derby's indoor day... on Sunday." So the derby runs both days? I had to stop and sort Saturday (Linda's) from Sunday (the Open).
- **Line 167**, "pegged out the hand" and "the board on the upturned bucket": I guessed cribbage. The word is never used.
- **Line 189**, Hollis: "So say yes, or get out of the paper." Say yes to what? Does Hollis know about Ellen's ask for Linda's name? I assume the Bench told the whole town, but the page doesn't say.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **Ellen reads "subject to committee" as a formality (line 257).** This is the woman who quoted her own sentence back word for word "with the stop in the same place," who told Ray "an answer was only true with its condition attached," and who wrote up thirty-one years of errors inside the hour. Now she misses the one condition on the loan that the whole book hangs on. The page knows it's out of character ("the first thing he had ever watched her read wrong") and puts it down to her stress-calm. I half bought it. It reads as the plot needing a new obstacle at the midpoint.
- **Judy happens to be the second vote on the derby committee, and happens to be fishing two hundred yards away that Saturday.** Small towns overlap, I know. But the loan officer, the derby gatekeeper and the next scene are all on the same patch of ice at once, and that felt arranged.
- **Ellen bought a fishing license "yesterday"** so she could pull Hollis's flag in front of him. It's a staged competence display. She walked out to pitch a bowling event, not to fish. It's charming, but I could see the gears.
- **Arlene gives up the paper that holds her bed at The Pines** on a principle about her late husband ("I won't hold a man to a paper he's asked out of"), one breath after telling Ray she'd go back on the list at eighty-four. Passing it to Ellen makes it workable, but Arlene comes off as a plot valve, not a woman protecting her last home.
- **Ellen's last line.** In front of Ray she keeps his no ("The league's Saturday stays the way Linda bowled it"). Two pages later she announces she'll go around him to Linda's friends about his dead wife's name. One push from Hollis and Judy, and she has reversed herself. The obvious safer option, an Open that draws without Linda's name, is closed only by Hollis's one opinion. Some romance readers will turn on her here: he said no about his wife, twice, and she's going over his head.
- **Ray's release, the pressure test.** It follows from what he knows: Normie's sentence, Danny's condition, his own "make my no cost me." The page closes the obvious alternative (say yes to the Memorial) through his grief. I believed Ray. I believed Ellen's refusal to sign too ("You want me to sign it so your no feels clean"). Her refusal is the most logical thing in the chapter.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **Talking to objects, again.** "'You're late,' Danny told the carton of ice cleats." "'You're near the end of this roll,' Ray told the price gun." This is the third chapter running with a man talking to a thing (the lock in Ch 2, "Normie told his plate" in Ch 4). It's a tic now.
- **"Fella I knew in Wolfeboro..."** is Ray's third "fella I knew in [town]" anecdote (Tilton in Ch 2). The clock story itself is good, but the frame is a formula.
- **Every comparison comes from the character's job, by formula.** Arlene tells it "the way she'd tell a man his fuel-oil bill." Judy looks "the way she'd look at a balance sheet with a coffee ring on it." Hollis looks the fish over "like a measurement" (the same comparison as Ch 3). Danny: "those are different invoices." Everyone speaks commerce.
- **Characters talk alike.** Danny's "Because those are different invoices" is Ellen's vocabulary in her love interest's son's mouth. "You're allowed to want something that isn't on a shelf" is a therapist's line, not a hardware clerk's.
- **The narrator rules on the dialogue.** "She had it exactly, and the last was the true one." "Her voice got calmer the further she went, and she had told him herself what that calm was." Both tell me what the scene already showed.
- **A neat exchange**: "'You're right,' he said. / 'So are you.'"
- **An aphorism with no owner**: "The lake saved her temper for March and never gave notice." Who thinks this? It's a plant wearing a proverb.
- **Every scene ends on a placed object, quietly:** the plate set down without asking; "left both papers lying on the counter where they were"; the desk drawer "open, and shut"; "the board left on the bucket and his peg ahead"; "She had friends." Five for five.
- **Reused details:** supper at a fixed hour and bed by half past nine (Ch 2, Ch 4, Ch 6); red knuckles from the cold (every chapter); "squared" (he squared it, he squared the envelope).

## 6. What I think is going on (and what the page has already given away)

**Already given away:** the ice is going to go on derby weekend. "Plan if the ice goes?", "Cash back if weather stops play. I'd be the one calling it," "The lake saved her temper for March and never gave notice," "walking ice," and Ellen's soaked gloves all point one way. I'd bet heavily that derby weekend brings an ice emergency on the lake. Ellen will run it in her tower voice, and either the refunds cost her the number or the rescue wins the town. Also given away: Judy's committee is the late trap. Even a winter that "clears" may not be enough if it's close, so expect a near miss on the number and a committee scene. Locker fourteen still holds Linda: her ball, her shoes, maybe an unfinished score sheet, and Ray will open it for Ellen. The dark K will be lit for the ending. Ellen's untold "third item" is about him.

**My committed read:** Ellen goes to Linda's friends (Denise, Gil Arsenault, Verna's records), and they say Linda would have wanted her night open to everyone who wanted to beat her. Ray hears it from the Bench before he hears it from Ellen, and that's the black moment: she went around him. He comes round after sitting in the truck one more time. He gives the name (or bowls in the Open himself), the derby weekend is a crowd, the ice scare turns into the town stuck at the alley again, and the winter clears by a hair. Then Ellen finally signs the release, but only once she's "winning," or tears it up. The ending is Ray as Kettle Maintenance on an invoice for good, the store with Danny, the K lit, and all ten lanes going at once. I'm fairly sure of the shape and not interested in being proved right, which is the problem.

**Chapter 7 prediction:** Ellen goes to ask "the people it belongs to": Denise first, then probably Gil Arsenault in Halsey Falls or Verna's binder. Ray finds out, or Danny sees it. 65% sure. A 25% chance it cuts to a Kate visit or to Judy's committee details instead.

## 7. The listener pass

- **"Voyons donc."** A TTS voice will mangle it, and a listener won't know what language it was.
- **Untagged speakers, lines 273–281:** "You heard Hollis." / "And you heard Judy." / "Are you changing your answer?" / "No." By ear I lost who said "And you heard Judy," and with it who answered "No." That's the emotional core of the closing exchange.
- **Lines 189–193:** "So say yes, or get out of the paper." / "Signed my half. She won't sign hers." / "Ray studied the board a while. 'Did I say I'm ahead?'" Two Ray lines in a row after an untagged Hollis line. Hearing it, I thought "Did I say I'm ahead?" was Hollis.
- **Line 243**, "Hollis says yes to the derby's indoor day at my place on Sunday. He says it wants yours." No tag, and "the derby's indoor day at my place on Sunday" is a clotted noun stack aloud.
- **"Her temper" (line 201)** sounds like Ellen's temper until the ear catches up and decides it means the lake's.
- **Paper words blur aloud:** release, paper, that paper, her paper, envelope, second envelope, credit-union form, commitment, option, invoices. A listener cannot tell which document is on the table.
- **Name pile, line 229:** "Gil Arsenault'd drive over from Pine Ridge in Halsey Falls for it. She beat him in the 'ninety-four open." That's a person, a contracted "'d," a lane, a town and a year in two sentences. I'd lose all of it.
- **Rhythm:** long "and... and... and" sentences end almost every paragraph ("She folded the release along its creases and slid it into the envelope and put the envelope in his hand"). Read aloud for twenty minutes it becomes a lull, and the lull is the boredom.
- **"Ray pegged out the hand"**: cribbage jargon in a chapter's opening line, with the game never named.
- **"Ten inches of black ice under three of white"** is fine aloud and actually good.

## 8. The romance

Yes, in the back half, and not before.
- The knuckle: "she drew her two fingers up the margin, and the knuckle with the pencil lead on it came against his thumb and stayed. It was warm." A good small charge, earned by a page of both of them refusing to let go of a document.
- The laugh: Hollis repeating "You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?" after Ray's clock story, word for word. Then "I had my blade up, Ray." I laughed.
- The want: "Now I'd like to spend them where she is."
- The gloves (below) made me want them together more than either kiss did.

I didn't laugh in the first three scenes, and nothing in them made me want the couple together. Those scenes are about a man and his paperwork.

## 9. What I want to know next

- Will Ellen actually go around Ray to Linda's friends, and what does it cost her with him when he finds out? This is the one real pull out of the chapter.
- Is she close or not? "Close, is it?" / "At present." I don't know the number, so I can't feel the gap. That question has no pull for me, and it's supposed to be the main plot.
- Does the ice go on derby weekend? I want it to, because it's the only physical danger the book has offered since the snowstorm.
- What's in locker fourteen? The question is still there, but six chapters in it has gone stale.

I do not want to know what happens to the release. I'm told it's in her desk drawer, and I'm content to leave it there.
