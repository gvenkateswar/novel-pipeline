# Chapter 6: Walking Ice — cold reader notes, round 2

**Warning above the score: I'd keep reading, but only for the last scene.** I did not stop. I skimmed scene 2 and would have skimmed it on a phone. The closest I came to putting it down was at "Danny opened the drawer under the register, took out the credit-union form from December and laid it beside the envelope" (scene 2). By that point there were three papers on two counters and the scene was telling me things I already knew.

## 1. Pull score: 4 / 10

This is the paperwork chapter. Five scenes go around the same March-first deal from five chairs. The release, Arlene's condition, Danny's condition, the bank's committee and the derby committee each add a clause, and none of them changes the question I have had since Chapter 1. What saves it from a 3 is the alley counter scene, Ray's "four hours" line on the ice, and a last line that finally promises a real fight.

## 2. Where I got bored

1. **Scene 1, from "It's a release. One clause, and the three of us sign it, and that backup paper's done with."** It's the sixth chapter in a row that explains the March-first deal. I know the deal, and I don't need it from a new chair.
2. **Scene 1, "He had been sore at her since the day he took his offer up Kettle Road, a man who had kept the store a museum six years and another in a padlocked locker down at the alley."** My eyes slid off it. It's backstory dressed as a riddle.
3. **Scene 2, nearly all of it.** "Grandpa put this store up once, in 'seventy-four, and you swore you'd never ask it twice." I have heard about 1974 and the truck in the dooryard twice already, and the overcoat-and-drawer paragraph about Lucien is a rerun of Ch 4. Danny gets a good line and then a speech.
4. **Scene 4, "'Plan if the ice goes?' / 'Page two.'"** Ellen's competence shown again, the same way: she has the sheet, the number, the license bought yesterday. I already believe she's prepared. Doing it a fifth time is texture, not story.
5. **The book as a whole at this point:** thirty thousand, March first, the paper, the no. Every chapter re-briefs them. By Ch 6 I don't care whether she clears the number. I'm sure she will, so the money plot isn't pulling. Only the Linda's-name fight is.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **Scene 1, "Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain."** It has no tag and sits right after a Ray line, so it reads like Arlene talking to herself. Why does Ray need to ask, anyway? In Ch 4 he already worked out that without the paper Arlene loses her place at The Pines.
- **"I get my money months from now, after I've foreclosed on that woman of yours."** So Arlene holds a note on Ellen? It was mentioned once, in passing, in Ch 4. I had to rebuild the deal myself: Ellen owes Arlene, Ray is the backup buyer, and without him it becomes a foreclosure.
- **"She'd been angry at his anger since the day she sold."** I can't picture what this means.
- **Scene 2, "Ray laid the envelope on the counter in front of his son: the store, Danny's, on the first of March."** I thought this was the release envelope from the last scene. Only "the new envelope" and "both papers" at the end told me there were two.
- **Scene 2, "He put the gun in his back pocket. 'You're allowed to want something that isn't on a shelf.'"** Which he? It sounds like a father's line. I gave it to Ray, then saw the gun was in Danny's back pocket at the end of the scene and went back. Danny then speaks four paragraphs running.
- **"I signed that so you could buy it on her yes."** It took a beat to place this: the loan form from Ch 2, for buying the building if Ellen sold.
- **"I had my blade up, Ray."** I worked out that Hollis means he saw the kiss under the sign, but only after a second's pause.
- **"Twenty-eight years I asked men out on this ice what they were doing there."** As what? I know Hollis as a plow driver. Was he a warden? It's never said.
- **"The lake, Hollis meant, saved her temper for March and never gave notice."** He said nothing like that. The narrator is translating a line that doesn't contain the meaning.
- **"Two flags sprang up in the window."** What flags? I don't know ice fishing. "Tip-up" is never explained, and neither is "walked out from the access along his stakes" or "Point side, two tip-ups."
- **Why Ellen hauls the fish.** Whose line is it? Hollis's? Does she go out to prove something, and to whom? On the page it reads like she did it so her gloves would get wet.
- **Judy's committee.** Ellen calls "subject to committee" a formality, the narrator says it's "the first thing he had ever watched her read wrong," and then Judy says "For a winter that clears, it is." That sounds like Judy agreeing with her. What did Ellen get wrong? Is there a new risk (a near miss gets no grace) or not? I can't tell whether the stakes went up.
- **The derby days.** Ch 5 put the derby on the last Saturday. Now it's "derby Sunday," with the Memorial on Saturday. I sorted it out, but a listener wouldn't.
- **The ending against scene 4.** In the shack Ellen publicly keeps Ray's no ("Nobody's yet... The league's Saturday stays the way Linda bowled it"), and Ray sits there moved by it. Two scenes later she's going to Linda's friends for Linda's name. Did Hollis's argument change her mind? Did Judy's? The page never shows her change, only the result.
- **"You heard Hollis."** About the ice? The name? The gloves? "And you heard Judy" is the same problem.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **Ray's release. He already rejected this in Ch 4, for the reason Arlene gives him here.** Ch 4: "He could tear up the backup contract tonight. But the paper was what let Arlene hold her note, and without it her place at The Pines went to the next name on the list." Nothing new has happened since, yet he pays a Laconia lawyer and asks the woman he protects to give up her bed so his no can be clean. Ellen names the selfishness, which helps. Still, the page never closes the obvious question: why would he think this could work? He knew the answer two chapters ago.
- **Arlene agreeing at all.** She has just said that without the paper she goes back on the list at eighty-four, then says "I won't hold a man to a paper he's asked out of" and agrees to sign last. Pride, maybe. But she's risking the one thing she has talked about in every scene, on principle, at 7 a.m., and handing the decision to Ellen. I half-believed it.
- **Ellen plunging her wool gloves into the hole in January.** This woman tapes every switch and has a page two for a melting lake, and she kneels at the hole and hauls a line bare-wristed in wool. It's out of character, and it's convenient: it exists so Ray can glove her. Ray watches and "said nothing yet," which also rings false for a man who has spent the whole book warming her hands.
- **Judy is both the loan officer and the second vote on the derby committee.** It's a small town, so I'll take it, but it's handy.
- **Ellen's reversal at the end** (see Confusion). In danger-and-pressure terms she has a safer option on the page: Hollis has already given her the warm room, Judy has said yes for Sunday, and a nameless Open still brings in derby families. She chooses to go around a widower to put his dead wife's name on a cash cup. That may be the point, but the page doesn't show why the safer, nameless Open won't do. Hollis's one line ("you'll get the ones who were coming anyway") is all there is. So I lost a little sympathy for her at the very moment the book wants me leaning in.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **"Hollis looked it over like a measurement."** It's Ch 3's line again ("Hollis said nothing, which from Hollis was a measurement"). It has become his tag.
- **The "the way she'd..." similes in a row:** "told it the way she'd tell a man his fuel-oil bill"; "the way she'd look at a balance sheet with a coffee ring on it"; "like a man buying a boat." Same construction, every character, one chapter.
- **"She'd been angry at his anger since the day she sold."** An abstract emotion statement.
- **Feelings explained after the gesture:** "He sat on his bucket and watched her keep his no for him, in front of him." / "Her voice got calmer the further she went, and she had told him herself what that calm was." / "She meant the bank's committee, a different table from Hollis's." / "The lake, Hollis meant, ..." The narrator keeps stepping in to tell me what a line meant.
- **"'You're right,' he said. / 'So are you.'"** Too symmetrical. It's the kind of exchange a script makes.
- **Every scene ends on a neat image:** the plate set down "without asking whether he'd eaten"; "left both papers lying on the counter where they were"; "the steel drawer of the office desk open, and shut"; "with the board left on the bucket and his peg ahead"; "She had friends." Five for five.
- **Everyone talks in the same wise-laconic Yankee register.** Arlene: "I wanted it to live." Danny: "those are different invoices" and "Half out's not out." Hollis: "I never once had to ask a third time." Judy: "The underwriter has never met you" and "Then it won't take long." Every secondary character gets an aphorism, and Danny borrows Ellen's invoice motif. Only Ray's "fella I knew in..." story has its own shape, and that's now a repeated device (Tilton in Ch 2, Wolfeboro here).
- **Cold hands warmed by Ray, for the fourth time:** Ch 3 the shovel, Ch 4 the pen ("You'll want that hand in your vest"), Ch 6 the knuckle on the release, Ch 6 the gloves. Two hand scenes in this chapter alone. The glove scene is good on its own, but I could see it coming the moment her gloves went into the water.
- **Rerun callbacks:** 1974 and the truck in the dooryard (a third time), Lucien at the register the week he died (a second time), "eight days" (a third time), "museum."

## 7. The listener pass

- **Scene 1, "Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain."** Untagged, after a Ray line. A voice reads it as Arlene.
- **"Voyons donc."** A TTS voice will mangle it, and there's no gloss.
- **Scene 2, Danny's run.** Four paragraphs of Danny, one starting "He put the gun in his back pocket." Aloud, I'd assign at least one of them to Ray.
- **The paper count.** Aloud I heard: the envelope, the release, that paper, the backup paper, the credit-union form, the new envelope, both papers. A listener can't keep track of which is which.
- **"Gil Arsenault would drive over for it from Pine Ridge, his place in Halsey Falls."** A new name and two place names in one breath, gone by the next line.
- **Ice-fishing and cribbage jargon:** "pegged out the hand," "the board on the upturned bucket" (the word cribbage never appears), "tip-ups," "two flags sprang up in the window," "walked out from the access along his stakes," "Point side, two tip-ups." Each is a small fog, and four of them stack in one scene.
- **Run of identical sentence shapes in scene 3:** "She took the bulbs first. She put the sack on the shelf... She counted three more pencils... She finished a job... She laid the last pencil in... She put her glasses on... She took the glasses off." That's "She [verb]ed" six times in a short stretch.
- **"Hollis says yes to the derby's indoor day at my place on Sunday. He says it wants yours."** "It wants yours" is opaque aloud.
- **"Ten inches of black ice under three of white."** Fine aloud, but it's another number to hold, and the book keeps handing me numbers.
- **"'nineteen seventy-one,' ''seventy-four,' ''ninety-four open'"** read cleanly. No codes or long numbers this chapter, which is good.
