# Chapter 6: Walking Ice

At seven o'clock the scanner on Arlene's counter reported smoke showing from a chimney out past the Pelletiers', and Arlene said, "They never clean it," in the voice she used for the price of oil.

Ray had come up the steep side stair with the envelope inside his jacket, and the treads were worn hollow in the middle, and by spring there would be somebody else's feet on them. Boxes stood along the wall and under the window that looked down Main Street to the lake, each one marked for The Pines in the green hand of her ledgers. The ham was in the oven at seven in the morning, which was Arlene all over.

He laid the envelope beside her cup before he sat down.

"I'd like you to let me out of that paper."

Arlene didn't touch it. She turned the scanner down a notch.

He told her what was in it. A mutual release, one clause, that let all three of them out of the backup contract once all three had signed. A lawyer in Laconia had drawn it on Monday, on Ray's money.

"Have your own lawyer read it before you sign anything."

"You had a lawyer draw it before you asked me."

"Seemed a poor way to ask, with nothing in my hand."

She let that sit where he'd put it.

A week ago at his own counter, in front of his son, he had said out loud what his no was worth, and on the first of March the store went to Danny, and since then the two had come to be one paper. He would not hand his boy the store as the man the end stool had made him out to be over the cake.

"Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain."

She set her cup down and took a photograph in a dime-store frame off the windowsill, he didn't see of what, and laid it face down in the open box on the chair beside her.

"If she misses and there's no sale on the first of March, The Pines gives my place to the next woman, and I go back on the list at eighty-four."

He didn't answer. She folded one flap down over the photograph.

"I get my money months from now, after I've foreclosed on that woman."

The second flap went down. Her hands were spotted and steady, the knuckles gone big the way his mother's had.

"And the man from Concord buys the lot for the land under it."

She tore a strip of tape off the roll with her teeth and ran it down the seam and printed the name of the place across the top in green, and pushed the box with her foot to stand by the door with the others.

Then she gave him the part he hadn't asked for, in the same voice.

"It sat listed two years, Raymond. You came in eight days after she did. I'd signed with her by then, and I'd have signed with her anyway. You'd have kept it a museum. I wanted it to live."

Kettle Road came back to him whole, the dark and the offer folded in his coat and the lights already on up the hill. He had been sore at Arlene over it since, and never said, and she had known.

"In nineteen seventy-one I held Normand to a building he never wanted, and he gave it eighteen years. I won't hold a man to a paper he's asked out of."

She laid the green pen on the envelope. Ray slid the release out and found his line and signed it, fast and plain, the way he signed for a pallet of rock salt, and didn't read it over again.

Arlene watched him do it.

"Raymond, you've signed that like a man buying a boat."

He laid her pen by her cup and pushed the paper across, and she read his name on it and left the pen where it lay.

"Voyons donc. Bring me her signature. It's hers to risk as much as mine. I sign last."

She folded it back into its envelope, and put it into his hand, and closed his fingers on it.

Then she was up with the oven door open, the ham out on the stovetop and the knife going, and she set a plate in front of him without asking whether he had eaten.

\* \* \*

"You're late," Danny said to the carton of ice cleats.

"I was at Arlene's."

The price gun clicked, and a pair of cleats went into the bin with its tag on, and the gun clicked again. Over by the front window the Bench sat on its three stools with the free coffee.

Ray took the second envelope from the Laconia office out of his jacket and laid it on the counter in front of his son. Inside was the store, Danny's on the first of March, with a line for each of them. He wanted Danny's name on it before the coffee got cold, the whole store handed across on one clean page, which was more than Lucien had ever managed. He picked up the price gun while he waited, because it was there.

Danny read the top page and didn't reach for a pen.

"Is this you giving me the store, or you leaving the store? Because those are different invoices."

"I signed my half of getting out this morning."

Danny looked at him a while. Then he opened the drawer under the register and took out the December form from the credit union, his own name on the last line, and laid it beside the envelope. It was the first time he had ever mentioned it.

"Grandpa put this store up once, in 'seventy-four, and you swore you'd never ask it twice. I signed this before you could ask."

Ray had the gun in his hand and nothing to price.

Danny laid one finger on the form. "I signed that so you could buy it on her yes. You're standing in her paper waiting on her no, and I won't put my name to that. Half out's not out."

He moved the finger to the envelope. "Get all the way out of her paper and I'll sign this one."

Danny reached across and took the price gun out of his father's hand, and was gentle about it.

"It's said Son on that sign since before I was born. I'd like it to mean me."

He slid the gun into his back pocket. "You're allowed to want something that isn't on a shelf."

The Bench had heard every word of it, and for once the man on the end stool called nothing. The envelope would sit on this counter in front of three men every morning until it was signed.

The bell over the door went, and a man in a plow jacket came in stamping his boots, wanting something. Danny went down the aisle to him, said, "Morning," and left both papers where they lay.

\* \* \*

Ellen was counting the open bowlers' pencils back into the cigar box, point down, when he came in out of the vestibule with the sack of bulbs under his arm. Nine lanes were dark. The light was still on over lane two, where the last open bowler had finished his string and sat on the settee in his socks, and behind the snack bar the grill ticked as it cooled.

He set the sack on the counter. "You left these."

She finished the pencils first. The man from lane two came up in his boots and laid his pencil on the counter and said good night to the both of them, and went out past Ray into the cold, and she counted his in with the rest and closed the lid. Then she took the bulbs and put them on the shelf under the register.

Ray took the release out of his jacket and unfolded it and laid it on the counter between them, his name on the first line and two lines blank under it, and turned it round to face her. He kept the far edge under his fingers.

"Sign the release."

She put two fingers on the near corner and left them there.

"So that when I tell you no, it's only a no, and nobody in this town can say I'm waiting on your building."

She waited. He gave her the rest of it plain.

"Danny won't take the store while I'm behind you. He told me so this morning. Arlene signs last, after you."

She read it. He watched her eyes go down the one clause and come back up to the top and go down it again, and the grill ticked behind her.

"This is the cost you meant."

"It's the half of it I could sign."

She had not sat down since he came in, and he saw now that she wasn't going to.

"That paper is Arlene's bed."

He took that one. He had watched Arlene tape a box of it shut this morning and print the name of the place on the lid. Her two fingers pressed a little flatter on the corner, and his stayed where they were.

"It's the house Kate let me spend, too."

He took that one too. He had heard about that house across his own counter the day she asked him, and he had worked at not thinking about it since.

"Without you behind me, a miss is a bank sale."

He took that one, and it came to him in Arlene's voice as much as hers, the list and the foreclosure and the man from Concord walking the lot. His thumb had come onto the face of the paper, and he left it there.

"You want me to sign it so your no feels clean."

She had it exactly, and the last one was the true one.

He didn't let go of the paper, and neither did she.

"Then I keep the paper and write you an option to buy it back off me." He said it slowly, so she'd hear he had thought it through in the truck. "At what I paid, any day you've got it, and it's nobody's business how long that takes."

She didn't answer it right off, and he watched her take it apart. Her eyes went to the lit lane and the office door and back to his name. He could see her running it. Arlene would go into The Pines on the first of March, paid. The building would come back to her, in a year or in five. The lanes would stay lit. For as long as it took the grill to tick twice, he thought she was going to take it.

"Then you'd be my landlord till I could afford you."

She said it gently, and it closed the thing as neatly as she'd closed the cigar box.

"You're right," he said.

"So are you."

The grill had gone quiet behind her.

The release lay between their hands. She had the near corner under two fingers. He had the far edge, and his thumb had come onto the face of the paper at the bank sale and was still there, a little way down the one clause. Her knuckles were dry from the winter, and there was a gray smudge of pencil lead along one of them from the box, and he looked at it longer than a man needs to look at a smudge. Neither of them moved first. Then she drew her two fingers up the margin, the way she'd keep her place in a line she meant to read again, and the knuckle with the lead on it came against his thumb and stopped there. It was warm. She didn't take it back, and he didn't move his, and the light over lane two went on shining on an empty lane.

"What would get your name on it?"

"A winter I was winning."

He took his hand off the paper. "It'll keep."

He left it on her counter and went out through the vestibule, and with his hand on the cold bar of the outer door he heard the steel drawer of the office desk run open on its dry runner, and shut.

\* \* \*

Ray counted his hand and moved his back peg past Hollis's front one. "That's me ahead, Hollis. First time in eleven winters."

The heater hissed in the corner. A week to the day after Arlene's kitchen, the bob-house was warm at head height and cold at the ankles.

Hollis gathered the cards. "You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?"

"Four's elevator is hanging pins again," Ray said.

Hollis shuffled. "You courting her?"

Ray looked at the board.

"I had my blade up, Ray."

"I'm courting her," Ray said to the board. "Supper's done by six, and I'm in bed by half past nine, and that's the better part of four hours with nobody in them. I meant to spend them up at Arlene's, on her machines. Now I'd like to spend them where she is."

Hollis wrote nothing down and said nothing for a second, which from Hollis was a speech.

"So say yes, or get out of the paper."

"Signed my half. She won't sign hers."

Hollis dealt. Ray picked up his cards and studied the board. "Did I say I'm ahead?"

"Twice."

Somebody knocked on the orange door. It was Ellen, walked out from the access along Hollis's trail flags, and Hollis gave her the ice he had measured at first light before he gave her good morning.

"Ten inches of black ice under three of white. That's walking ice. You walked it."

She stayed on her feet, which with her meant nothing was settled yet.

"An Open on derby Sunday. Anyone who pays may enter. A new cup, with nobody's name on it. The league's Saturday stays the way Linda bowled it."

She didn't look at Ray to see if he'd stop her. She was keeping his no for him, in front of him, and he watched her do it.

"Plan if the ice goes?" Hollis said.

"Page two." She handed him her sheets, and he turned to it and read it to himself.

Behind him in the little window two flags sprang up off the snow.

"Stand by," Ellen said, and went out.

Through the door Ray watched her kneel at the near tip-up and bring the line in hand over hand, both gloves in the hole to the wrist, and land a pickerel the length of her forearm. She came back in with her gloves soaked black, and he looked at her hands and said nothing yet.

"Kids' weigh-in and the raffle want a warm room on Sunday," Hollis said, "and it gets the families off my ice. That's my yes. You'll want Judy's."

The derby's indoor day was hers on Sunday, for the house's fee, and she still didn't sit down.

Hollis handed back her pages. "People come in off this ice for Linda's name. They bowled against her. Gil Arsenault'd drive over from Pine Ridge in Halsey Falls for it. She beat him in the 'ninety-four open, and he still brings it up. Put up a cup with nobody on it and you'll get the ones who were coming anyway."

Ellen said nothing to it.

"Judy's the other vote out here," Hollis said. "Point side, two tip-ups."

Ellen was out the orange door. Ray was up and after her, and the board stayed behind on its bucket with his peg ahead.

\* \* \*

"Saturdays I come out here so nobody talks to me about money," Judy said.

She was crouched at the hole with a smelt from her bucket, and the snow squeaked under Ray's boots and Ellen's as they came up, the dry squeak of hard cold. The sun was up over the far shore with no warmth in it.

"Hollis says yes to the derby's indoor day at my place on Sunday. He says it wants yours."

Judy hooked the smelt through the back and looked up at Ellen once, the way she'd look at a file.

"Close, is it? Then you know March first goes to committee. I like you. The underwriter has never met you."

She lowered the smelt into the black water and began paying the line down after it.

"The commitment says subject to committee," Ellen said. "I read that as a formality."

It was the first thing he had ever watched her read wrong. Her voice had gone calmer from the first word to the last, and in the dark on lane five, with a pin in her hands, she had told him what that calm was.

Judy let the last of the line down, set the flag, and stood up.

"For a winter that clears, it is."

She wiped her fingers on her coat. "You've got my yes for Sunday."

Then she looked from one of them to the other. "Going to check my flags."

"They're both down," Ray said.

"Then it won't take long."

She walked off across the ice toward the access.

Ellen's hands had gone white inside the wet wool, and she was holding them a little away from her coat.

Ray pulled his gloves loose and took her left hand, and peeled the soaked wool from it and put it in his pocket. Then he put his own glove on her. He worked the thumb down first and then each finger into its place, one at a time, pushing the leather down between them with his own, and her hand was cold enough that he could feel it through the lining.

"You heard Hollis," he said, working.

He peeled the right one and did it the same way. The last hands he had done this for were Danny's, small, at the bus stop at the bottom of School Street. His gloves were too big for her and warm from him, and the fingers stood out empty at the ends, and he pinched each one down to the end of hers.

"And you heard Judy."

"These went in that hole to the wrist after his fish, and they'd have frozen to the line before they asked anybody for gloves." He closed the cuff over her wrist. "You've got about the coldest hands on this lake, and I'd like them warm."

Ellen looked down at his gloves on her hands, and then up at him. "Are you changing your answer?"

"No."

"Then your no stays what it is, and I'll go and ask the people it belongs to."

"It belongs to me."

"It has Linda's name on it. She had friends."
