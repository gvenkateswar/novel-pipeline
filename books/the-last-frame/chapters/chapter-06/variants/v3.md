# Chapter 6: Walking Ice

At seven o'clock Arlene's scanner was turned low on the counter, and the dispatcher sounded like a man talking to himself in the next apartment. Ray had come up the side stair with the envelope in his jacket, and the treads were worn hollow in the middle, and the rail had worked loose at the top bracket again. Boxes stood along the wall and under the window that looked down Main Street to the lake, each one marked THE PINES in the green hand she had kept her ledgers in. The oven was on, and there was a ham in it.

He laid the envelope beside her cup before he sat.

"I'd like you to let me out of that paper."

Arlene didn't touch it. She reached over and turned the scanner down a notch, which was as near as she came to giving a man all of her attention.

"Tell me what it is."

"A mutual release. One clause." He pulled out the chair across from her and sat. "A lawyer in Laconia drew it Monday, and the bill came to me. Have your own lawyer read it before you sign anything."

"You had a lawyer draw it before you asked me."

"Seemed a poor way to ask, with nothing in my hand."

She looked at the envelope the way she'd look at a tax bill, as a thing already in the house.

He had said it himself a week ago, at his own counter with his son ten feet off: what his no was worth, coming from the man who bought if Ellen missed. On March first the store went to Danny, and the two were one paper now, and he would not hand his son the keys as the man the end stool had described over the cake.

The scanner spoke up about a chimney fire out past the Pelletiers'.

"They never clean it," Arlene said.

"Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain."

She got up. A box stood open on the chair by the window, and she folded its flaps down one over the other and drew the tape across with her thumb riding behind it.

"If she misses and there's no sale on the first of March, The Pines gives my place to the next woman, and I go back on the list at eighty-four."

She tore the tape on the dispenser's teeth. Ray let that go by without a word.

"I get my money months from now, after I've foreclosed on that woman."

She ran the side of her hand down the seam, the whole length of it, and pressed the corners in.

"And the man from Concord buys the lot for the land under it."

She gave all three the way she gave the price of oil, and she wrote nothing on the box. It already said where it was going.

She came back and sat down across from him.

"I've paid every man in this town on the day I said I would. I mean to walk into that place on the first of March with my good hat on, owing nobody. And since you've come up my stairs with a lawyer, you can have the rest of it."

He hadn't asked for the rest of it.

"It sat listed two years, Raymond. You came in eight days after she did. I'd signed with her by then, and I'd have signed with her anyway. You'd have kept it a museum. I wanted it to live."

He could see the drive up Kettle Road from where he sat, his offer folded on the truck seat and the office light already on up the hill. A month ago he'd have argued it. This morning he let her have it.

"In nineteen seventy-one I held Normand to a building he never wanted, and he gave it eighteen years. I won't hold a man to a paper he's asked out of."

She took a pen out of the jar by the scanner and laid it on the envelope.

He flattened the release on the oilcloth and signed the first line with her pen, fast and plain, and didn't read it over. He'd read it four times in the truck.

Arlene watched him do it.

"Raymond, you've signed that like a man buying a boat."

She turned the paper to the window and read his name, and the two blank lines under it, and gave nothing away.

"Voyons donc. Bring me her signature. It's hers to risk as much as mine. I sign last."

She folded the release along its creases and put it back in the envelope, and put the envelope in his hand, and kept her own hand on the outside of it a second. Then she was up with the oven door open and the ham out on the board, carving, and she set a plate in front of him without asking whether he'd eaten, and went back for the mustard.

\* \* \*

Danny had a carton of ice cleats open on the counter and the price gun in his right hand, and he was pricing them a pair at a time, click and click.

"You're late," he told the carton.

"I was at Arlene's."

The Bench had its three stools by the front window and its free coffee, and the man on the end stool was blowing across his cup. Nobody called anything.

Ray took the second envelope out of his jacket, from the same office in Laconia, and laid it on the counter in front of his son: the store, Danny's, on March first. Danny set the gun down to read, and Ray picked it up because it was there, and thumbed the label roll to see how much was left on it. Plenty.

Danny read the top page through. He had a pen in his shirt pocket, and he left it there.

"Is this you giving me the store, or you leaving the store? Because those are different invoices."

"I signed my half of getting out this morning."

Danny looked at him a while. Then he opened the drawer under the register and took out a form Ray knew, a credit-union form gone soft along its fold, and laid it beside the envelope with his own name on the last line.

"Grandpa put this store up once, in 'seventy-four, and you swore you'd never ask it twice. I signed this before you could ask."

It had lain under Ray's hand at five o'clock that Thursday in December, and neither of them had said one word about it since.

"I signed that so you could buy it on her yes. You're standing in her paper waiting on her no, and I won't put my name to that. Half out's not out."

He squared the two papers side by side with one finger.

"Get all the way out of her paper and I'll sign this one."

He reached over and took the price gun out of his father's hand, easy, the way he'd take a box cutter off a new clerk.

"It's said Son on that sign since before I was born. I'd like it to mean me."

Ray had nothing ready for that, and Danny didn't wait on him.

"You're allowed to want something that isn't on a shelf."

By the window the end stool held its cup and called nothing, for once. All three of them had heard every word, and the envelope would sit on this counter in front of them every morning until somebody signed it.

The bell went over the door, and a man in a plow jacket came in stamping, wanting something. Danny put the price gun in his back pocket and went down the aisle to him, "Morning," and left both papers where they lay.

\* \* \*

Ellen sat on the stool behind the counter putting the open bowlers' pencils back into the cigar box, point down, one at a time. Nine lanes were dark. Over lane four one light still burned on a man in a Legion jacket throwing the last box of his last string, and behind the snack bar the grill ticked as it cooled.

"Mr. Gendron has one box left," she said. "Then I am closed."

"I'll wait on him."

He set the paper sack on the counter. "You left these."

She took the bulbs first. She put the sack on the shelf under the register and went back to the pencils.

He laid the release beside the cigar box, his name on the first line and the two lines under it blank, and turned it round to face her.

"Sign the release."

Another pencil went into the box, and another.

"Tell me what it's for."

"So that when I tell you no, it's only a no, and nobody in this town can say I'm waiting on your building."

The pencils were done. She shut the lid on them.

"Danny won't take the store while I'm behind you," he said. "Arlene signs last. After you."

"Arlene has seen it."

"This morning. She gave it back to me and fed me ham."

She put on her glasses from the cord and read it. There wasn't much of it to read. He watched her eyes go down the one clause, and back up to the top, and down it again.

"This is the cost you meant."

"It's the half of it I could sign."

Down on four the last ball went in, and Mr. Gendron said something to his pins and went for his boots. Ellen waited until the vestibule door had shut behind him. Then she got up off the stool, and Ray knew from that what was coming.

"That paper is Arlene's bed. It's the house Kate let me spend, too. Without you behind me, a miss is a bank sale. You want me to sign it so your no feels clean."

She had it exactly. The last sentence was the true one.

"Then I keep the paper and write you an option to buy it back off me." He had worked it out in the truck. "Any day you're ready, same price, in writing."

"Then you'd be my landlord till I could afford you."

The grill ticked. He looked for the hole in it, and there wasn't one.

"You're right," he said.

"So are you."

"What would get your name on it?"

"A winter I was winning."

She had two fingers on the near corner of the release. He had the far edge, and he had never let go of it. Neither of them pulled, and neither let go, and the paper lay between their hands with his name on it and nothing under his name. There was pencil-lead along the side of her first knuckle, a gray smudge from a few dozen pencils, and her nails were cut short and square, and the cord of her glasses had swung forward and lay across the back of her wrist. Out in the lot Mr. Gendron's truck started, and its lights went across the glass block and away. The light over four burned on above an empty lane. He could have reached the smudge with his thumb without lifting his hand off the paper. He knew to the inch how far it was.

He took his hand off it.

"It'll keep."

He left it on her counter and went out through the vestibule. He had his hand on the door when he heard, from the office behind the counter, the steel desk drawer open and shut.

\* \* \*

Ray counted his hand out loud and pegged it. "That's me ahead, Hollis. First time in eleven winters."

The propane heater hissed by the door. It was warm at head height and as cold as the lake at the ankles, and the two holes in the floor had skinned over since first light. The board sat between their knees on an upturned bucket.

Hollis gathered the cards. "You courting her, or just fixing her machines a lot?"

"Her machines are some old, and they want a lot of fixing."

Hollis shuffled, squared the deck on his knee, and didn't deal.

"You courting her, or fixing her machines?"

Ray kept his eyes on the deck.

"I had my blade up, Ray."

"I'm courting her." It came out plainer than he'd sent it, and he let it stand. "Supper's done by six, and I'm in bed by half past nine, and that's the better part of four hours with nobody in them. I meant to spend them up at Arlene's, on her machines. Now I'd like to spend them where she is."

Hollis wrote nothing down and said nothing for a second, which from Hollis was a speech.

"So say yes, or get out of the paper."

"Signed my half. She won't sign hers."

His name had been in her desk a week to the day. Hollis dealt. Ray studied the board.

"Did I say I'm ahead?"

"Twice."

Somebody knocked on the orange door. Hollis opened it on Ellen, who had walked out from the access along his trail, and before he said good morning he gave her the ice.

"Ten inches of black ice under three of white. That's walking ice. You walked it."

She came in and stood. There was nowhere else to do it, and she'd have stood anyway.

"I'd like the derby's Sunday. An Open at Kettle Lanes, anyone who pays may enter, with a new cup and nobody's name on it. The league's Saturday stays the way Linda bowled it."

She didn't look at Ray to see whether he'd stop her. She was keeping his no for him, in front of him, and he sat on the cooler and watched her do it.

In the little window two flags had sprung.

"Got a license?" said Hollis, twenty-eight years a conservation officer and still one in the face.

"I bought it yesterday."

"Plan if the ice goes?"

"Page two." She handed him the paper, and he turned to it and read it to himself.

"Stand by," Ellen said, and was out the door.

Ray stood in it after her. She was on her knees at the first tip-up taking the line in hand over hand, both gloves in the water to the wrist, and she brought a pickerel up onto the snow. When she came back in, her gloves were black with wet. He saw that and said nothing yet.

Hollis folded page two.

"Kids' weigh-in and the raffle want a warm room Sunday. Gets the families off the ice by noon." He put the paper inside his coat. "Twenty-six derbies. I'd like one where nobody goes through and nobody calls me after. That's my yes. You'll want Judy's."

So the derby's indoor day would come to her on Sunday, for a venue fee. Ellen nodded once.

"People come in off this ice for Linda's name. They bowled against her. Gil Arsenault'd drive over from Pine Ridge in Halsey Falls for it. She beat him in the 'ninety-four open, and he still brings it up. Put up a cup with nobody on it and you'll get the ones who were coming anyway."

Six feet by eight, and all three of them had heard it.

"Judy's the other vote out here," Hollis said. "Point side, two tip-ups."

Ellen was out the orange door. Ray was up and after her, and the board stayed on the bucket with his peg ahead.

\* \* \*

"Saturdays I come out here so nobody talks to me about money."

Judy Ostrowski didn't lift her head from the hole. She was crouched at the nearer of her two tip-ups with a smelt in one hand and the bait bucket against her knee, and the sun was up over the point with no warmth in it at all. The snow squeaked under Ray's boots and Ellen's when they stopped.

"Hollis says yes to the derby's indoor day at my place on Sunday. He says it wants yours."

Judy put the hook through the smelt behind the back fin and didn't hurry about it.

"Close, is it?" She let the smelt down into the black water and paid the line after it through her fingers. "Then you know March first goes to committee. I like you. The underwriter has never met you."

"The commitment says subject to committee." Ellen's voice was polite and even and very clear. "I read that as a formality."

It was the first thing Ray had ever watched her read wrong. Her voice got calmer as it went, and she had told him what that calm was, in the dark, with a pin in her hands.

Judy paid down the last of the line, set the spool over the hole, and bent the flag down under its catch.

"For a winter that clears, it is." She stood and knocked the snow off her knees. "You've got my yes for Sunday."

She looked from Ellen to Ray and back again. "Going to check my flags."

"They're both down," Ray said.

"Then it won't take long."

She went off across the ice toward the point with her bucket, squeaking at every step, and didn't turn round.

Ellen's hands hung at her sides in the wet wool. The fingers that showed past the cuffs were white.

Ray pulled his own gloves loose and put them under his arm. He took her left hand and peeled the sodden glove down off it, and it came away heavy as a rag, and he worked his glove on in its place a finger at a time, pressing the leather down into each fork with his thumb.

"You heard Hollis," he said. "And you heard Judy."

She let him have the hand. He did the third finger, and then the little one, which never wanted to go. He hadn't done this for anybody since Danny was small enough to stand at the bus stop on School Street with his hands out.

"A cup with nobody on it gets you the ones who were coming anyway. And the bank gets a vote." He took the right hand. The wet glove came away, and the fingers under it were white to the second knuckle and wouldn't close. "This one's worse. Hold it still. The thumb goes in last, and it goes in hard."

He got the thumb in. He held the gloved hand between his two bare ones a second longer than the job needed, and her fingers moved inside the leather, and he felt them move.

"Are you changing your answer?"

"No."

She took her hands back, in his gloves, and closed them once.

"Then your no stays what it is, and I'll go and ask the people it belongs to."

"It belongs to me."

"It has Linda's name on it. She had friends."
