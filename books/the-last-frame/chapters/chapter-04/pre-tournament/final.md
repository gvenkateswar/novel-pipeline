# Chapter 4: Say Intentions

Ray stood at the foul line of lane one with a house ball in each hand and the lane lit out ahead of him, and behind him the other nine lay dark to the far wall. At a quarter to seven he had let himself in with the front key she'd laid on his counter, the first time he'd used it, and the cut he'd made on his own wheel went over smooth. Passing the trophy case he set one knuckle on the glass over Linda's card and kept walking. The Memorial was the last thing he'd asked her for, and she'd said it stays. On Kettle Road at twenty to seven Hollis's plow had passed him going the other way, and Hollis lifted one finger off the wheel and said nothing, same as Friday.

He had come early so he'd have something in his hands when she walked in.

Down cellar the boiler was still working off the night, knocking twice to a cycle like a man wiping his feet, and the maple under his boots held the cold. The glass block across the front of the room had gone from black to blue.

Her car came into the lot at two minutes to seven, and he heard it shut off. The door didn't open. He turned the balls on the return so the maker's marks lined up, and then turned them back, and four minutes went by before the car door went and the front door after it.

She came in at two minutes past seven with a coffee and a clipboard and her gloves already in her vest. From the foul line he could read the top sheet: ten lanes down the side in her printing, and three parties blocked across them from ten o'clock on, every hour ruled off to the end of the night.

"Boiler's catching up," he said.

"It's fifty-four degrees in here," she said, at the same time.

They both stopped.

"She'll be at sixty by eight," Ray said. "She's slow the first hour after a cold night, and you'll want to leave her be."

"I have not touched it." She set the coffee on the scorer's table. "Hollis has done the lot."

"Some cold out there this morning."

"It was nine degrees at the camp when I left."

"I'd have said ten."

"Then it is ten here. The camp is on the water."

Neither of them had said a word about Friday. They had been through the boiler and the plow and the weather, and the next thing up was the lot again, and he could see her see it coming.

He took a slip from his jacket and laid it under her clip. It was off the pad by the store register, in his writing: lane seven, the second of the month, at the Worcester man's rate.

She read it twice. "This is the Worcester rate."

"And every call after, the same. Danny's after me to keep the books straight."

"Danny keeps very straight books," she said.

He wasn't having her owe him, not after Friday.

"I'll pay it Monday. It is already seventeen days late." She slid it in behind the schedule and let the clip down on it. "I start at seven. You have until a quarter past."

He held out a ball.

She took it in her bare right hand, a grapefruit of a thing, small and heavier than it looked, and he watched her find that out again.

"It's the wrist," he said. "You come over the top like you're dealing cards. Turn it under, so. Lower it six inches more than you think, and let the lane have it."

"Let it have what, exactly."

"The rest of it. Lane's been at this since 'fifty-eight, and she knows her business."

He stepped in behind her right shoulder, put his hand over her wrist, and turned it. The small bones went over under his thumb, and her coffee smelled better than anything the Bench had ever poured him. He could have stood there holding her wrist turned the right way, with the glass block going gray and the boiler ticking under the floor and nobody in the building to call it, until the parties came at ten, and after.

"It is nine minutes past seven," she said, and didn't move.

He let go first.

The hand went into his jacket pocket, where her key was.

She threw. It was the best ball he'd seen out of her, low and turning, and it took the front of the pins and scattered wood across the deck, and when the noise quit four stood up behind it.

"Reset it," she said.

"Machine won't, in the middle of a box."

"Then I'll start over from the first ball."

"The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."

She looked down the lane a long second at the wood lying where it had gone and the four pins standing behind it, with the sun coming gold through the glass block at her back. Then she took the second ball off the return, turned her own wrist under the way he had turned it, with his hand nowhere near, lowered it, and let go, and the ball went off down lane one, low and turning, toward the wood.

\* \* \*

The bell went over the door at five to nine, and Normie was ready for it.

"Lot out at the alley was empty by eight Friday, boys, oldest first, just the way she called it," he said into his cup, "and the last truck still sitting in it was the veteran's. Let's go to the replay. First light, under the sign, the owner kisses the veteran, Hollis and the whole house watching, and the veteran is seen to kiss back. And this morning his was the first truck in the lot. Danny opened at six, folks. Forty years, and the veteran's been beat to his own front door."

Ray went past the three stools and the pot and down behind the counter and through to the back without answering any of it.

The back room smelled of kerosene and cut brass. The desk had sat under a box of strap hinges since before Danny was born, and under the hinges, since Friday's mail, was the letter from the bank in Concord. Danny had laid it there. Ray had read the return address and set the hinges back down on top of it.

He opened it standing up, with his back to the heater. It was one page, and the date on it that counted was the thirty-first.

He had opened this store at six every morning since he was twenty-one, near enough, and at six this morning he had been in his own kitchen shaving, to go stand on somebody else's lane with a ball in each hand. He couldn't say he'd given the store a thought until Normie said it.

Danny came in behind him with a carton of furnace filters he had no call to be carrying and stopped in the doorway with it.

The Concord bank kept Saturday hours, and at nine Ray dialed the number off the letterhead without sitting down.

"Still the bowling alley on Kettle Road?" the loan officer said, once he had the file up.

"Same building. Last I looked she hadn't moved. And I'd guess you'll still lend me what the appraiser says she's worth."

"Appraised value, Mr. Gagnon, and not a dollar over. The commitment's good through the thirty-first."

"And after the thirty-first?"

"It lapses. If you walked in here March first you'd have nothing to buy with, and we'd be starting you over from the beginning."

"What's it take to keep it good to March first?"

"There's an extension fee. You pay it whether you close or not."

The officer named the figure. Ray heard it out and asked the one he knew the answer to. "And if she makes her number?"

"Then you've paid us to hold a loan you never use."

Ray looked out past Danny at the front of the store, the roof rakes hanging from the ceiling and the pallet of salt by the door, all of it where it had always been.

"Keep it," Ray said. "Check'll be in the mail today."

He hung up and pulled open the drawer where the store's checkbook lived, the long one with the stubs, and he had his hand on it. He shut the drawer. He took his own checkbook out of the inside pocket of his jacket, set his boot up on a carton of stove bolts, wrote it out on his knee, tore it off, and folded it into the letter.

Danny watched the whole of it from the doorway with the filters in his arms.

"If she makes it, Dad," he said, "what've you bought?"

\* \* \*

"Kettle Maintenance, Kettle Counter. Lane four is setting nine. The six pin is missing."

Her voice came out of the radio on his belt as if she were sitting on the kickback beside him, low and level over all ten machines. She had bought two radios for the day, one for the counter and one for the pit, and named them herself, and since any call to him today would go on a slip, he wore his.

The warmth came off the motors and filled the pit, and through the gap under the masking went feet in rented shoes.

He unclipped the radio and keyed it. "Kettle Counter, this is Kettle Maintenance, roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out."

"Kettle Maintenance, one of those will do. Ten-four is the state police. Over and out asks me to answer you and tells me not to."

"Kettle Counter, roger, I've got all that, over."

"Read it back."

He took his time over it. "Lane nine is setting four, and the four pin's gone missing, and the counter would like it found, over."

"Lane four is setting nine, and it is missing the six."

"That's what I said, near enough."

"You said it in another order."

He was grinning at a radio in the half-dark.

He'd had four by ear already, under the other nine: her elevator coming round with a hitch in it every turn, where a slot that ought to be carrying a pin came up empty and caught. He went along the catwalk and found the six jammed crossways where she'd been chewing on it since the last box. He worked it loose with two fingers and set it in its slot. Four took it, and swept, and set ten.

"Kettle Counter, four's setting ten," he said. "She'd like it known she was provoked."

The radio clicked, and he got the counter before he got her: the register drawer, the fryer basket going in, and Denise, close enough to the microphone to be standing on it.

"—and those shoes have been on better feet than yours, sweetheart, they've been on the Legion and the whole Highway crew, all right, bigger feet, and every pair sprayed toe and heel, so what you're smelling is the spray, and if you'd like shoes that smell like you, you can bowl in the ones you came in and explain the marks on my lanes to the owner, and you're twelve, and your father bowls a ninety-one, did you know that, no, I didn't think so, and he's never said one word about those shoes—"

The click cut her off, and the pit had only the machines again.

At five past five the radio said, "Kettle Maintenance, say intentions."

Ray looked at his watch, which said ten minutes to supper, and keyed the radio. "Intentions are to quit."

The radio stayed open a second, with the counter's noise behind it.

"Unable."

He clipped it back on his belt and sat down with his back to the kickback, where the motors had warmed the steel, and he stayed put.

The minute hand on his watch came round to the three. In thirty years the clock in his kitchen had watched the pot go on late once, Friday, when he slept through the afternoon after her storm night, and now it was watching a cold stove, and he was in her pit. He had always kept time by what broke: the winter the K went, the winter nine's belt went the first time, the two pinsetters after Linda. Nothing had broken since the six pin. He sat with ten machines running clean in front of him and listened for one of them to go, and none of them went.

Her voice came once more, on the PA this time, calling the six o'clock party up to the counter for shoes. She had the radio keyed somewhere near the microphone, and he got her twice: close, at his hip, and a half-second after from the whole building, over ten lanes and down through the masking to him.

Then the radio went quiet on his belt, and under the masking all ten lanes ran bright and backward toward him, ten balls coming out of the light and the wood going down across the whole width of the house at once.

\* \* \*

Ray had the scraper going on the windshield when she came down the drive. His truck sat on the shoulder under the sign, where he'd left it at noon when the party cars filled the lot, and the sign was lit over it, the red script and the white candlepin and the first letter dark, and the red lay on the hood and on every strip of frost he took off the glass.

She had one sheet of paper in her bare hand, and she came down the middle of the drive as she had come down his center aisle, with something to tell him and an order to tell it in.

"I paid Concord this morning," he said, before she could start, "to keep my loan good to March first."

She stopped at the bottom of the drive, a little uphill of him, with the paper at her side.

"Tell me why."

He took another long pull at the frost. "Thing is, if I'd let it go at the end of the month and you came up short, that contract couldn't close, because there'd be nobody on the other end of it with money to close it. So Arlene would have to take the place back the long way—"

"Foreclose."

"—through the lawyers, and wait months to be paid, and The Pines won't wait months. And when that's all done, the condo man gets the lot for the dirt under it, and the maple goes out in a dumpster." He knocked the scraper against his boot. "If somebody's going to be standing at the bottom of your drive on March first, I'd sooner it was me."

He was standing at the bottom of her drive when he said it, and neither of them remarked on it.

"Then you're my buyer," she said.

"That's what Arlene asked me to be."

"Then as my buyer you'll want to know what your league costs me." She said it level, the way she'd told him the time from the far end of lane seven. "Seven hundred dollars every week it bowls."

"Always has," Ray said. "Arlene carried it."

"Arlene had you for nothing."

He let that go by. She had it right, and there was a slip in her clipboard now that said so.

She held out the sheet. He took it and tipped it to the red light. A notice for the vestibule board, in her printing: the league's name at the top, hers at the bottom, and the new lineage in between, starting Tuesday.

"It goes on the board tomorrow," she said. "I wanted you to hear it from me first."

"Lot of that going around tonight."

"Two cases."

He read it again, all the way down, and handed it back.

"Read paragraph four, Mrs. Daly. Take your time. It's short, and it's been sitting in your desk since September."

Every September Arlene had signed a season agreement with the league, and this one had come to her at closing with the ledgers and the keys, and paragraph four held the rate where it stood until the January meeting voted.

She didn't ask him what it said. He watched her hands. She folded the notice in half and ran a thumbnail down the crease and folded it in half again, and squared the edges against each other until they met.

"At the new rate I make my number," she said. "At the old one I do not. I will put it to the January meeting, and the notice waits for it."

"Then I'll be standing up against it." He went back to the windshield. "The Highway crew's on twelve-hour shifts all winter, and the Legion's living on what the government sends them, and they can't pay it, and they'll go where they can. You ought to hear it from me tonight, before you read it in Verna's minutes."

He stopped scraping. "I eat supper at a quarter past five, have for thirty years, and you've made me late twice this week, and I can't tell if you're doing it on purpose or you're just no good with a clock."

"I'm very good with a clock."

She stood still in the red light, as steady as a machine somebody has set up right, and he had been around enough machines to know that was when you listened hardest.

He held out his hand for the notice, and she gave it to him, and for a second they both had hold of it, her bare fingers on one folded edge and his on the other. He didn't open it, and he gave it back.

He looked up at the dead K. "I could get that lit for you. Wouldn't take me a Saturday."

"No. I hire out what I am not qualified for, and so should you. You are a pinsetter man. That is a neon tube."

He looked at the dark letter a while, and couldn't find a thing wrong with that. "There's a shop in Laconia bends them—"

"Then it is a sign shop's job, and it is not on my list this winter."

Ray put the scraper on the seat. "I'll be on my feet in January, Ellen."

"Good. I'd hate to beat somebody who wasn't ready."

He had waited at the truck braced for her to be hurt, and he looked for it in her face under the sign, and with the red on everything he couldn't tell whether it was there.
