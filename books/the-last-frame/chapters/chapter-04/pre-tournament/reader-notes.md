# Reader notes: Chapter 4, "Say Intentions"

**Draft read:** final.md, Oct 28 late (chapters 01 to 04 read in order, nothing else opened)

## 1. Pull score: 4 / 10

This is the chapter after the first kiss, and it spends that kiss on weather small talk, a bank call and a rate dispute that only exists because the most careful woman in the book never read a contract in her own desk. The radio stretch is the one place I leaned in. The closing fight points at a January meeting weeks away, so nothing is pressing on me. A reader with other books on her phone could put this down here and not miss it by Tuesday.

**"I'd keep reading, but..." warning:** I would read Chapter 5 only on the strength of Chapter 3. This chapter didn't earn the next one on its own.

## 2. Where I got bored

- **Opening scene, the small talk (lines 13 to 31).** "Some cold out there this morning." / "It was nine degrees at the camp when I left." / "I'd have said ten." I understand that they are avoiding Friday on purpose. But the avoidance is all I get, and then the narrator explains it: "Neither of them had said a word about Friday. They had been through the boiler and the plow and the weather, and the next thing up was the lot again." I already got the joke. Once it's spelled out it's no longer a joke, just a delay. I came in wanting the morning after, and the book gave me the thermostat.
- **The bank call (lines 91 to 113).** A commitment letter, a lapse date, an extension fee whose figure is hidden from me, "Appraised value, Mr. Gagnon, and not a dollar over." This is paperwork read aloud. By now there are three lenders in the book (credit union, Granite Lakes, the Concord bank) and I no longer care which is which. Danny's closing line ("what've you bought?") is good, but I had to sit through a loan servicing call to reach it.
- **The pit afternoon reverie (line 167).** "In thirty years the clock in his kitchen had watched the pot go on late once, Friday... He had always kept time by what broke." My eyes slid. It's pretty, but it's the writer musing, and nothing happens in it.
- **Windshield, the foreclosure explanation (lines 185 to 189).** "So Arlene would have to take the place back the long way— / Foreclose. / —through the lawyers, and wait months to be paid, and The Pines won't wait months. And when that's all done, the condo man gets the lot..." It's a legal flowchart delivered as dialogue in the middle of the one scene with real conflict.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **"The camp."** "It was nine degrees at the camp when I left." What camp? Up to now I didn't know where Ellen lives. If you're not from New England, "camp" sounds like a summer camp or a campground. Then "The camp is on the water." I'm still not sure.
- **Which loan is this?** In Chapter 2 Ray's credit union committee was going to lend against the store so he could buy now. Here a Concord bank holds a "commitment" for the March first purchase that lapses "the thirty-first." Is that a second loan? Did it exist all along? Thirty-first of which month? I worked out that it's probably December and today is about the 19th, but only by counting "seventeen days late" back to lane seven.
- **"since any call to him today would go on a slip, he wore his."** I read it three times. Is he wearing the radio because she's paying him now? Because he wants every call on the record? The logic doesn't land.
- **Denise on the radio (line 153).** It opens mid-sentence with a dash. For a beat I didn't know whose voice it was or why it was coming through, until "sweetheart" and the shoes.
- **What the ending means.** "He had waited at the truck braced for her to be hurt." Hurt by what: that he's still her buyer, or that he'll oppose her in January? I think the first, but it's muddy.
- **Where's the time?** Saturday morning she's at seven. "Danny opened at six" and he's at the store at nine. His truck was left on the shoulder "at noon," then he's in her pit until after five. I followed it, but only with effort.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **Paragraph four.** This is the big one. Ellen is the woman who read the candlepin rules before throwing a ball, labeled every shelf of the ball rack on day one, and keeps a legal pad of the fuse drawer and the urn's habits. The season agreement "had come to her at closing with the ledgers and the keys," and she never read the paragraph that sets her biggest weekly loss? And she drafts a public price-change notice without checking it? The whole closing conflict rests on her missing a short paragraph in a document in her own desk. It's convenient for the plot and it goes against everything the book has shown me about her.
- **She carries the notice to Ray before posting it, at night, to his truck.** "I wanted you to hear it from me first" is in character. But she walks down to him one sheet in hand with "an order to tell it in," and it never occurs to her that the league coach would know the league contract. That repeats the same blind spot.
- **Ray switches to charging her.** "He wasn't having her owe him, not after Friday." I can half see it (he doesn't want money between them now that they've kissed), but the line tells me rather than showing me, and it's the reverse of the Chapter 2 gesture where he kept the invoice and refused the money. I needed one more beat for the flip.
- **Nobody mentions the kiss, all day.** Two adults who kissed in front of a whole building at dawn spend a full day side by side, alone in an empty alley at seven a.m., and neither says a word about it. Ray's own logic wouldn't produce that: he came early "so he'd have something in his hands." All right, but he's had a day and a half to think. It reads as the book holding the conversation back, not as the characters.
- **The extension fee from his personal checkbook.** This one I believed, and it's good. Reaching for the store checkbook and switching to his own follows from the Chapter 2 vow about putting the store up only once.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **Explaining after the gesture:**
  - "Neither of them had said a word about Friday. They had been through the boiler and the plow and the weather, and the next thing up was the lot again, and he could see her see it coming."
  - "He wasn't having her owe him, not after Friday."
  - "He let that go by. She had it right, and there was a slip in her clipboard now that said so."
  - "He was standing at the bottom of her drive when he said it, and neither of them remarked on it." The narrator is nudging me toward the irony.
- **Recap at the top:** "The Memorial was the last thing he'd asked her for, and she'd said it stays." It reads as a reminder for a reader who wasn't paying attention. Normie's "Let's go to the replay" recaps the kiss too. That one is funny, but it's still the second recap in two pages.
- **Details reused until they're tics:**
  - The sign. "the red script and the white candlepin and the first letter dark." That's the third time across four chapters, nearly word for word, plus "the dead K" again.
  - The knuckle on the trophy-case glass (Chapter 1, again here).
  - "with his back to the kickback, where the motors had warmed the steel" (Chapter 3, again here).
  - Hollis "said nothing, same as Friday." The Hollis-says-nothing joke is on its third outing.
  - "for a second they both had hold of it." The ball in Chapter 1, the notice here. Same staging.
  - Ellen's "came down the drive as she had come down his center aisle." The book is quoting itself.
- **Aphorism endings:**
  - "as steady as a machine somebody has set up right, and he had been around enough machines to know that was when you listened hardest."
  - "The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left." It's a lovely line, but it has the theme on its sleeve.
  - Danny's "If she makes it, Dad, what've you bought?" The scene ends on a button line again.
- **Rhythm:** paragraph after paragraph of "and... and... and" chains. Line 3 alone has five clauses and three time stamps. Four chapters in, every page has the same gait.
- **Voices** are not the problem. Ellen (no contractions, numbered items, "That is a neon tube"), Ray ("Thing is," "fella," "some cold"), Denise (breathless run-on) and Normie (play-by-play) are all distinct. The trouble is the narration around them.

## 6. What I think is going on

The page has told me: Ellen needs the winter to clear thirty thousand dollars, with all of it still in the account, or Ray buys the alley at appraisal on March first. Ray has now paid out of his own pocket to keep that buy alive, because otherwise Arlene gets foreclosure delays and loses her place at The Pines. The league costs Ellen $700 a week. She can only make her number by raising the rate, and the season agreement blocks that until a January meeting where Ray will speak against her. In the storm she promised the Memorial "stays... unless the numbers say otherwise," Ray missed the condition under Denise's strike, and she chose not to repeat it. That's the loaded gun. I predict the January vote goes against her, the numbers come up short in February, and she has to cut, charge for or move Linda's Memorial (last Saturday in February). Ray will hear it as a betrayal of a yes he thought he got, and that's the black moment. Locker fourteen holds Linda's ball and shoes, and it gets opened at the Memorial. Ellen's call with Kate ("The house. Not the rest. I swear.") says she has put more into this than she told her daughter, probably her retirement, and it will come out. Ending: the town or the league closes the gap (the Highway crew, the Legion, the pool sheet money, Denise's cup), Ray never uses his loan, and either he becomes her partner or the extension fee is the thing he "bought": the right to stand at the bottom of her drive. A sign shop lights the K in the last chapter.

## 7. The listener pass

- **Opening paragraph timestamps:** "a quarter to seven... twenty to seven... two minutes to seven... four minutes... two minutes past seven... nine minutes past seven... a quarter past." Seven times in the first page. They're told out of order (he let himself in at 6:45, but passed the plow at 6:40, mentioned later). By ear it's mush.
- **The readback joke:** "Lane four is setting nine. The six pin is missing." / "Lane nine is setting four, and the four pin's gone missing" / "Lane four is setting nine, and it is missing the six." On the page I can see the numbers swap. Through a voice it's four, nine, six, nine, four, four, four, nine, six. The joke depends on the listener holding three numbers in order, and most won't. They'll hear the rhythm of a joke without catching it.
- **"Kettle Counter, this is Kettle Maintenance, roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out."** This works aloud. It's the best audio moment in the chapter.
- **Call signs:** "Kettle Maintenance" and "Kettle Counter" sound nearly alike in a flat voice. In a run of untagged radio lines I had to rely on content, not names, to know who was speaking.
- **Denise's radio rant** opens on an em dash, mid-sentence. A TTS voice gives no cue that we've switched to a radio feed. It will just sound like Denise suddenly in the pit.
- **Numbers read in full:** "fifty-four degrees," "nine degrees," "ten," "seventeen days late," "seven hundred dollars every week," "since 'fifty-eight," "the thirty-first," "March first," "ninety-one." Not terrible on their own, but this book is now dense with numbers and they all sound alike.
- **Untagged exchanges** at the windshield are fine because the voices differ, but "Lot of that going around tonight." / "Two cases." took me a second by ear to place.
- **Sentence shape:** long "and"-chained sentences followed by a short stopper ("He let go first." "He held out a ball." "They both stopped." "He was grinning at a radio in the half-dark."). Aloud it becomes a pattern of drone, thud, drone, thud.
- **The line 167 reverie** is one I'd skip with my eyes. A voice can't skip it.

## 8. The romance

Yes, in two places, and both are dialogue.

- The wrist: "'It is nine minutes past seven,' she said, and didn't move. / He let go first." That got me. Her telling the time, the thing she does to stay in order, while not moving.
- The radio. "Ten-four is the state police. Over and out asks me to answer you and tells me not to." I laughed. "She'd like it known she was provoked." Laughed again. And then "Kettle Maintenance, say intentions." / "Intentions are to quit." / "Unable." is the chapter.
- "No. I hire out what I am not qualified for, and so should you. You are a pinsetter man. That is a neon tube." Laughed.
- "Good. I'd hate to beat somebody who wasn't ready." It has spark, but it's also the third quip-close in the scene.

What I didn't get: anything about the kiss from either of them. Thirty-some hours later, no look, no reference, no awkwardness. The romance runs entirely through banter, and that makes the kiss feel like it didn't happen.

## 9. What I want to know next

- Will they ever talk about Friday? This is the strongest question, and the chapter made it weaker by stalling.
- Does Ellen find out the extension came out of Ray's own checkbook, not the store's?
- What is "the rest" that Ellen swore to Kate she didn't put in?
- What's in locker fourteen?
- What's her third item from the pit?

None of these is urgent. The next marked event is a league meeting in January, which isn't something I'd stay up for. The chapter's only new tension (the rate fight) is slow and procedural. Honestly, what pulls me now is Chapter 3's leftover goodwill, not anything Chapter 4 planted.
