# Reader Notes: Chapter 4, "Say Intentions"

**Draft read:** `chapters/chapter-04/final.md` (Oct 30, 2,951 words). For context I read `chapter-01/final.md` through `chapter-03/final.md` first. I read nothing else in the folder.

---

## 1. Pull score: 4 / 10

I'd keep reading, but only on the leftovers from Chapters 1 to 3 (Kate and "the house," the third item, locker fourteen). This chapter adds no new question of its own. Chapter 3 ended on a secret and a kiss. Chapter 4 settles the secret in the first sixty lines, with no cost and no fight, and spends the rest on four pleasant vignettes: the lesson, the radio, the cake, the sign. Normie's line is the one turn. It tells Ray something I have known since page one (he buys the building if she fails). By the end my thumb was moving faster than my eyes.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **Line 47 and line 51.** "This one took me thirty hours and nineteen minutes." Then: "That was at eleven last night. I could have phoned you then." What was at eleven last night? The thirty hours run from the pit (about midnight Thursday) to now (about seven Saturday morning), so eleven last night isn't the end of that count. I read it three times and still can't say what the eleven o'clock moment was. For a character defined by precision, the numbers don't add up for me.
- **Line 9.** "Yesterday at your counter you told the Bench Linda's Memorial stays." The event the whole first scene turns on (Ray vouching publicly for her) happened offstage between chapters. I had to rebuild it from her accusation. I didn't see him do the thing I'm being asked to feel bad about.
- **Line 61.** "Then hear mine. It stays." What stays? The Memorial? His word to the Bench? Him? Line 63 suggests all three, but on first pass I couldn't tell whether he was forgiving her, holding her to it, or refusing to back down in front of the town.
- **Line 165.** "Any no he said to her between here and March would be a no that paid him." A no to what? To her using the Memorial? To billing? To a loan? I can guess, but the line that's supposed to be the chapter's turn is cryptic.
- **Line 3.** "the key that was laid on his counter on the third." I had to work out that "the third" is the date of the clock scene in Chapter 2. I'd lost the calendar. I also didn't know Chapter 3 was two weeks later until Ray said so there.
- **Line 149.** "Lucien" appears with no tag. I worked out he's Ray's father from "which his father had never managed," two sentences later.
- **Who is speaking at line 73.** "Two boxes. Winds calm. One ball in the gutter." / "I was here." By voice I think Ellen gives the report and Ray answers. On the page there's no tag, and the paragraph before it is from Ray's side.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **The obvious safer option for Ray is never closed.** Normie points out that Ray profits if she fails. Ray's whole problem in the back half is that every favor or refusal now looks self-interested. He signed the backstop because Arlene asked him to. The clean fix is one sentence to Arlene or to Ellen: "I'm out. Find another backer," or "I won't exercise it." The page never raises it and never says why he can't (is it legally binding? would Arlene lose her place at the Pines?). Without that, his silence in the cake scene ("He didn't say any of it") reads as the book holding its conflict open, not as something he can't do.
- **Ellen's billing fix works against her own goal.** She needs every dollar to clear thirty thousand and keep it in the account, and she volunteers to pay Ray for everything he used to do free. The page knows this ("Every dollar you pay me comes off the winter your bank is counting" / "If I miss, I'll miss by an honest bill"), and it is in character. But she's a planner who listed fuses on a legal pad, and she doesn't stop to work out the number. I'd believe it more if she named a figure and chose to pay it anyway.
- **Bare hands in the snow, again.** Chapter 3: gloves in her vest, knuckles red and then white, he warms them. Chapter 4 (lines 175, 197, 221): no coat, bare-handed, "Her knuckles were raw with the cold, and nobody had told her," and he tells her to put her hand away. A woman who runs her life by checklist forgets her gloves in a New Hampshire December twice in two chapters so the hero can notice. It feels arranged.
- **The overheard line is convenient.** The building has ten lanes of birthday parties, and Ellen happens to be standing ten feet behind the settee, holding the cake knife, at the second Normie says it.
- **The settled fight costs nothing.** She hid a condition, kissed him on it, and let him vouch on it in front of the whole town. He says "It stays," then offers a bowling lesson. I believe Ray is a forgiving man. I don't believe it costs him nothing in front of the Bench, and the page skips how he'll handle the town now.

  - "since the winter Danny got his license" (line 183; verbatim from Ch 2)
  - "red script and the white candlepin and the dead K" (line 173; the sign is described this way in every chapter)

- **Personification as a running habit:** "the boiler was going on and off the way she did when she wanted a thing remembered"; "Down cellar the boiler came on and thought better of it"; "the old wood had no intention of warming up for anybody"; "one ball had dropped in the gutter as if it had somewhere else to be"; "the heater running hard for nobody, which it took personally"; "the pot sat on the back of the stove... and it would know"; "I'd guess she's used to it by now, which makes one of us." Seven in one chapter. Each is cute alone. Together they're a tic.
- **The theme said as dialogue**, line 95: "The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left." That's the book's moral, delivered as bowling advice. Then the scene ends on the image of it (line 99).
- **Every scene ends on a button:** "Her second ball was already on the lane..." / "Snack Bar, two baskets for lane six." / "cut the first piece, and handed it to Normie." / "Ms. Daly. Net thirty." Chapters 1–3 did the same ("Ray won't." / "That's the bell." / "Honey."). I can now predict where a scene will stop.

- **A quip in place of a beat:** "He turned the ball over. There was nothing on the bottom of it he hadn't already seen on top."
- **Minor characters share one voice.** Denise's run-on ("I've got a boy at my counter twelve if he's a day... they are going to walk down lane three without him"), Normie's commentary and Ray's "Thing is..." are all the same New England deadpan rhythm. Ellen is the only one who sounds different.

## 6. What I think is going on

Ellen has put her house up for this. In Chapter 2 her daughter (I think) Kate calls, and Ellen says "The house," then "Not the rest," then "I swear." My read is that she mortgaged the house and swore to Kate she hasn't touched her retirement. I think she breaks that swear before March, and Kate's Sunday visit exposes it. The third item she won't brief is either that she came to the lake because she was alone after retirement, or something about Kate. Locker fourteen holds Linda's ball and shoes, maybe a note, and it gets opened for Linda's Memorial. That last Saturday in February is the climax: Ellen will need it as a paid event to clear thirty thousand, and Ray will have to choose between keeping it "the way Linda bowled it" and letting it save her. I predict she comes up short by a small amount. Ray either refuses to exercise his right or turns the Memorial over to her, and the dead K gets lit by the end (Christmas, or the last page). The ending: they run it together, with the money settled by honest bills. I'm committed to the house, the Memorial as the climax and the K being lit. I'm less sure about the shortfall.

**Chapter 5 prediction (about 35% sure):** Sunday. Kate arrives at seven, or Kate's call turns into the plot. Ray's first invoice arrives in "her format." Ellen floats a money-making idea that touches Linda's Memorial, which sets up the first real disagreement between the leads that isn't a misunderstanding. The sign quote comes back.

## 7. The listener pass

- **"She" for machines and for Ellen, back to back.** Line 3: "the boiler was going on and off the way she did," before Ellen enters, so the first "she" I hear is a boiler. Line 111: "she went back to rumbling" (the ball return) right after Ellen's radio line. Line 119: "lane three was thinking something over and hadn't made up her mind. Through all of it her voice came the length of the building." In two sentences "her" goes from lane three to Ellen. Line 183: "She's been dark since..." is the sign, said to Ellen. A listener can't sort these.
- **Quotes inside quotes without marks.** "In the pit I said, It stays. Unless the numbers say otherwise." / "I told her, It's half." A voice reads these as one flat line, and "It's half" is lost.
- **Untagged lines.** "Two boxes. Winds calm. One ball in the gutter." / "I was here." Out loud I can't tell who's talking. The radio block (lines 105–139) mostly works because of call signs. Denise's line works because she's named.
- **Sentences that lose a listener:** line 3 (key, door, third, mat, listened, thirty years, wiped his feet); line 149, the Bench/drawer/Lucien paragraph, where I lost whose drawer it was; line 165, the "two drawers" paragraph, which uses "drawer" as a metaphor right after a paragraph about a literal cash drawer.
- **Repeated words in a short span:** "drawer" (six or more times in lines 149–165); "winter(s)" (about twelve in the chapter: "two winters" three times, "this winter," "the winter the roof rakes," "the winter Danny got his license," "the winter your bank is counting"); "counter"; "Bench"; "stays" and "stayed" (lines 59–63: "Nine lanes stayed dark... It stays... stayed where he was").
- **Run of identical shapes:** Ray's beats in the cake scene are all "He [did X]. He [did Y]." ("He didn't say any of it. He put the knife... cut the first piece, and handed it"). In the opening scene, the narration between lines is mostly short declaratives: "He weighed the small ball in his hands." / "He turned the ball over." / "He held the ball still." / "He held it out across the ball return." That's four ball-handling beats in one scene.
- **Numbers aloud are fine** ("thirty hours and nineteen minutes," "'ninety-six," "five-twenty"). "Roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out" plays well aloud.

## 8. The romance

Yes, in places, and this is the strongest thread in the chapter.

- The lesson: "Is there a next instruction?" / "There was. Give me a minute." This made me want them together. It's the one moment where Ray loses his footing and says so.
- "Wilco means you will comply. I have not asked you for anything yet." I laughed.
- "Say I'm driving by and I happen to stop." / "Then you bill me for the stop." I smiled.
- "Then you'll be paying me, Mrs. Daly." / "Ms. Daly. Net thirty." A good callback to her first word in the book. It's also a neat button, which is the problem from section 5.
- "I kept quiet because the price of telling you was you." It works, but it's the line the whole scene was built to deliver, and I could feel the build.

**Would one honest conversation fix their problem now?** The Chapter 3 problem (she hid the condition) got its honest conversation, in scene 1, so the book didn't drag it out. That's to its credit. What's left is mostly structural, so talk alone won't fix it: Ray gains if she fails, she needs the Memorial if the numbers come up short, and he wants the Memorial left alone. One exception is unclosed. Ray could say "I'll release the backstop" in one sentence, and the page never says why he can't.

**Does the hero want anything besides saying no?** This chapter finally says what he wants: give Danny the store whole, buy the alley and work on his own machines in the evenings, keep Linda's Memorial as it is, light the K by Christmas, and something "to do with the hours after supper" (loneliness, the pot on the stove) that he won't name. On the page, though, he mostly withholds. He puts the heavier ball back, laughs half a second late, "didn't say any of it," cuts the cake, and hands a piece to Normie. His one active push is the sign quote, and she deflects it. The retirement toast is the only thing he starts and finishes.
