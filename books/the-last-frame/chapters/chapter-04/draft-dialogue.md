# Chapter 4: Say Intentions

Only lane one was lit. The other nine ran off into the dark to their pits, and down cellar the boiler was still sore about Thursday, going on and off the way she did when she wanted a thing remembered. At seven o'clock Ray had let himself in at the front on the key that was laid on his counter on the third, the first time it had been in that door, and he had stood on the mat after it turned and listened to the place the way he'd listened to it for thirty years, except that this morning he'd wiped his feet.

He had a house ball in each hand. One was the weight most people took. The other was the lightest on the rack, and he had picked it for her by holding it a while, the way he'd pick a washer out of a bin, and then taken the second one down so it wouldn't look like that. On the drive out he had decided there were two ways to go at this morning, and one of them was to say something about Friday. Somewhere around the town beach he had settled on the other, the lesson he'd owed her since her owner's ball hopped into lane six and took his wood with it, which a man could give a woman in an empty building without either of them bringing anything up.

The office door opened. She came out with a clipboard held flat to her chest and was talking before he had his mouth open for good morning.

"Yesterday at your counter you told the Bench Linda's Memorial stays. You told them on my word. My word had two parts, and you heard one."

It was the voice she'd used on the PA her first night, and on a snowed-in building Thursday night. Her thumb lay on the clip of the board and stayed there.

Ray turned and put the heavier ball back on the rack. It went back into its own slot, among the ones worn smooth, and he squared it there. He kept the light one.

"In the pit I said, It stays. Unless the numbers say otherwise." She gave it to him word for word, with the stop in the same place. "Denise's strike took the second half."

He turned the small ball once in his hands.

"If this winter comes up short, Linda's Memorial is the biggest day I have, and I will want it." She let that stand. "Read it back."

He did it flat, with no tune to it. "It stays unless the numbers say otherwise. If the winter comes up short, you'll want Linda's day."

"Correct." Her thumb moved on the clip and went still. "You gave me no readback, and I let it go."

"You knew." It came out shorter than he'd sent it. "Under the sign?"

"Yes."

Down cellar the boiler came on and thought better of it.

"The town had us by noon yesterday," she said.

"Normie had us before Hollis got his plow back to the shed."

One corner of her mouth went, and she put it back where it belonged.

"Normie told Denise. Denise asked me whether that was what I had said. I told her, It's half."

He could see Friday from here without trying: his own hand flat on the wood by the register, and his own voice telling three old men on three stools what she'd told him, plain, so nobody would ask twice, and Danny down at the far register with a box cutter open in his hand, cutting nothing, hearing his father vouch.

"While it was the two of us, I could carry it," she said. "Then Hollis's light came up the drive. Every face in the building was in the glass block. Denise was in the door. After that you were vouching on it, and I let you."

"That the lot?"

It wasn't. With her there was always an item after.

"Every error I ever made at work, I wrote up inside the hour. This one took me thirty hours and nineteen minutes."

He turned the ball over. There was nothing on the bottom of it he hadn't already seen on top.

"That was at eleven last night. I could have phoned you then. I am counting that too."

She would be. She'd have it on a ruled page somewhere, to the minute, with the total underlined twice.

"Did you kiss me so I wouldn't ask?"

"I kissed you because I wanted to." She kept her eyes on him for the rest, with a whole dark lane beside her she could have used. "I kept quiet because the price of telling you was you."

The boiler shut off. Nine lanes stayed dark.

"Then hear mine." He held the ball still. "It stays."

He had told the Bench, and now he'd told her, and what he'd told the Bench was still so, because he meant to hold it so. A man vouching on nothing but his own word was a thing he'd been before Friday, and nobody had sent him a bill for it yet. The front door was forty feet behind him, and he had a key to it, and the truck was in the lot. He could go. He turned the small ball once round in his hands and stayed where he was.

He held it out across the ball return.

"Lane's open. You turn it loose a foot off the floor and it lands like a dropped plate. Want that fixed?"

\* \* \*

Her third box, and the small ball was already in her bare hands. Her first two boxes had gone the way all her boxes went, up off her fingers and down onto the maple a good yard past the line with a crack that made the head pin flinch, and one ball had dropped in the gutter as if it had somewhere else to be.

"Two boxes. Winds calm. One ball in the gutter."

"I was here."

Ray got down on one knee at the foul line. The boards were cold through his trousers; the heat hadn't come this far yet, and the old wood had no intention of warming up for anybody before the doors opened at ten. He held his right hand out flat, palm down, six inches off the floor.

"Under my hand."

To get the ball under it she had to bend, lower than she cared to, and for once since the first of the month she was the one looking down. Her face was over his, intent, with her mouth set the way it set when she had a laugh and was keeping it. Her bare knuckles went in under his palm and stopped an inch short of it, and whatever he'd meant to tell her next went clean out of his head.

"Is there a next instruction?"

"There was. Give me a minute."

She held the bend and waited on him. He looked down the lane, which helped some.

"Let the lane have it."

She let it go under his hand. It went down the maple low and quiet, hardly a sound out of it until it got where it was going, and took more wood than she had taken all winter. When the clatter quit, two pins lay across the deck in front of the ones still standing.

She straightened and looked at what she'd done. "On my first night Verna had them set fresh."

"That was your ball in my lane." He got up off his knee. "The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."

He stepped back off the approach to give her the room. She was still looking at him, and he was the one who looked away first, down the lane at the wood.

Her second ball was already on the lane, low, rolling at the two pins lying across the deck.

\* \* \*

By the middle of the afternoon there was a party on every lane, and the pit had the warmth it only got on the alley's best days, ten motors running at once and the heat coming off them along the catwalk until a man could work in his shirtsleeves in December. The radio on Ray's belt had a strip of her tape across the back that said Kettle Maintenance, which was the first he'd heard he was a department.

"Kettle Maintenance, lane eight has a birthday at the half hour, and eight's ball return is running slow."

He keyed it. "Roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out."

"Wilco means you will comply. I have not asked you for anything yet."

Eight's return had a house ball hung on the lip of the track, sulking, and he lifted it over with two fingers and she went back to rumbling as if she'd never stopped.

The radio clicked, and Denise came on with no call sign and no breath to spare. "I've got a boy at my counter twelve if he's a day, in house shoes he could sleep in, and does he care, no, he tells me they're fine, and are they fine, they are going to walk down lane three without him."

"Have him walk to the end of the counter and back. If he arrives in them, they're fine."

Ray turned the radio up.

He stood on the catwalk with the crash of ten lanes rolling back at him under the masking. Seven was running sweet, the way a machine will after somebody's paid her some attention, and three was thinking something over and hadn't made up her mind. Through all of it Ellen's voice came the length of the building whole, every word where she'd set it, without being raised, and he had turned it up to hear her settle a twelve-year-old's shoes, and he left it up.

Lane four's machine had stopped mid-rack.

"Kettle Maintenance, say intentions."

"Intentions are to quit and go home."

"Denied. Lane four."

He went along the catwalk to where a pin had come up crooked, and he had both hands inside lane four's elevator when the radio opened again.

"The last Saturday in February. Who holds the lane draw for Linda's Memorial?"

"I do. I drew all ten at Thanksgiving, and it's on my clipboard."

She didn't answer. The radio stayed open a second on the noise of her counter, and he knew what a pencil sounded like when he couldn't hear one. His answer was going down on the sheet she kept the winter on.

Her next call wasn't for him.

"Snack Bar, two baskets for lane six."

\* \* \*

The Bench came to Gagnon & Son's staff party every year for the reason it came to the store every morning, which was that something was free, and not one of the three had ever drawn a paycheck there, and Danny had quit counting them against the cake about the year he took over ordering it. They sat in a row on the burnt-orange settee at lanes nine and ten with paper plates on their knees, early. The two clerks stood at the scorer's table with the birch beer in paper cups, and the sheet cake sat between them with the store's name across it in blue, waiting on a knife.

Ray stood up. Nobody had asked him to.

"And the veteran's on his feet," Normie told his plate.

A thing said in front of the Bench was said. You didn't get it back; they kept everything they heard on a shelf in their heads, dated, and handed it down to you a year later in front of company. He had put this one off two winters. Every morning at six he took the drawer Danny had counted the night before and counted it again, twenties first, the way his father had, and called it helping, and Danny let him call it that, which was worse. This morning Danny had opened alone, with nobody to count his drawer twice. Lucien had been behind that counter the week he died, counting Ray's drawer after him. Ray meant to hand it across whole, the drawer and the counter and the keys, which his father had never managed. Under that was something else, to do with the hours after supper, and he left it down there where it was.

"Thing is," he said, "I've been retiring after the season since about the winter the roof rakes came in short, and Danny counts a drawer better than I do, and I count it again behind him anyway. March first, the store's his. I'll come in Saturdays." He looked at the cake. "If he asks."

Danny had been waiting two winters for this. He held his paper cup in both hands, the way he held a carton he didn't trust the bottom of, and for a second he didn't raise it. Then he did.

"To Dad's last winter behind the counter. He swears it's real this time."

The clerks laughed and drank. Normie, at the end of the settee, turning a plastic fork over in his fingers, didn't drink.

"Then he can go stand behind hers."

Lanes nine and ten laughed, the clerks and the Bench and a couple from the party on eight who had no business listening. Ray laughed too. He heard it come out of him half a second late.

Ellen was ten feet off on the walkway behind the settee, holding the cake knife. Danny had asked the snack bar for one, and she had brought it down herself. She'd heard. Ray watched her face go calm, the calm she'd told him about in the pit with a pin in her hands, and he knew now what it cost her to have it.

He had kept two things in two drawers and never once had them out on the counter at the same time, and Normie had just put them in one sentence: the store to Danny on March first, and the building on March first. Every man on that settee had a square on the pool sheet behind the register at the store, and every one of them knew who bought the building if she missed. The sentence wasn't wrong. Normie had been calling things straight since 'seventy-four. For two winters Ray had meant to give Danny the store and buy Arlene's alley off her, and spend his evenings on machines that were his own, and he had come up the drive with his offer eight days after Ellen did. The building would come to him the same afternoon the store went out of his hands. Any no he said to her between here and March would be a no that paid him.

Ellen held the knife out to him, handle first. He took it by the handle. She went back up the walkway.

He didn't say any of it. He put the knife in the corner of the cake, cut the first piece, and handed it to Normie.

\* \* \*

The sign was on at the bottom of the drive, red script and the white candlepin and the dead K at the front of it, and Ray's truck sat in the mouth of the drive with the door open and the heater running hard for nobody, which it seemed to take personally. Ray stood out of it in the snow, looking up at the dark letter. The trench from Friday was at his boots, drifted half full.

Ellen came down the drive in her vest with no coat on, bare-handed. She'd have seen the truck stop from the glass block. He had stopped it where it could be seen.

"I eat supper at a quarter past five," he said. "Have since 'ninety-six. It's five-twenty."

"Then go."

He didn't go. Up on School Street the pot sat on the back of the stove, turned low since before light, and it would know.

"There's a shop in Laconia bends tubes," he said, still looking up. "She's been dark since the winter Danny got his license, and I'd guess she's used to it by now, which makes one of us. I could have her lit by Christmas."

"Send me a quote."

"I could give you one now, near enough."

"Near enough is not a quote."

He looked at her, and kept looking, until she said what was under it.

"I heard Normie."

"Whole two lanes heard Normie."

She took the pad out of her vest, and the pen. Her knuckles were raw with the cold, and nobody seemed to have told her.

"From Monday, everything you fix in this building, you bill me for. Your hours and your parts, in my format." She wrote a line and drew another under it. "Then nobody in this town can say you are minding your own building."

"Say I'm driving by and I happen to stop."

"Then you bill me for the stop."

On her first night he had stood on her step and told her he wasn't buying her mistake cheap, and here she was in the snow with no coat on, seeing to it that nobody could say he was waiting on one.

"Haven't got a rate."

"You have the Worcester man's. It's in your shirt pocket."

His hand went to it before he could stop it. The page was there under the flap, folded twice, where he had moved it from shirt to shirt every morning since the third, and she had known that, and he hadn't known she knew.

"Twenty years, Arlene never got a bill off me."

"I'd like to know what I owe you."

He had been paid for a good many things across a counter in forty years, and never once by somebody who meant it to cost her, and he stood at the edge of the trench and turned that over, and it didn't get any easier to hold. He'd take it all the same. A bill was the only answer to Normie's sentence he had.

"Every dollar you pay me comes off the winter your bank is counting."

"If I miss, I'll miss by an honest bill."

She was still holding the pen, bare-handed, her fingers gone stiff around it.

"You'll want that hand in your vest," he said. "Pen'll keep."

She put the pen away and her hand after it.

"Go on in," he said.

"In one minute."

He was standing closer than a quote needed. Behind him the heater ran on in the empty cab, and the sign put its red along the shoulder of her vest, and neither of them looked down at the trench.

"Then you'll be paying me, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms. Daly. Net thirty."
