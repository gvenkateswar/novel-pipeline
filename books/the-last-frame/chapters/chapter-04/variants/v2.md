# Chapter 4: Say Intentions

Ray had let himself in on the front key she'd laid on his counter, the first time he had put it in her door, and he had lit lane one and left the other nine running dark to their pits. The heat wasn't up yet. He stood at the house-ball rack in his canvas jacket with a ball in each hand. The one in his right was the lightest she owned, picked by her own tape on the rack so she couldn't say he'd guessed.

The office door opened behind the counter, and she came out with a clipboard held flat against her chest, and he had his good morning about halfway out when she spoke.

"Yesterday at your counter you told the Bench Linda's Memorial stays. You told them on my word. My word had two parts, and you heard one."

She said it from the end of the counter in the voice she used on the PA, and came no closer to say it.

Ray looked at the two balls. Then he walked back up the approach and set the heavy one in its place on the rack, two from the end where her tape changed weights, and turned it in the cradle until it sat, and came back down to the foul line with the light one.

Her thumb was on the clip of the clipboard, holding it shut on paper that wasn't going anywhere.

"In the pit I said, 'It stays. Unless the numbers say otherwise.' Denise's strike took the second half."

He had been there for that strike. He still had the crash of it going into the cushion, and the whoop off the settees, and her face close in the bad light, and nothing under any of it.

"If this winter comes up short," she said, "Linda's Memorial is the biggest day I have, and I will want it."

Down at the far end of lane one the ten pins stood in the only light in the building.

"Read it back."

Nobody had ever asked it of him, and he knew what it was for anyway: you said it the way you'd heard it, so she would know what you heard. He gave it to her flat, the way he'd read a part number off a tag. "It stays unless the numbers say otherwise. If the winter comes up short, you'll want Linda's day."

"Correct."

"You gave me no readback," she said, "and I let it go."

He turned the ball a quarter turn in his hand and looked at her over it.

"You knew. Under the sign?"

"Yes."

"The town had us by noon yesterday."

"Normie had us by seven," Ray said. Hollis had sat in his cab with the blade up and said nothing to a living soul, and it hadn't slowed Normie down any.

The corner of her mouth went, and came back, and she went on.

"At your counter the Bench asked you whether I would take Linda's day. You told them, 'She told me it stays.' Normie told Denise. At close Denise asked me whether that was what I said, and I told her, 'It's half.'"

"While it was the two of us, I could carry it. Hollis's light gave it to the whole town, and then you vouched for me at your counter on half my sentence."

She stopped long enough to take a breath, the first he'd seen her take since the office door.

"Every error I ever made at work, I wrote up inside the hour. This one took me thirty hours and nineteen minutes." She looked down at the board for that part, and back up. "Denise asked me at eleven last night. I could have phoned you then. I am counting that too."

Yesterday morning he had been at his own counter with the drawer done twice and put away when Normie asked it from the end stool, plain for once: was the new owner going to take Linda's day off the league, now she had a building to keep up. Ray had put his hand flat on the wood and said, She told me it stays, and he had said it the way he said a price, so nobody would bother arguing it. Three men on three stools had heard him. So had Danny, ringing up a roof rake at the far register, and Danny had looked up.

"Did you kiss me so I wouldn't ask?"

It was out before he'd decided to say it, and he left it out.

"I kissed you because I wanted to." Her thumb came off the clip. "I kept quiet because the price of telling you was you."

He believed her. It didn't make the morning any easier.

"Then hear mine." It came out short, and he let it stay short. "It stays."

Neither of them said anything after that. The front door was forty feet back up the approach with the key to it in his pocket, and down on Main Street there was a drawer he had walked out on this morning without counting. He turned the light ball over in his hands, and over again, and didn't go.

Then he held it out to her across the ball return.

"Lane's open. You turn it loose a foot off the floor and it lands like a dropped plate. Want that fixed?"

\* \* \*

On her third box Ray knelt beside the foul line, the approach still cold through the knee of his work pants, and held his right hand out flat, palm down, six inches off the boards.

"Under my hand."

She had the small ball already in her bare hands. He had not seen her let anybody in Ambrose show her how to do a thing. To get under his palm she had to bend low, and for once she was the one looking down. Her mouth was set the way it went when she was trying not to laugh, and he could see why: a man on one knee on her lane with his hand out like he was waiting to be paid.

He'd had the next thing ready. It went off somewhere.

"Is there a next instruction?"

"There was. Give me a minute."

It came back to him. "Let the lane have it."

She let it go under his hand, low and quiet, and it went down the maple and took more wood than she'd taken all winter, and left two pins lying crossways on the deck.

She came up out of the bend. "On my first night, Verna—"

"The wood stays on the lane," Ray said. "You play what's left."

He got up off the knee, which had an opinion about it, and stepped back off the approach, and he was the one who looked away first.

Her second ball went in low under nothing at all and was rolling at the wood lying across the deck.

\* \* \*

Every lane had a party on it, and on the catwalk the heat came off ten motors at once, so that Ray had his jacket off for the first time since November. The radio rode on his belt.

"Kettle Maintenance, lane nine has a birthday at the half hour, and nine's ball return is running slow."

He keyed it. "Roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out."

"Wilco means you will comply. I have not asked you for anything yet."

He pulled a child's mitten out of nine's return, and the balls came up the track at their own speed again.

Denise came on next, without a call sign or a full stop. "There's a boy at my counter, twelve if he's a day, in house shoes he could sleep in, and do I think they fit, no, I think he's going to walk right out of them on the approach in front of his whole party, and does he think they fit, he says they're fine."

"Then they're fine," Ellen said.

She wasn't talking to him. Ray turned the radio up a notch anyway, and her voice came through the racket as if she stood on the catwalk at his elbow.

Lane four stopped setting.

"Kettle Maintenance, say intentions."

"Intentions are to quit and go home."

"Denied. Lane four."

He went along the catwalk to four, who had quit with her table halfway down and was sulking about it, and he had both hands in the table when the radio came on again.

"Kettle Maintenance, the last Saturday in February. How many lanes does Linda's Memorial take?"

He keyed it with a knuckle. "All ten. I drew the lanes at Thanksgiving."

Nothing came back. He knew where his answer had gone: onto the sheet she kept the winter on, in her printing.

The radio clicked again. "Snack Bar, two baskets for lane six."

\* \* \*

The Bench had never worked a day for Gagnon & Son, and it came to the staff party anyway, for the free cake, the way it came to the store for the free coffee, and took the burnt-orange settee at nine-and-ten as if somebody had sent for it.

Ray booked the far pair every year on the Saturday before Christmas. Danny had the sheet cake out on the scorer's table, white, with GAGNON & SON across it in green, and a row of paper cups of birch beer going flat beside it, and the two clerks were bowling on ten like men who'd been told it was part of the job. Somebody had already run a finger along one corner of the frosting, and Ray had a fair idea who. On the other eight lanes eight other parties went on with their noise, and not one of them was thinking about the hardware store.

Ray stood up. Nobody had asked him to.

"And the veteran rises," Normie said, to his plate.

A thing said in front of the Bench was said. It went out the door with them and was down Main Street before supper, and you couldn't call it back short of moving to Vermont, and that was the use of them. He had put this off two winters. He'd said after the season, and the season had come round, and he'd said it again. His father had been behind that counter the week he died, counting Ray's drawer after him with his lips moving, and Ray had stood there beside him and let him. Ray meant to hand it over whole, which Lucien never managed. Under that there was something about evenings, and the house on School Street once the radio had done the weather, and he didn't go down after it.

He held his paper cup out a little in front of him, the way you'd hold a level to a shelf.

"Thing is," he said, and on ten the clerks stood with the ball return filling up behind them, "I've been retiring after the season since about the winter the roof rakes came in short, and every season comes round and there I am at six with the lights on, and Danny counts a drawer better than I do, and I count it again behind him anyway, twenties first, the way my father counted mine, and Danny lets me, and he's been decent about it longer than I'd have been."

He looked down at the cake and up again.

"So I'll say it here, where Normie can't get it wrong. March first, the store's his. I'll come in Saturdays." There was one more thing, and he had not known until he was on his feet that it was the thing. "If he asks."

The three words went out over nine-and-ten and stayed there, and he let them. He'd meant all of it, and the last part most.

Danny had waited two winters for that. He was standing by the cake with a paper cup of birch beer in both hands, holding it the way he held a carton he didn't trust the bottom of, and for a second he didn't raise it. He looked at his father across the scorer's table and swallowed once. On lane eight a party Ray didn't know threw a ten box, and the whole settee came up off the cushions yelling, and Danny waited them out.

Then he lifted the cup. "To Dad's last winter behind the counter." He let it hang there a second, the way his father would have. "He swears it's real this time."

The clerks laughed, and Ray laughed with them, because it was a good one and the boy had earned it with two winters of six o'clocks.

Normie had a paper plate on his knee and his fork up, and he didn't put either one down. "Then he can go stand behind hers."

Nine and ten went up all at once, the clerks and the other two on the settee and a couple of wives along the scorer's table, and it carried. Ray laughed too. It came out of him half a second late, and he heard it come late.

Ellen was ten feet off on the walkway behind the settees with the cake knife in her hand. Danny had asked the snack bar for one, and she had carried it down the length of the house herself, and she had been standing there long enough. Ray watched her face go calm. He had seen that face across his own counter at two minutes to five and not known what he was looking at. In the pit she had told him that when she sounded calm she didn't take her own word for it, and he watched her put the calm on now in front of two lanes of his people, and it went on smooth, and he knew now what it cost.

He had kept two things in two drawers all month, and Normie had taken them both out and put them in one sentence: the store to Danny on March first, and her building on March first. The sentence wasn't wrong. For two winters Ray had meant to give Danny the store and go up Kettle Road evenings to Arlene's alley, where there was always something knocking that wanted him, and he had come up the drive eight days after Ellen did. If she missed, the building came to him the same day the store left him.

She came down the last ten feet and held the knife out to him, handle first. He took it. She went back up the walkway toward the counter without hurrying, and the noise of the other eight lanes closed in behind her.

He didn't say any of it. He cut the cake.

\* \* \*

The truck sat at the mouth of the drive with the driver's door open and the heater running for nobody, and Ray stood out on the gravel with his head back, looking up at the dead K. The lot had half emptied between the afternoon parties and the evening ones. Over him the sign was lit, red script and the white candlepin and the first letter dark, and at his boots Friday's trench had drifted in half full and gone soft along the edges.

The vestibule door went, up at the building. She came down the drive in her vest with no coat over it and her bare hands tucked under her arms, and stopped on the far side of the trench.

"I saw you stop," she said. "From the glass block."

He looked back up at the letter. "I eat supper at a quarter past five. Have since about the winter Arlene gave me the key. It's five-twenty."

"Then go."

He didn't go. Behind him the heater went on blowing into the empty cab. Up on School Street the pot was sitting cold on the back of the stove.

"I could have her lit by Christmas," he said. "There's a shop in Laconia still bends the tubes."

"Send me a quote."

Three weeks he'd been handing her things, and she had answered most of them with a piece of paper. A man could get tired of being answered in writing, and he hadn't yet. He looked at her, and kept looking, and she stood on the far side of the trench and let him, and then she said what was under it.

"I heard Normie."

"Whole two lanes heard Normie."

So she had. He had watched her hear it, and then he had cut the cake with her knife and sent the knife back up to the snack bar by way of Danny, and he hadn't seen her since.

"From Monday, everything you fix in this building, you bill me for. Your hours and your parts, in my format." She said it the way she'd given the order on the storm night, as a thing already decided that she was letting him in on. "Then nobody in this town can say you are minding your own building."

"Haven't got a rate."

"You have the Worcester man's. It's in your shirt pocket."

His hand went to the pocket before he'd told it to. The page was there under the flap, folded twice and gone soft at the folds, where he had moved it from one shirt to the next every morning since the third. She had known that. He didn't know how long she had known it.

"Twenty years, Arlene never got a bill off me."

"I'd like to know what I owe you."

He could have argued it. He had the twenty years to argue it with, and Arlene, and every machine in the building that had ever run on his account. He took it anyway, for a reason of his own. A bill was the only answer to Normie's sentence he had.

Behind the glass block the evening's first party was already at the cubbies; he could see the shapes of them bending to their shoes. He did the arithmetic the way he did all arithmetic, roughly, and didn't care for where it came out.

"Every dollar you pay me comes off the winter your bank is counting."

She didn't take long over it. "If I miss, I'll miss by an honest bill."

The red off the sign lay across the backs of her hands where they showed at her elbows.

"You came down here with nothing on those hands," he said, "and they've got ten lanes of shoe spray ahead of them tonight, and it's some cold."

"Nineteen degrees. I checked on the way out."

"Then you'll want to go in."

"In one minute."

He stepped over the soft end of the trench, which put him nearer to her than any quote needed. Yesterday at first light they had stood in this same trench, the two of them. Neither of them said so. The heater ticked in the cab behind him.

"Then you'll be paying me, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms. Daly. Net thirty."
