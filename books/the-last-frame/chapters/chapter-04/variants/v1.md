# Chapter 4: Say Intentions

Only lane one had its lights on. The other nine ran off dark to their pits, and the one strip of maple lay lit from the foul line to the deck with nobody on it, the way a lane looks at seven in the morning and no other time. Ray had let himself in on the front key she'd laid on his counter, the first time it had turned in her door for him, and he had stood in the vestibule a minute afterward with the key still in his fingers and the heat coming up through the floor. Then he'd gone along the house rack and taken down two house balls: one for himself, and the lightest one Arlene had ever bought, small and chipped at the seam, which he had picked out for her.

She came out of the office with her clipboard held flat against her chest, and she spoke before he had got as far as good morning.

"Yesterday at your counter you told the Bench Linda's Memorial stays. You told them on my word. My word had two parts, and you heard one."

He took the heavier ball back to the rack. Her tape on the steel upright said what every slot weighed, and he set the ball in its own slot, between the one lighter and the one heavier, and squared it with the flat of his hand, and squared it again. The light one he kept.

"In the pit I said, It stays. Unless the numbers say otherwise." Her thumb lay along the clip at the top of the board. "Denise's strike took the second half."

He had the strike. He had the crash of it, and the settees whooping up the lane, and her face a foot from his, and nothing of the five words after the first two.

"If this winter comes up short," she said, "Linda's Memorial is the biggest day I have, and I will want it."

She said it in the voice he'd heard come over the PA on her first night, every word set down in its place and nothing in it a man could get a hand on. Down cellar the boiler shut off, and the building went on ticking without it.

"Read it back."

He did it flat, the way he'd read a part number off a tag. "It stays unless the numbers say otherwise. If the winter comes up short, you'll want Linda's day."

"Correct."

"You gave me no readback," she said, "and I let it go."

He turned the small ball a quarter turn in his palm. It was still cold from the rack.

"You knew," he said. "Under the sign?"

"Yes."

"The town had us by noon yesterday," she said.

"Normie had us by seven."

One corner of her mouth went, and she put it back.

"At your counter the Bench asked you whether I would take Linda's day. You told them, She told me it stays. Normie told Denise. At close Denise asked me whether that was what I said, and I told her, It's half."

She had not moved the clipboard an inch.

"While it was the two of us, I could carry it," she said. "Hollis's light gave it to the whole town, and then you vouched for me at your counter on half my sentence."

"Every error I ever made at work, I wrote up inside the hour." Nothing on her moved but the thumb, which went once along the clip and stopped. "This one took me thirty hours and nineteen minutes. Denise asked me at eleven last night. I could have phoned you then. I am counting that too."

Thirty hours and nineteen minutes, and he had spent a good piece of them at his own counter yesterday afternoon, with the Bench on its three stools and the dark coming down outside the front windows at four, and his hand flat on the wood the way his father used to lay a hand down once he'd named a price. *She told me it stays.* He'd said it plain so nobody would need to ask him twice. The Bench had heard him, and so had Danny, down at the far register, who had stopped what he was doing to listen.

"Did you kiss me so I wouldn't ask?"

"I kissed you because I wanted to." She held the board where it was. "I kept quiet because the price of telling you was you."

He didn't say anything for a while. The light over lane one hummed in its fixture, and up the lane the pins stood on their spots, set and waiting.

"Then hear mine," he said. "It stays."

Neither of them said any more on it. The front door was forty feet behind him, and the key to it was in his jacket, and his truck sat out in her lot with the frost still on the glass. He could go. He turned the small ball over in his hands until he had its weight again, and stayed where he was.

Then he held it out to her across the ball return.

"Lane's open," he said. "You turn it loose a foot off the floor and it lands like a dropped plate. Want that fixed?"

\* \* \*

She had the small ball in her bare hands when he got down on one knee at the foul line. It was her third box. Her first two had gone down lane one the way everything she threw went, up off the boards and down again with a crack, and the maple under his knee still had the whole night's cold in it. He put his right hand out flat, palm down, six inches off the boards and just past the line.

"Under my hand."

She looked at the hand, and then at him, and bent. To bring the ball through under his palm she had to come down low over the line, and for once she was the one looking down. The glasses swung out on their cord. Her mouth was set the way it set when she was trying hard not to laugh at him and losing, and he looked up at it from the cold boards, and the next thing he'd meant to tell her went clean out of his head.

"Is there a next instruction?"

"There was," he said. "Give me a minute."

Her mouth held, barely. He found something else to tell her. "Let the lane have it."

She let go under his hand, so low he felt the air of it cross his palm, and the ball went in quiet without a bounce and ran long and straight down the lit maple, and took more wood than he'd seen her take all winter. When the crash had settled, two pins lay crossways on the deck among the ones still standing.

She looked at the wood a while. "On my first night Verna had them set fresh."

"The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."

He got up off the knee, which clicked, and stepped back off the approach to give her room. He was the one who looked away first, at a score pad on the table that wanted squaring, and he squared it.

Her second ball was already on the lane, low and quiet, rolling at the wood across the deck.

\* \* \*

Ten machines going at once on a party Saturday put out a warmth Ray could feel in his face from the end of the catwalk, all of them sweeping and setting behind the masking, and the radio on his belt was the only thing back there that talked.

"Kettle Maintenance, lane nine has a birthday at the half hour, and nine's ball return is running slow."

The register drawer rang under her voice. She was at the counter, then, by the gooseneck. He keyed the radio.

"Roger that, copy, ten-four, wilco, over and out."

"Wilco means you will comply." The drawer shut behind her. "I have not asked you for anything yet."

Nine's return belt had gone slick, and he dressed it and stood back while she sulked over it a minute and came round.

The radio went again, and it was Denise, who had never used a call sign in her life. "There's a boy at my counter, twelve if he's a day, in a pair of house shoes he could sleep in, and did I offer him a pair his size, I did, and does he want them, he does not, he says they're fine, and are they fine, they're about as fine as two rowboats, and who fills out the form when he walks out of one on the approach, me?"

Ray turned the radio up.

"Have him walk to the end of the counter and back," Ellen said, with lane one crashing close behind her. "If he arrives in them, they're fine."

Four had stopped. She'd heard it from the far end of the house, over nine lanes going, before he heard it from behind the machine.

"Kettle Maintenance, say intentions."

"Intentions are to quit and go home."

"Denied. Lane four."

He went along the catwalk to four. Her voice came to him down sixty feet of maple, live, telling the party on four it would be one minute, so she was standing on their approach looking up the lane at the machine, and he put his hands into it with her that close.

The next call came with a shut door behind it and the house gone quiet on the far side. The office.

"Kettle Maintenance, the last Saturday in February. How many lanes does Linda's Memorial take?"

"All ten. I drew the lanes at Thanksgiving."

The draw was on the league clipboard on the seat of his truck, in his own hand, as it had been every November there'd been a Memorial to draw.

She held the key down and said nothing. Behind the open line the adding machine ran one short line and stopped. Then the radio clicked, and he knew where his answer had gone, onto the sheet where she kept the winter.

Her next call came a minute later.

"Snack Bar, two baskets for lane six."

\* \* \*

The Bench had come up from the store for the cake. It was the Gagnon and Son staff party, on lanes nine and ten, which Ray booked and paid for every December, and the Bench had never been staff a day in its life and came every year all the same, the way it came for the coffee, on the understanding that anything free in Ambrose was free to them first. Normie had the end of the burnt-orange settee with a paper cup of birch beer on his knee. The two clerks had the other settee and were arguing over the corner pieces of a sheet cake nobody could cut yet, because Danny had sent up to the snack bar for a knife.

Ray's radio was still on his belt, turned up from the afternoon, and it had been quiet ten minutes, which meant she was at the counter, where she could see all ten lanes and had nobody to call.

He stood up. Nobody had asked him to. Up at the far end of the house he could make out the gooseneck and a navy vest beside it, and no more than that at this distance.

A thing said in front of the Bench was said. Those three had carried every word spoken at his counter down Main Street since about the Carter years, and he'd put this off two winters on just that account, because once Normie had it there'd be no taking it back. His father had stood behind that counter the week he died, counting Ray's drawer after him, twenties first, the way Ray counted Danny's now, and Lucien had never once handed the store over to anybody. Ray meant to hand it over whole, in one piece, with people watching. Under that was something else, to do with supper and the long evening after it, and he let it lie where it was.

"Thing is," he said, "I've been retiring after the season since about the winter the roof rakes came in short, and Danny counts a drawer better than I do, and I count it again behind him anyway. March first, the store's his. I'll come in Saturdays." He looked at his boy. "If he asks."

Danny had waited two winters for this. He held his paper cup in both hands, and for a second he didn't raise it. Then he did.

"To Dad's last winter behind the counter. He swears it's real this time."

Cups went up all down nine and ten. Normie left his on his knee.

"Then he can go stand behind hers."

Nine and ten laughed, the clerks first and then the Bench, and Danny too, a little, into his cup.

Up at the snack bar somebody keyed a radio and let go without a word. The click came off Ray's belt, and with it the same click again, smaller, from the walkway at his back.

He knew before he turned. Ellen stood ten feet off on the walkway behind the settees, holding the cake knife Danny had asked the snack bar for, which she had brought down herself. She'd heard. He watched her face go calm, the calm she had told him about in the pit, the kind she didn't take her own word for, and he knew now what it cost her.

He'd kept two things in separate drawers two winters, and Normie had taken both out and laid them on the table in one sentence. March first the store went to Danny. March first, if she missed, the paper she'd signed sold Ray her building. Normie had it about right. For two winters Ray had meant to give Danny the store and drive up Kettle Road evenings to Arlene's alley, and fix what wanted fixing there till somebody turned the lights out on him, and he had come up that drive with his offer eight days after Ellen came up it with hers. If she missed, he'd have the building the same day the store left him, and every no he said to her between now and then, the whole town could work out which pocket it went in.

She held the knife out to him, handle first. He took it by the handle. She went back up the walkway.

He didn't say any of it. He cut the cake.

\* \* \*

Ray had the truck stopped at the mouth of the drive with the door hung open and the heater running for nobody, the weather coming low out of the dash radio, and he stood out on the packed snow beside it looking up at the sign. It was full dark. The red script was lit and the white candlepin was lit, and over his head the first letter hung black, the dead K, with an inch of new snow along its top. At his boots Friday's trench had drifted in half full.

She'd have seen the truck stop from the glass block. She came down the drive in her vest without a coat, her arms folded against the wind off the lake and her bare hands tucked up under them, and stopped on the far side of the trench.

"I eat supper at a quarter past five," he said. "Have every night since nineteen ninety-six. It's five-twenty. This'll be twice this month on your account."

"Then go."

He didn't go. He looked back up at the K. Behind him the truck went on running with its door open, and the man on the radio finished the weather and started in on the Bruins, and the heat came out over the sill and blew away up the drive.

"There's a shop in Laconia still bends tube," he said. "I could have her lit by Christmas."

"Send me a quote."

"I could give you one now, near enough."

"Near enough is not a quote."

He looked at her then, and kept looking, and she let him a good while before she said what was under it.

"I heard Normie."

"Whole two lanes heard Normie," he said.

She unfolded her arms. "From Monday, everything you fix in this building, you bill me for. Your hours and your parts, in my format. Then nobody in this town can say you are minding your own building."

"Say I'm driving by and I happen to stop."

"Then you bill me for the stop."

"Haven't got a rate."

"You have the Worcester man's. It's in your shirt pocket."

His hand went to the pocket before he could stop it. The page was there under the button, folded twice, where he'd moved it from shirt to shirt every morning for better than two weeks, and she'd known that too. She watched his hand find it, and she very nearly laughed.

"Twenty years I had my head in those machines," he said, "and the boiler twice, and Arlene fed me a hot dog now and again and called it square, and I never thought to ask her for more, and I don't know as I'd know how. Twenty years, Arlene never got a bill off me."

"I'd like to know what I owe you."

He'd take it, then, and for a reason of his own. Normie's sentence had been going round in him since four o'clock like a bearing with grit in it, and a bill was the only answer to it he had.

"Every dollar you pay me," he said, "comes off the winter your bank is counting."

"If I miss, I'll miss by an honest bill."

She said it the way she said the time. He looked at her hands. She had them out in the wind now with nothing in them, red across the knuckles, and she never would keep them warm.

He'd come round the end of the trench without deciding to, and he stood closer to her than a quote needed. The snow had filled in soft over what they'd dug. Neither of them said where they were standing.

"Then you'll be paying me, Mrs. Daly."

"Ms. Daly. Net thirty."
