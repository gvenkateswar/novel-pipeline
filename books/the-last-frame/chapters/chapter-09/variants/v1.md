# Chapter 9: The D-side

Her hands were empty. No pad, no pen, no clipboard, nothing to square on somebody's counter while she found her place. On Monday he had said it across her counter, with a squad sheet between them: "Wednesday. Quarter past five. Bring nothing." So she had brought nothing, and she stood on his back step on School Street a minute early, as she was for everything, deciding whether a knock a minute early counted as early. His gloves were in her coat pocket. The key he had laid on her counter was in her vest. She left them both where they were.

The door opened before she knocked.

"Heard you being early," Ray said.

He had been standing on the other side of it since her car, then. She came in past him into a heat that took the cold off her face, and a smell of hamburg and green pepper and tomato cooked down, and a small kitchen painted a yellow nobody had argued with in forty years. On top of the refrigerator a radio gave the weather on low: a few inches Saturday morning, over by noon. Ray reached up and turned it down, and neither of them said a word about it.

He took his own bowl off the drainboard, white, with a chip in the rim. Then he opened the cupboard beside the stove and reached past the stack to the back of the shelf and brought out a second bowl, a blue one, and ran it under the tap and dried it before he filled it.

She sat where he set it down. The table under the oilcloth was built for two people who didn't mind knees. The bowl was hot clear through to the bottom, and she held it in both hands anyway for the heat after the step. Elbow macaroni, tomato gone soft into everything, green pepper with a little bite still left in it. She had not known she was this hungry. She ate half of it without a word, and he let her, and ate his.

She looked over at the dented pot on the stove. It would feed a man four nights running.

"What do you make the second night?"

"That one."

Under the oilcloth his boot came against her right knee and stayed there. She did not move her knee.

"You favor one on a stair," he said. "Which?"

"The left."

"What happened to it?"

She had told it to an orthopedist and an insurance form, and to nobody else. She told him by time and place, as she would have written it up. December of two thousand and nine, a small hill up north, Kate fourteen and wanting the top. Ellen had kept them both on the beginner trail, which was the plan she had made in the car. Halfway down, three boys on a toboggan came out of the trees from the left, at a speed that left no room for discussion.

"It had the right of way," she said, "so I gave it the right of way."

Ray laughed. It came up out of him short and surprised, and his boot shifted against her knee and came back.

She went on, in the same order. She had given it the right of way into a snow fence. The fence held, the knee went, and Kate skied down alone to bring the patrol up, at fourteen, before anybody told her to. He listened to all of it with his spoon down and his boot where it was. He had laughed at the toboggan and only there, and she could have said to the word where it started, as she had once known to the word where a voice changed on a frequency.

It was a quarter to six. She had it as she had every minute of every day, from somewhere under her breastbone, and this one she sat inside and let go by. The window over the sink had gone full dark, the yard fence a gray line under the porch light. She had a slot for every other quarter to six in her life, a shift change, a briefing, a squad called to its lanes, and this one would not take a slot. It had his boot against her knee in it, and the blue bowl with a little red left at the bottom, and she did not want to give it one.

Ray looked up at the clock over the stove, which she had not needed. "About now this house goes quiet," he said. "Supper's done, and it's the better part of four hours to bed, and nobody in them."

"Denise told me. In January."

"Denise would."

"I wanted it from you."

He turned his spoon over on the table, and turned it back.

"I told myself I'd go up that drive the winter Danny was ready," he said. "You went up it."

"It's a good supper," she said.

He looked at her for a second across the table. Then he got up and took her bowl to the stove, and the ladle went into the pot.

\* \* \*

The phone's light was on the ceiling before her feet found the floor. She had woken at ten to four, as she did every morning, and opened the radar lying down.

She had it at once. The band had parked over the lake, and its edges were holding while the color over the water deepened. In December she had let the forecast tell her when a band would move on, and it had sat there and buried Kettle Road while her league bowled.

Since Wednesday the forecast had said a few inches Saturday morning, over by noon, so she had kept both evening squads. Snow that quit by noon would be plowed off the hill hours before either of them. Last night, with page two in her hand, she had held Sunday's lanes spare anyway. Four squads, thirty teams, every place taken.

The floor was cold under her bare feet. She was dressed and lacing her snow boots when the phone rang.

At ten past four it was Hollis.

"You up?"

"Since ten to."

"Figured. Five inches since midnight and an inch an hour now. I'm calling the outdoor day. You'll get the families on top of your indoor day."

He had said in January he would be the one calling it.

"Ice I can call," Hollis said. "The roads are Dale's."

She called Dale at a quarter past four, from the captains' list in her phone. He picked up in his truck, with the wipers going.

"Road agent, Kettle Lanes."

A second of nothing, and the wipers.

"You've never once called me."

"Your band's stalled over the lake. Give me your call before the forecast's."

"Roads hold till the middle of the afternoon. After that they go. I'll call the hour."

Then the morning could come up her hill on his word. She would empty the building inside whatever hour he gave her, and leave the evening squads and Linda's final where they stood until he called it, because a band that had stalled could still move off.

"Call it to me."

"You'll have it."

\* \* \*

She counted the room: a hundred and fifty, children in it, snow boots under every settee. The phone was ringing under the counter, the fryer was out of baskets, and lane three dropped its last rack crooked and stopped.

Behind lane three the noise came through the backs of the machines like weather through a wall. Ray had both hands inside the pinsetter, and he took them out when he saw her face. She put a hand on the kickback.

"Relief briefing. First, weather. The band's parked over the lake, and Dale calls the hour. Second, traffic. The morning squads are off the lanes by half past twelve, the families have the settees, and the two evening squads are fifteen teams, every one of them in the building. Third, lane three is down until you tell me it isn't. Fourth, if Dale calls it early, Linda's final goes to Sunday at one, on the lanes I held."

"I want you on the position. Today and after, whatever Monday says."

"At the Center they call it the D-side. The one on the radar works the traffic. The D-side sits beside her and takes everything else. The phones, the doors, the lot. Dale."

"Read it back to me. I want to hear it in your voice."

"Weather's the band parked over the lake and Dale calling the hour, and the morning's off by half past twelve and the families on the settees and fifteen teams for the evening, every one of them in the building, and lane three's down till I tell you it isn't. And if Dale calls it early it's Linda's final Sunday at one on the lanes you held, and the phones and the doors and the lot, and Dale. Today. And after."

Half past twelve, fifteen, one: he had rounded nothing. Her own words were coming back in his voice, in her order, and it was the best sound she knew.

"Readback correct."

The radio on the bench came up with Denise, from the snack bar. "Counter needs somebody. Man from Lowell asking does the evening still go."

Ray keyed it before her hand was off the kickback. "Pit. Coming up."

He was already moving down the catwalk.

\* \* \*

Dale stood at her counter with snow on the shoulders of his plow jacket, and the Legion watched from the snack bar, Judy's sister's boy among them.

"Four o'clock. I want everybody off the roads by four. After that I've got one plow for Kettle Road and Main Street both, and I won't be digging out a hundred and fifty cars."

She turned to Ray before she answered.

"D-side. Your read."

Dale watched her ask somebody.

"December was thirty-three people. This is a hundred and fifty with kids in it, and the heat goes when the power does. I'd send them home."

"So would I."

On the PA she said it plainly: off the hill by four. Linda's final would bowl Sunday at one, on the lanes she had held.

She offered the evening teams Sunday morning. The Lowell captain, in his team's matching shirt, shook his head.

"It's two hours home on a dry road, and we're on at six Monday."

Then a woman captain from Maine.

"I lost to Linda Gagnon twice in Lewiston. I drove up to have her cup in my hands once."

She took her envelope anyway. Beside Ellen, Ray wrote each team's name on the squad sheet as she said it.

She counted each team's money out of the drawer, then the floor safe, into an envelope with the team's name in her printing, and handed it across the counter to a face, and the drawer's bell rang every time.

Dale's crew came last, and she offered him Sunday.

"My crew's on the trucks till it quits."

He put the envelope inside his plow jacket. "Nobody ever refunded me for a snowstorm either."

He went out without saying no to Sunday. By a quarter to four the last headlights had gone down the hill.

At four o'clock a squad would have thrown its first ball, and nothing threw. All ten lanes were lit for nobody. Denise had gone down the hill with the others. Ellen ran the tape herself, with the register's printing the only thing to hear, and read it to the bottom. She counted what had gone in the Lowell shirts going out the door, and the woman who had wanted the cup in her hands once, and fifteen envelopes in her printing with the names Ray had written down beside her.

Ray had stayed.

"Fourteen hundred dollars under, with the fries counted. Sunday can't make that."

"Judy called the committee a formality," Ray said.

"For a winter that clears. Monday it goes to people who've never met me."

"Then put my name behind yours at Judy's."

"Beside me, I said. Behind mine, your name pays if I miss. Arlene signed that away on the fifteenth, and I will not sign it back in at Judy's desk."

"It might carry them."

"It might."

The rest had sat in her name since before the building, and it was Kate's to say yes or no to.

"Dale's one plow comes down the hill on the hour," Ray said. "I'll take you down behind it."

She went for her coat.

"What'll you tell Arlene?"

"That I'm under."

\* \* \*

Arlene's good hat sat on top of the last taped box, gray felt with a pin in the band. Arlene was at her table. Ellen stood, with snow going to water on her coat. Below the window Ray's truck idled at the curb, its dash lit green across the passenger seat she had come down in. On the scanner by the sink the plow crews were talking low, Pelkey somewhere past the culvert.

"I'm under," Ellen said. "By more than Sunday can make."

Arlene didn't ask by how much. Her hand went to the brim of the hat and stayed there.

"On the fifteenth I told you the money I swore to my daughter wasn't in this. If she says yes, it is. I'm asking her tonight."

"Why that money?"

"It's mine, and it's sat since before I signed for the building. It's the only money that counts."

Arlene took her hand off the hat.

"Then ask her tonight, dear. The woman behind me on the list has been packed since August, and I've been packed since September."

\* \* \*

She had no pad out. Nothing lay on the table in order, and she wrote nothing down. The baseboard heat ticked along the wall, and past the window the lake was white to the far shore. Her feet were cold on the boards. She had called her daughter at seven on a Sunday for years, and never on a Saturday night. She dialed.

Kate picked up on the second ring.

"It's Saturday."

"I know."

"Then something's wrong."

Ellen had it in the first three words, the shift half gone and the fright under it held down by speed. Behind Kate a voice overhead paged somebody to triage.

"Nobody's hurt," Ellen said.

"Okay. Then what?"

"There's a man who was behind me on the building. If I missed on the first of March, the contract I signed sold it to him. His name is Ray Gagnon. He fixes my machines. I signed it at the closing, and I never told you."

"The man who fixes your machines." Kate made a short sound through her nose. "The one you mention every Sunday and never say anything about."

"I'm saying something now. On the fifteenth I let him out of it, with Arlene. Since then, if I miss, it's a bank sale, and the house goes with it."

She said it in order, and plainly, and it took less time than she had thought it would. On the line she heard a chair take Kate's weight.

"The fifteenth. That's twelve days. You called me Sunday."

"I did."

She had called at seven, and asked about the night shifts, and talked about the Open.

"Why didn't you ask me?"

"Because you'd have said no, and I had already decided."

Kate didn't say anything. A cart rattled past in a hallway behind her and was gone.

"You're not doing the voice."

"No."

She had not thought to put it on. She noticed that now, standing at the window with the phone, and left it off.

"I want to put the rest in. I haven't decided. I'm asking you first."

Kate said nothing for a moment. Ellen could hear her choosing which question went first, and let her choose.

"What's it cost? Both ways."

"If you say yes, it takes more than half of the rest, and what's left heats the camp through one bad winter. If you say no, Arlene goes back on the list at eighty-four."

"How much is more than half?"

"Judy writes the figure Monday morning."

A pen scratched on something stiff.

"What's left?"

"One bad winter."

"One."

Kate said it as she might have checked a dose.

"Is there anybody— Is there anybody else?"

"Ray offered his name behind mine at the bank. I said no."

The pen stopped.

"Why?"

"Because then he's behind me again, and I asked him beside me."

"So it's the rest because you won't take his name."

"Yes."

Kate didn't answer.

Ellen held the phone and waited. Nothing came down the line, no breath and no words, for so long that she took the phone from her ear and looked at the screen to see whether the call was still counting, and it was, the seconds going over one at a time. She put it back. Far off behind Kate a voice overhead paged a doctor to the desk, and a door went shut on its closer, and somebody near the phone said a name, a woman's, twice, and got an answer Ellen could not make out. Somebody laughed down a hallway, and a monitor sounded and was silenced. She did not fill any of it. She had filled every silence on a frequency for most of her life, and she had a sentence ready now, and another behind it, about Monday and Judy's figure and the first of March, and she let them stay where they were. She had the length of the quiet to the second, as she had every length, and she let it run. She thought of Kate at fourteen, skiing down alone for the patrol, and did not say that either. The baseboard heat ticked. On the far shore a light went out. She stood at the window with nothing in her hands but the phone and waited on her daughter.

Then Kate's breath came back on the line, close, as though she had turned her face into the phone.

"Mom. Scale of one to ten."

"Ten."

Kate breathed out, long.

"That one I believe."

Ellen opened her mouth to answer it.

"I'm on till three. Let me sleep on it. And I'm going to want something for it."

The line went dead.
