# Chapter 9: The D-side

Her hands had nothing in them. No pad, no pen, no clipboard. On Monday he had stood at her counter long enough to say, "Wednesday. Quarter past five. Bring nothing," and gone out to his truck before she could ask what nothing covered, and she had brought exactly that. His gloves were in her coat pocket and the front key was in her vest, and she left both where they were.

She was a minute early, as she was for everything, and stood on his back step deciding whether a knock a minute early counted as early or only as a knock.

The door opened before she had decided.

"Heard you being early," Ray said.

He had heard her car a minute ago, she worked out, and had been standing on the other side of the door since.

The kitchen's heat met her on the sill, with hamburg and green pepper and tomato cooked down in it. The walls were a yellow nobody had argued with in forty years. On the refrigerator a radio gave the weather on low, a few inches Saturday morning and over by noon, and Ray turned it down on his way past. Neither of them remarked on it.

The second bowl came from the back of the cupboard, behind two others, white with a thin blue band where his was plain. He rinsed it under the tap and dried it on a towel before he filled it.

She was hungrier than she had known. The bowl was too hot to hold, so she held it by the rim with her sleeves pulled down over her fingers, and ate elbow macaroni and hamburg and tomato with the green pepper gone soft in it, salty and plain and very good, and had the second spoonful before the first had cooled. The table had been built for two people who didn't mind knees.

She looked at the pot on the stove. It held about four of these.

"What do you make the second night?"

"That one."

Under the oilcloth his boot came against her right knee and stayed there. She did not move it.

"You favor one on a stair," he said. "Which?"

"The left."

"What happened to it?"

She told it by the calendar, the only way she told anything. December of two thousand and nine, a hill up north, Kate fourteen and somewhere above her, and Ellen on the beginner trail. A toboggan came across from the left with three boys on it.

"It had the right of way," she said, "so I gave it the right of way."

He laughed out loud, once, and she heard exactly which word had done it. Then the snow fence, and the knee, and the patrol's sled down to the lodge, and he heard the rest out with his spoon resting in the bowl.

It was a quarter to six. She could have given anyone the time at any minute of her working life, and she could give it now, and for once there was no job in it. On any other evening this winter it was a minute she spent checking something, and tonight she was spending it at an oilcloth table with her coat on the back of a kitchen chair and a man's boot against her knee and the bowl cooling in her hands, and she let it go by with her in it, at the speed it went. The key lay in her vest. She did not yet know what she was in this kitchen, and she had come without a list to tell her.

"About now's when it goes quiet in here," Ray said. "Supper's done and the one bowl's washed, and it's near four hours to bed with nobody in them."

"Denise told me. In January."

"Denise would."

"I wanted it from you."

He looked down at the oilcloth, and back up at her.

"I told myself I'd go up that drive the winter Danny was ready," he said. "You went up it."

His boot stayed where it was.

"It's a good supper," she said.

He looked at her for a second. Then he got up, took her bowl, and carried it to the stove, and the ladle went into the pot.

\* \* \*

The radar put a blue light on the ceiling over the bed, and she read it lying down, before her feet had found the floor. The band lay along the lake from the town beach to the far shore, and the loop behind it showed it arriving at midnight and going nowhere since. It was parked over the water and staying. In December she had taken a band like this one for something on its way to somewhere else, because the forecast said so, and it had settled over the lake and buried Kettle Road while she ran league under it. The corner of the screen said ten to four. Her feet found the floor, and the floor was cold.

From Wednesday through Friday the forecast had said a few inches Saturday morning, over by noon, and she had kept both evening squads on it. A few inches did not touch a six o'clock squad. On Friday night, with page two of the terms in her hand, she had held Sunday's empty lanes spare anyway. Four squads, thirty teams, every place taken.

The phone rang at ten past four with her dressed and lacing her snow boots.

"You up?" Hollis said.

"Since ten to."

"Figured. Nine inches at the access since midnight. Two more since three. Page two says I call the outdoor day. I'm calling it. Nobody drives on that ice today."

"The families?"

"Phone tree has them by five. That puts them on you, on top of the indoor day. You got room?"

"I have ten lanes and the settees. Send them."

"Ice I can call. The roads are Dale's."

She called Dale Hutchins first, at a quarter past four. His number was on the captains' list taped beside the camp phone, and he picked up in his truck with the wipers going.

"Road agent, Kettle Lanes."

A second of nothing came down the line, with the wipers in it.

"You've never once called me."

"Your band's stalled over the lake. Give me your call before the forecast's."

The wipers went across and back, and across. She waited.

"Roads hold till the middle of the afternoon. After that they go. I'll call the hour."

The day would empty inside his hour, and the evening would stand till he called it. A parked band could still pull out.

"Call it to me."

"You'll have it."

\* \* \*

She counted the room: a hundred and fifty, children in it, snow boots under every settee. Every evening team was in the building already; they had come up for the derby and come in off the cancelled ice with the families.

"These say four o'clock at the dock," the derby treasurer said, with the raffle stubs already tipped into lane one's ball return.

"They'll still say it," Ellen said. A child in a snowsuit drew.

On lane two the Legion was finishing the late-morning squad with Judy Ostrowski's nephew at anchor, her sister's boy.

The phone rang under the counter while she had the handheld to her mouth. The lot was filling. The fryer was out of baskets. Lane three dropped its last rack crooked and stopped.

In the pit the room's noise came through to the backs of the machines like weather through a wall. Ray had both hands inside the pinsetter on three, and when he saw her face he took them out.

She put one hand on the kickback.

"Relief briefing. First, weather. The band's parked over the lake, and Dale calls the hour. Second, traffic. The morning squads are off the lanes by half past twelve, the families have the settees, and the two evening squads are fifteen teams, every one of them in the building. Third, lane three is down until you tell me it isn't. Fourth, if Dale calls it early, Linda's final goes to Sunday at one, on the lanes I held."

He waited.

"I want you on the position. Today and after, whatever Monday says. At the Center they call it the D-side. The one on the radar works the traffic. The D-side sits beside her and takes everything else. The phones, the doors, the lot. Dale."

He picked a rag up off the bench and held it.

"Read it back to me. I want to hear it in your voice."

"Weather's parked over the lake and Dale calls the hour, and the morning's off the lanes by half past twelve and the families have the settees, and the two evening squads are fifteen teams and every one of them is in the building. And three's down till I tell you she isn't, and if Dale calls it early Linda's final goes to Sunday at one on the lanes you held, and the phones and the doors and the lot, and Dale. Today. And after."

He gave her every number back exact, which he had never once done for her, and it was the best sound she knew.

"Readback correct."

The radio on the bench opened: Denise, from the snack bar. "Counter needs somebody. Man from Lowell asking does the evening still go."

Ray keyed it before Ellen's hand was off the kickback. "Pit. Coming up."

He was already moving down the catwalk.

\* \* \*

Dale Hutchins had snow on the shoulders of his plow jacket.

"Four o'clock," he said. "I want everybody off the roads by four. After that I've got one plow for Kettle Road and Main Street both, and I won't be digging out a hundred and fifty cars."

It was twenty to two. Ellen turned, before she answered him, to Ray at her end of the counter with the phone.

"D-side. Your read."

Dale watched her ask somebody.

"December was thirty-three people," Ray said. "This is a hundred and fifty with kids in it, and the heat goes when the power does. I'd send them home."

"So would I."

On the PA she said it in plain words: everybody off the hill by four. Linda's final would bowl Sunday at one, on the lanes she had held.

She offered every evening team Sunday morning.

The captain from Lowell had three men behind him in matching shirts. "It's two hours home on a dry road, and we're on at six Monday. We came up Thursday for the derby and never got a line in the water."

A woman captain came next, with her coat zipped to the chin. "I lost to Linda Gagnon twice in Lewiston. I came up here to hold her cup in my hands one time." She took her envelope anyway.

Ray stood beside her and wrote each team's name on the squad sheet as she said it.

She counted each team's money out of the drawer, and then out of the floor safe, into an envelope with the team's name across it in her printing, and passed it across the counter to a face. The drawer's bell went every time. Fifteen envelopes, and at the snack bar Judy's nephew stood with the Legion and watched them all go.

Dale was last in the line. "Bring your crew Sunday," she said.

"My crew's on the trucks till it quits." He took the envelope and put it inside his plow jacket. "Nobody ever refunded me for a snowstorm either."

He went out. He had not said no to Sunday.

By a quarter to four the headlights were going down the hill in a line through the glass block.

At four o'clock, when the first evening squad would have thrown its first ball, nothing threw. All ten lanes were lit for nobody, end to end, the settees empty in their pairs with paper boats left on them, and Denise gone down the hill in the line with the others. Ellen ran the tape herself and stood at the register and read it as it came out. She thought of four shirts from Lowell on a road that was no longer dry, and a woman who had come to hold a cup, and the envelopes in their coats somewhere on Kettle Road, and the figure at the bottom of the tape, which she read twice.

Ray had stayed, and she gave it to him.

"Fourteen hundred dollars under, with the fries counted. Sunday can't make that."

"Judy called the committee a formality."

"For a winter that clears. Monday it goes to people who've never met me."

"Then put my name behind yours at Judy's."

"Beside me, I said. Behind mine, your name pays if I miss. Arlene signed that away on the fifteenth, and I will not sign it back in at Judy's desk."

"It might carry them."

"It might."

She knew where she was going next, and it was to Kate.

"Dale's one plow comes down the hill on the hour," Ray said. "I'll take you down behind it."

She went for her coat.

"What'll you tell Arlene?"

"That I'm under."

\* \* \*

Arlene's good hat sat on the last box by the wall, dark felt with a pin in the band, on a lid taped shut and labeled for The Pines in her ledger hand. Arlene was at her table. Ellen stood inside the door with the snow going to water on her coat, and did not take it off, and Arlene did not tell her to. Below the window Ray's truck idled at the curb, its dash lit green, and the passenger seat she had come down in sat empty beside him.

On the counter the scanner had the town's plow crews on it, low, and somebody was asking for a wing at the culvert.

"Pelkey," Arlene said. "He's had that culvert since his father had it."

"I'm under, Arlene," Ellen said. "More than Sunday can make."

"You told me one storm would take it. In this kitchen."

"It took it."

"I want my bed, Ellen." Arlene got up, lifted the hat off the box, and set it on the table between them. "I don't need the figure, dear. Go on."

Ellen had come up the stair with it in order, and she gave it in order.

"On the fifteenth I told you the money I swore to my daughter wasn't in this. If she says yes, it is. I'm asking her tonight."

Arlene turned the hat by its brim until the pin faced Ellen.

"Why that money?"

"It's mine, and it's sat since before I signed for the building. It's the only money that counts."

"Then ask her tonight, dear. The woman behind me on the list has been packed since August, and I've been packed since September."

\* \* \*

She had no pad out. Nothing lay written down in any order on the table in front of her, and she did not reach for a pen. On every Sunday of Kate's grown life she had called with the week already sorted, each piece of news in its slot and nothing in any of it that Kate could change. Tonight she knew the two answers Kate could give and what each one cost, and she had not picked between them, and she kept herself from it. The baseboard heat ticked along the wall. Past the window the lake lay white to the far shore, and the floor under her socks was cold. She dialed.

Kate picked up on the fourth ring, with a door swinging shut behind her and something dropping in a vending machine.

"It's Saturday."

"I know."

"Then something's wrong." A chair scraped. "I'm on my break. Go."

Ellen told her the paper first. "There's a man who signed behind me on the building. Ray Gagnon. If I missed on the first of March, my contract sold it to him. On the fifteenth I let him out of it, with Arlene. Since then the house has been on my winter."

"Wait. Behind you how, like a—" Kate stopped herself. "Gagnon. The man who fixes your machines." A breath. "The one you mention every Sunday and never say anything about."

"That one."

"Okay. Okay." Something was set down hard. "And before the fifteenth, if you missed?"

"He'd have bought it at appraisal. Most of the house would have come back to me."

"And now?"

"A bank sale. The house goes with it."

Somebody spoke at Kate's end, close. "Two minutes," Kate told them. "It's my mother." She came back. "The fifteenth. That's twelve days. You called me Sunday. You told me the Open was full."

"It was full."

"Why didn't you ask me?"

"Because you'd have said no, and I had already decided."

"That's the reason?"

"That's all of it."

"Did anybody tell you to?"

"Ray. On the fifteenth."

"Then he's got more sense than you."

A monitor alarmed somewhere behind Kate and stopped.

"You're not doing the voice."

"No."

"So why are you calling me on a Saturday?"

"I want to put the rest in. I haven't decided. I'm asking you first."

"If I say yes?"

"It costs more than half of the rest."

"How much is more than half?"

"Judy writes the figure Monday morning."

"What's left?"

"One bad winter."

"One." A pen scratched at Kate's end. "And if I say no?"

"Arlene goes back on the list at eighty-four."

"The woman with the scanner."

"Yes."

"Is there anybody else?"

"Ray offered his name behind mine at the bank. I said no."

"Why?"

"Because then he's behind me again, and I asked him beside me."

"So it's the rest because you won't take his name."

"Yes."

Kate was quiet a long time. The hospital filled it for her: a page overhead, a door easing shut on its closer, somebody down a hall saying a name twice, the second time louder. Ellen did not fill it. She had filled every quiet she had ever been handed, on the job and off it, with the next item. She sat at the table with the phone at her ear and nothing in her other hand, and the baseboard ticked, and she waited, and did nothing else.

"Scale of one to ten."

"Ten."

"That one I believe."

Kate breathed out, close to the phone, and when she spoke it was fast again. "I'm on till three. Let me sleep on it. And I'm going to want something for it."

The line went dead.
