# Cold reader notes: Chapter 9, "The D-side"

## Above the score: "I'd keep reading, but…"

I did not quit inside this chapter. I'd keep reading, but only because it is clearly the second-to-last chapter and I want to see the K lit, not because I'm unsure what happens. This is the storm chapter, the low point the whole book has been building toward. On the page it plays mostly as logistics: clock times, squad counts, a briefing read out twice, and a line of refunds. Every cost it lands was priced out loud in Chapter 8 ("One storm takes that"). Ray, the other lead, drops out of the second half of his own dark moment. He waits in the truck during Arlene, and his drive home with Ellen after the loss gets one summary sentence.

The point where I came closest to putting it down was the radar scene's third paragraph (about 25% in): "Wednesday through Friday the forecast had said a few inches Saturday morning, over by noon, and on that she had kept both evening squads. On Friday night, with page two of the terms in her hand, she had held Sunday's empty lanes spare anyway. Four squads, thirty teams." I skimmed from there to Dale picking up the phone.

## 1. Pull score: 4 / 10

The Kate call at the end is the best scene in the chapter and gives me a real hook ("I'm going to want something for it"). It lifts a chapter whose first three-quarters are scheduling, and whose outcome (Kate's money, a squeaked-through finish, the K, the key) I can already see.

## 2. Where I got bored

- **About 25%, radar scene:** "Wednesday through Friday the forecast had said a few inches Saturday morning … Four squads, thirty teams." This is schedule bookkeeping at the exact moment the storm should feel like weather. I stopped seeing the lake and started seeing a spreadsheet.
- **About 30%:** "She had the day laid out before he finished. First the morning up her hill on his word, then the building empty inside his hour, and after those, until he called it, the evening squads and Linda's final where they were." It's a plan I'll watch happen anyway, so I skimmed it.
- **About 40%:** "On lane two the Legion was finishing the half-past-ten squad, and their anchor was Judy's brother's boy, an Ostrowski, by the entry form Ellen had read." I could feel this being placed for later. It read as a plant, not a person.
- **About 45%, the readback:** Ray repeats the briefing nearly word for word. I read the same content twice in sixty seconds. The idea is lovely (he gives her the numbers exact), but the execution is a recital.
- **About 90%, Kate call:** "How much is more than half?" / "Fourteen hundred dollars…" / "What's left?" / "One bad winter." / "One." The scene stops being about a mother and daughter and becomes a budget check.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **"for the first time since the winter of 'eighty-four nobody had bowled under Linda's name on it."** The morning squads did bowl, in an Open the book has called "The Linda Gagnon Memorial Open" since Chapter 8. So what didn't happen? The final? The league's Saturday? I don't know what this sentence is mourning.
- **Did Ray bowl?** Chapter 8 set up Linda's day going by "with him on a settee, waiting his turn like anybody." Here "he had been in the pit when the morning went off." Was Gagnon & Son a morning team or an evening team? Did they get a refund envelope too? The most personal thing the storm could take from Ray goes unmentioned.
- **"He had said it at her counter on Monday: 'Wednesday…'"** Which Monday? Chapter 8 ended on Monday the 15th on Arlene's stair with "Not yet." The invitation happened off the page, and I had to work out that it was probably the 22nd.
- **"The camp."** I don't remember being told where Ellen lives. "Heats this camp through one bad winter" made me picture a campsite for a second before I settled on a lake cabin. And where is it? Ray drives her "up Kettle Road" to it, but her car stays "up here" at the alley, which is also on Kettle Road.
- **"Road agent, Kettle Lanes."** Who is calling whom? The call-sign order took me a beat.
- **"whatever Judy's committee puts on top of it Monday morning."** Puts on top of what? A fee? A higher bar? A guarantee? I can't tell what the committee can do to her.
- **Why every evening team took a refund.** She offered Sunday morning. I see four teams decline, each with a reason. Then "Fifteen envelopes had gone down Kettle Road." All fifteen? None of the local teams (the Legion, the Lakeview Diner) stayed for Sunday?

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **Fifteen out of fifteen refunds.** It's convenient for the deficit. Local teams who drive five minutes home would surely take Sunday morning. The page shows four out-of-towners with good reasons and then lets "fifteen envelopes" stand for everyone. Arlene, Denise and the Legion are locals the book has spent eight chapters on, and none of them is shown choosing.
- **The stakes are $1,400.** The emotional climax of the book rests on a sum smaller than a transmission repair. I followed the rule (only money that has "sat in my name since before I signed" counts), but a retired federal controller whose entire qualifying reserve is roughly $2,500 to $3,000, with "one bad winter" of heat left after it, is a number I didn't believe. It made the stakes feel small rather than desperate.
- **Ellen refusing Ray's name "behind" hers.** I understand it from Chapter 8 ("beside me"). Still, when the alternative is spending more than half of her daughter's last-resort money, it reads as pride. Kate says it for me: "Then he's got more sense than you." The page names this but never closes it. Why is "beside" worth Kate's money? Ellen answers with a rule ("I will not sign it back in") rather than a reason I can feel.
- **Under pressure, did choices follow from what people knew?** Mostly yes. Ellen watches the band's middle, not its edge (she learned from December). She calls Dale before the forecast. She asks the D-side for his read and sends people home. All of it is consistent and sensible. That's part of the problem: nobody makes a mistake, so nothing goes wrong that anyone chose. The storm does all the work.
- **Ray offering his name, then dropping it after one exchange ("It might carry them." / "It might.").** This is the man who stood on her step to apologize a week ago. He gives up in two lines and drives off to wait in a truck.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **The ATC conceit is at saturation.** "Relief briefing." "I want you on the position." "At the Center they call it the D-side." "Read it back to me." "Readback correct." "Road agent, Kettle Lanes." "Give me your call before the forecast's." Every chapter title and most key beats run through the same metaphor. By Chapter 9 I could predict the jargon before it arrived.
- **Ellen counts the room again:** "She counted the room from the counter: a hundred and fifty, with children in it." She did this in Chapter 1 and Chapter 3. It's her move in every crisis, and now it's a tic.
- **Denise's run-on voice is the same template a third time:** "Counter needs somebody, and is that me, no it is not, I've got the fryer." Compare Chapter 4 ("and does he care, no… and are they fine") and Chapter 7 ("and did he sound sorry about it, he did not"). Same construction, same rhythm.
- **Feeling explained after a gesture:** "He had rounded every number she ever gave him. He gave her these exact, in the low voice she had worked whole midnights hoping to hear on a frequency." The second half over-explains a moment the readback already earned.
- **Stock phrasing:** "She had not known she was hungry until the first spoonful."
- **Scenes ending on neat lines, all of them:** "Call it to me." / "You'll have it." … "He was already moving down the catwalk." … "Nobody ever refunded me for a snowstorm either." followed by "He went out, and he had not said no to Sunday." (the narrator explains the callback) … "That I'm under." … "I've been packed since September." … "The line went dead."
- **Callback stacking:** "Nobody ever refunded me for a snowstorm either" (from Chapter 3), "Scale of one to ten" (Chapter 5), "the passenger seat she had put herself in once in January without being in it" (Chapter 5), "Judy called the committee a formality" (Chapter 6), "I skied down for the patrol once." Each is fine alone. Together they make the chapter feel assembled from earlier parts.
- **Everyone talks in the same clipped declaratives.** Hollis: "Ice I can call. The roads are Dale's." Dale: "Roads hold till the middle of the afternoon. After that they go. I'll call the hour." Kate: "Go." "One." "What's it cost? Both ways." Ellen: "I call Kate." "That I'm under." Cover the tags and I can't tell Hollis from Dale.
- **"Ellen knew six true sentences that would fill the quiet, and she said none of them."** Counting as a stand-in for feeling. The number "six" is the narrator showing its work.
- **"She had said harder things on a frequency, and could not think of one."** Neat, but the same shape as a dozen earlier lines.

## 6. Did the storm cost anyone something I had not priced in?

No. That's the main finding for this chapter.

- **The deficit:** priced. Chapter 8 said it outright: "With every lane of the Open full, I clear by six hundred dollars. One storm takes that." Chapter 6 planted "Cash back if weather stops play." The supper scene put the forecast on the radio and had the characters "not mention it."
- **Kate's money:** priced. It's been on the table since Chapter 2 ("Not the rest." / "I swear.") and was named again in Chapter 8 ("There's money I swore to my daughter. It isn't in this."). The moment Ellen said that at Arlene's table, I knew Chapter 9 would end with her calling Kate.
- **Arlene's bed:** priced since Chapter 1.
- **Ray:** the storm should have cost him his first Linda's day as a bowler instead of a coach. The page doesn't tell me whether he bowled, and doesn't show him feeling anything about it beyond standing "with his back to the lanes."
- **The romance:** costs nothing. Nothing between them is at risk tonight. There is no fight and no doubt, and the one private stretch they get (the drive) is summarized.
- **Unpriced, but small:** the walk-ons. The Lowell team's new creased green shirts, the Saco woman who "drove up here to hold her cup one time," the boy holding "the bag with their one ball in it against his chest." These were the only moments in the storm that hurt, and they belong to strangers.

## 7. What the page has already given away

- Ray ordered the K from Laconia "billed to himself the first of March" and hasn't told her (Chapter 8). The last image will be the sign reading Kettle Lanes.
- The key: Ellen offered it on the stair and he said "Not yet." It's in her vest all through this chapter. He'll take it back once she's clear, or once he's beside her.
- Ray let go of his credit-union loan, so he can't buy the building now. If she misses, it's the bank sale and the condo man.
- Linda's final moves to Sunday at one, on the lanes Ellen held. Gil Arsenault is "through to Sunday."
- Ellen will put in the rest. The chapter all but says so: "I want to put the rest in." Kate's "Ten." / "That one I believe" tells me Kate will say yes.
- Judy's nephew watched Ellen send 150 people home and refund every team. He's the witness who will carry that to Judy before Monday's committee.
- Danny takes the store now that Ray is "clean out of her paper."
- Locker fourteen and its padlock (Chapter 1, Chapter 3: "I've got the key. I don't use it.") is the last unopened plant.

## 8. What I think is going on (prediction for Chapter 10)

Kate says yes, but her price ("I'm going to want something for it") is that she drives up for Sunday and/or that Ellen takes Ray's name beside or behind her after all. My best guess is Kate shows up at the alley Sunday and meets Ray. I'm about 70% on that. Linda's final bowls Sunday at one. Ray bowls in it rather than coaching, and he opens locker fourteen first: Linda's ball or shoes, or her name on something. I'm 60% on the locker opening this chapter. Gil Arsenault gets his second look, and Denise wins the cup, or Ray does with Denise on his team (50/50 on who). Monday, Judy's committee approves despite the shortfall, swung by Kate's money plus the nephew's account of the refunds and a winter shown to be "winning." I'm 75% that she clears or is approved by a hair rather than missing outright. The K is lit at the end, Ray takes the key back, and the last line is about all ten lanes going or the sign. I'm 85% on the happy ending. I'd be surprised only if the committee says no and the book ends with Ray and Ellen running the place together under a different arrangement. I'd give that 15%, and it would be the more interesting book.

## 9. What I want to know next

- What does Kate want for it? This is the one real question the chapter leaves me with, and it's a good one.
- Does Ray bowl on Linda's day, and what is in locker fourteen?
- Will Ray say anything to Ellen about tonight? He has said almost nothing to her since the briefing.

That's all of it. Whether Ellen clears doesn't pull me, because the book has shown me the shape of the ending since Chapter 8. Whether they end up together doesn't pull me either, because the book settled that on the stair.

## 10. The listener pass (heard as text-to-speech)

- **"D-side" aloud sounds like "decide."** "D-side. Your read." will be heard as "Decide. Your read." Maybe that's intended, but a listener can't tell, and "The D-side" as a chapter title will be heard as "The Decide."
- **Clock-time pileup in scene 2 and onward:** ten to four, ten past four, a quarter past four, five, half past ten, half past twelve, twenty to two, four o'clock, a quarter to four, six. A listener can't hold this many times, and I lost track of which deadline mattered.
- **The briefing and readback.** Read aloud, the same block of logistics plays twice back to back, then a third tail. A listener will think the track skipped back.
- **Numbers read in full:** "December of two thousand and nine." "Four squads, thirty teams." "a hundred and fifty." "Fifteen envelopes." "Fourteen hundred dollars under." "the fifteenth… That's twelve days." The money exchange with Kate in particular is a run of figures in the middle of an emotional call.
- **"Road agent, Kettle Lanes."** Heard aloud, it sounds like Ellen introducing herself as the road agent.
- **Unclear speakers in the Kate call.** A long run of untagged one-word lines ("One." "Is there anybody else?" "Yes.") broken by narration. Once the voice loses the alternation, the listener can't recover it. "Yes. I would have." then "The wrapper went down on something hard." Is that Kate's wrapper? Yes, but I had to think about it.
- **Run of identical shapes in the refund line:** "The captain from Lowell had three men…" / "A woman from Saco came next…" / "After her came a father from Massachusetts…" / "Dale came last…" Same structure four times in a row, which aloud sounds like a list.
- **Skippable with eyes, not aloud:** "their anchor was Judy's brother's boy, an Ostrowski, by the entry form Ellen had read." The voice has to read every word of a tag I'd skip.
- **"Hamburg"** (meaning ground beef). A TTS voice may stress it like the city. Fine for New Englanders, odd for everyone else.

## 11. The romance

Yes, in the first scene, and then it goes quiet.

- "What do you make the second night?" / "That one." I laughed.
- "Heard you being early." / "One minute." A small smile.
- "It's a good supper," she said. He looked at her for a second across the table. Then he got up and took her bowl to the stove. This is the warmest moment in the chapter. She can't say it, so she asks for seconds, and he understands.
- The stocking foot against her knee under the oilcloth, and "The table was built for two people who didn't mind knees." Good.
- "I want you on the position. Today and after, whatever Monday says." Under the jargon it's a proposal, and I felt it.

After that, nothing. Once the storm hits, Ray is a pair of hands and a read ("I'd send them home"). After the loss they get four lines ("Then put my name behind yours…" / "Beside me, I said…"). He drives her to Arlene's and waits in the truck. The drive up Kettle Road (dash lit green, heater on her boots, the hockey game, the passenger seat she once imagined herself in) is the scene I most wanted to watch, and it's one summary sentence at the top of another scene. The book pays off the January daydream offstage.
