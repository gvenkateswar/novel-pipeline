# Chapter 7: Loss of Separation

The entry box sat by the register, a shoebox with a slot cut in the lid and a strip of her tape across the front that said ENTRIES. Ellen came in from the mailbox at the road with nothing for the Open and lifted the lid. Seven envelopes lay in the bottom, the same seven as last night. She knew the number and put the lid back on.

She worked Ray's gloves loose and pushed them into her vest pocket, and one cuff stayed standing up out of it.

Denise was laying singles into the drawer for league, and she spoke without turning round.

"Gil Arsenault called at two, from Pine Ridge, and was this the one Linda used to win, he wants to know, and I said no, it's a new cup with nobody on it yet, and he said then he'd stay home, thank you, Mrs. Lavoie, and did he sound sorry about it, he did not, and I said to him—"

She stopped. She reached up and turned a fry basket on its hook so the handle faced the wall, and left her hand on it, and did not go on.

Ellen had worked this counter beside Denise since the first of December and had never heard her leave a sentence lying. She could hear Gil in it as plainly as if he had called the office himself: a man who had made up his mind before he dialed and phoned anyway to hear it agreed with, polite to the last word, with nothing behind the thank-you. She could hear Denise too, at the place where she had stopped, her voice already climbing toward whatever she had said to him next and coming back down without it. Ellen didn't ask. She knew where the seven belonged. Hollis had put them there on the ice with a fish in his hand: the ones who were coming anyway. She knew every hand on those envelopes. Each came from somebody who already had a Tuesday or a Thursday in this building, or a brother-in-law who did. Not one had come across the lake, or from Halsey Falls, or from anybody who had ever bowled against Linda Gagnon and wanted another look at her. Hollis had told her so in one sentence, and she had built her Sunday around his sentence anyway, and ten days of mail had come up the drive to bear him out, and now Pine Ridge had borne him out by telephone, without being asked. She had told Ray on the ice that she would go to the people the name belonged to. For ten days she had let the mailbox go for her.

"Next Tuesday, after league, I'd like you to stay. I'm asking Hollis and Verna too. You were her friend longest."

Denise took her hand down off the basket. "I know what you're going to ask me, and I already told you what I think, and I'll stay."

Verna, at the scorer's table on lane one, said that the secretary would be available, and Hollis, at the shoe cubbies, asked what time, and Ellen told him twenty to ten.

Ray was at the house-ball rack between five and six, in ahead of his team, putting the balls in order lightest to heaviest. He weighed each one a moment in his palm before he seated it, though he had known every ball on that rack longer than she had owned the building. She stood at the end of the rack and watched his hands do five of them before she spoke. They were big hands, careful with anything small, and they were what she had wanted to look at all day.

On the sixth his eyes went to her vest pocket, where his own glove stood up at the cuff, and stayed there as long as it took to seat the ball. Then they went back to the rack. Neither of them mentioned it.

"Next Tuesday, after league, I'm asking Denise and Hollis and Verna to stay at the snack bar," she said. "I'm going to ask them about Linda's name."

He seated the ball. "Numbers said otherwise, then."

"Seven envelopes in ten days. Gil Arsenault is staying home."

He took the next ball down and said nothing, and she heard it go in the quiet before he spoke, as she had once heard a regular voice go wrong in her headset: the long voice that went the long way round, *and* after *and* with the best word saved for last, came back shorter.

"So you're going round me."

"I'm going to them, with you in the room."

"Same thing, Mrs. Daly."

He seated the ball and reached for the next. He hadn't said whether he would be there. She hadn't asked, and her voice had been perfectly even the whole time, and she had heard that too.

Dale Hutchins had come up the walkway for a house ball for the Highway Department and had heard the last two lines. He took one off the heavy end of the rack, looked at the two of them, and said it to her.

"Put me in that room too."

\* \* \*

Denise had put the chairs up on every table in the snack bar but one, legs in the air, for the mop, so the six of them came to one table built for four and nobody could sit apart. Verna had the head of it, with no binder for once and her pocketbook on her knees. Hollis sat at her left with his elbows on the table. Dale sat across from him, his jacket zipped to leave and his truck keys on the table in front of him. Ray had come in last, from coaching, and taken the last chair, at the foot nearest the lanes, with his bag at his feet and the league clipboard flat under his hand. Denise sat on the stool at the near end of her counter with her back to the fryer and the mop leaning beside her. There was no chair down for Ellen. She stood at the corner of the table nearest the counter, as she would have anyway, and past Ray's shoulder all ten lanes were still lit with nobody on them.

"I'd like the Memorial open to anyone who enters, on its own Saturday, with Linda's name on the cup. I have two questions. Whose is her name to give? And would Linda have given it?"

It came out level and pleasant and in order, at the size the room needed and no bigger, and under it she was going a great deal faster: Ray's hand on the clipboard and the clipboard not moving, Dale's keys, the clasp of Verna's pocketbook, the lit lanes behind Ray's head, who at the table would look at her first and who at him. She noticed that she had made her voice pleasant. Of all her voices it was the one she trusted least.

Nobody answered. Ray's hand stayed where it was.

Denise came down off the stool.

"I told her don't you touch it. I told her at this counter. Then Gil Arsenault calls me up and asks was it Linda's, and I heard myself want him to drive over here and lose to me. Linda entered every open with a cup on it from here to Lewiston, and you know she did, Ray. I want it to mean something when I take it."

It had no laugh round the edge of it, and it went straight down the table to Ray and stayed there. She sat back on the stool and put one hand on the mop handle and kept it there.

Ellen had brought Denise to the table for the other side. Denise had come for her own, and Ellen had never once thought to put that in her figures.

Verna had left the binder at home. She gave it from memory, in her voice for rulings.

"The secretary recalls the first open this house ever ran, the winter of nineteen eighty-four. The first name on the sheet was Linda Bouchard's, as she was then."

Hollis kept his eyes on Ray and asked it flat, with no change in his voice at all.

"Did anybody ask Linda?"

Ray didn't answer. Ellen watched his hand on the clipboard, and the clipboard did not move.

She could end it. She had the sentence ready, and she could hear how it would sound before she said it: *I'll withdraw the second question.* She would say it kindly, and the room would let its breath go, and Hollis would take his elbows off the table, and Ray could lift his hand off the board and say whatever he liked, or nothing, and go home to his quiet house with his wife's name still his. She knew the exact voice she would use. She had used it on frightened people all her working life, and it had never once failed her. She kept it where it was. She stood at the corner of the table with the sentence ready in her mouth and watched the man she wanted sit silent in front of the people who had known him longest, because the silence was doing her asking for her, and she let it go on. She had worked beside people who let a silence do their work for them. She had never thought well of a single one. The clipboard stayed where it was, and so did she.

Dale spoke to the table. His voice had the scrape in it of a man who had been on the radio to his plow drivers since before light, and he didn't clear it for her.

"She ran league past my call and charged me for a snowstorm, and now she wants Linda's cup for the same reason."

Under the scrape it was steady. He had been saying it to himself on his route since December, and he had it word-perfect.

Ellen kept her eyes on him.

"I had the three o'clock forecast and your call at ten past three. I went with the forecast, because for thirty-one years the forecast was what you went with. You were right. I was wrong."

She had never said the last two sentences aloud to anyone whose good opinion she wanted, and she said them now in the voice she had used for the forecast, with Ray at the foot of the table hearing every word go past him.

"Still no."

Dale said it the way he had said the rest. He picked nothing up and put nothing down. She had paid it at full price in front of Ray, and it bought nothing. Not one face at the table changed.

Verna spoke into the gap.

"The secretary notes that nobody has asked the secretary to take minutes, and the secretary is grateful."

Hollis laughed once, a short sound through his nose, and stopped. Ray didn't. Ellen didn't.

Verna laid her hand flat on the clasp of her pocketbook.

"The secretary finds the Memorial's terms have been the coach's since the first one was bowled."

Verna looked at Ray first, since she had made it his. Then Hollis, who had been looking at him all along. Then Dale, across the table, with his hand beside his keys. Then Denise from her stool, with the mop at her side. Ray took the faces as they came, one and the next round the table, and Ellen's was the last one he reached.

"Take it," he said, to the room.

His voice had gone short at the rack a week ago. Now there was nothing left in it but the two words.

He stood in the same breath. "And somebody else can coach it."

He set the clipboard down on the table, square to the edge, and took his hand off it.

She knew what was on it. The lane draw for Linda's Memorial was on it, all ten lanes, drawn at Thanksgiving in his square hand, and she had written that down herself off the radio the afternoon he told her so. He had coached this league for thirty years, and it was lying on the table between Hollis's elbows and Dale's keys.

Hollis looked at the clipboard and left it where it was. Dale stood up, took his keys off the table, and went out through the vestibule without a word. Ray picked up his bag and went down the walkway toward the lanes, under all that light, away from every one of them.

Denise was off her stool and after him before the vestibule door had swung shut behind Dale.

"Ray, I didn't—"

\* \* \*

Ellen put the lanes out at the toggles, two at a time, from ten back toward one. Each pair went dark the full length of the maple to the pits, and the dark came up the floor toward the counter a pair at a time. Denise went first, with the mop put away and her coat buttoned wrong. Verna went with her pocketbook under her arm. Hollis went last and held the vestibule door a second longer than anybody needed it held. When Ellen put out one and two, nothing in the building was lit but the lamp over the register. From there she could see the snack-bar table and the clipboard on it, square to the edge where he had left it. She didn't go to it.

The machines had been off since the last box. She had bought this building to hear all ten going at once, and it made no sound at all.

Ray hadn't gone. He stood at the counter in his jacket, zipped, looking down the dark lanes, and he had been standing there since Denise came back up the walkway by herself. Ellen came round and stood beside him and looked the same way. His shoulder was an inch from hers. She knew it was an inch, and she stood with her weight even on both feet, and neither of them closed it.

"Thank you."

It came out level and warm, and it was true. She heard how it sounded in a dark building at the end of a night like this one, like a woman signing for a delivery, and underneath it everything she had was running at once: the clipboard on the table, Dale's back going out through the vestibule, Denise's voice breaking off on the walkway, his hand coming up off the board, the inch. She kept the voice where it was. It was the only place she had ever been able to say anything from.

"There. Now you'll clear, and I'll lose the building, and you can stop wondering what my no was worth."

It was the long voice, the one with the *and*s in it, and it was the first time she had heard it carry nothing she wanted.

"I never wondered."

That was true too. She had known what his no was worth the minute he said it at his own counter, and she had gone round it with him in the room, which he had told her was the same thing. It was.

"I would like to tell you what it cost me to ask you."

She heard it come out formal, each word given its whole length, which was how she sounded when she was most afraid, and she went on.

"At your counter, that was the first time I have ever—"

"You sounded fine on the radio that night too, I bet."

He said it low, with no heat in it, looking down the dark lanes, in the voice she had liked best of any she had heard in her life. He had picked it. She could hear that he had picked it, the way she had always been able to hear a man who had been rehearsing his sentence for the last ten miles. It was short and accurate and aimed. She had handed it to him herself, in the pit, with a pin in her hands, and he had kept it since December and brought it out now. It went on landing after he stopped. It landed on the table behind them and the clipboard on it, and on the thank-you she had just said to him in that same voice, and it kept on.

She had lost separation once, at two in the morning, and nobody on the frequency could have told it from her voice, and tonight she had stood at that table while every face in the room turned to him and kept her voice exactly where it was.

She had watched it close. She had seen Hollis's question go down the table to him and the silence take hold, and Verna's ruling put it all on one chair, and each face come round to him in turn, and she had known where it was going from the moment Denise sat back down, as plainly as she had ever watched two slow lights draw toward each other on a dark screen, and she had stood there with her sentence ready and her voice pleasant and let them come together. Nobody at the table could have told. Nobody had. He was the one person in the building who knew what her calm was, because she had told him, and he had just told it back to her. It was the same morning Kate kept a name for, and he had found it without knowing it had one. He was right, and she had known he was right while she stood at the table, and she had kept her voice where it was anyway.

*Understood.* It came up in her ready to go, in the voice, the one word she had always had for this, and she heard what it would sound like to him, and did not say it.

Beside her Ray took his ring out of his jacket pocket. He worked a key off it with his thumbnail, without hurrying, a turn at a time round the split ring. It was her front key, the one she had laid on his counter on the third of December, with no tag on it. Arlene's old key stayed on the ring with its soft orange tag, dead in her door since the morning he rekeyed it. He laid her key on her counter and took his hand away, and said nothing, as she had said nothing at his.

He went out the front. She heard the inner door and then the outer one, and through the glass she saw him stop on the granite step under the outside light, with the dark lot beyond him. She wanted him to stay on the step one more time, as she had wanted it the first night, with an argument in her she hadn't finished. He went down it to the truck.

She picked up the key. She held it up to the hook by the register where the spares hung, and didn't hang it. She thought of the office desk, whose drawer already had his signature in it. She opened the register drawer and looked at the key in her palm over the till, and shut the drawer again. None of them would do. She put it in her own vest pocket and zipped the pocket shut over it.
