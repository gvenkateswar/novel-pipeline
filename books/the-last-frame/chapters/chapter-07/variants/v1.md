# Chapter 7: Loss of Separation

The mailbox at the road had held a fuel-oil statement, a hardware flyer and nothing for the Open. Ellen came up the drive with them against the wind off the lake, dropped them on the counter, worked Ray's gloves from her hands and pushed them into her vest pocket, and lifted the lid of the entry box.

It was a boot box with a slot cut in the lid and ENTRIES across it on a strip of her tape. Seven envelopes lay in the bottom, the same seven, each with a team's name on the front in a hand that was not hers. She did not count them. She had the number already, and a second count would only give it to her again more slowly.

Denise spoke from the snack-bar register across the entrance, where she was rolling quarters into paper for the bank, and she kept her eyes on the quarters while she did it.

"Gil Arsenault called at two, from Pine Ridge, and was this the one Linda used to win, he wants to know, and I said no, it's a new cup with nobody on it yet, and he said then he'd stay home, thank you, Mrs. Lavoie, and did he sound sorry about it, he did not, and I said to him—"

She stopped. She set the roll of quarters down, reached up and turned a fry basket on its hook a quarter turn, and turned it back, and left her hand on the handle.

The nameless Open was filling the way Hollis had said it would out on the ice, with the ones who were coming anyway, and there were seven of them.

Ellen put the lid back on the box and went across to the snack bar.

"Next Tuesday, after league, I'd like you to stay. I'm asking Hollis and Verna too. You were her friend longest."

Denise took her hand off the basket. "I know what you're going to ask me, and I already told you what I think, and I'll stay."

Ellen asked Verna at the scorer's table on lane one and Hollis at the shoe cubbies, and Verna said the secretary would be available, and Hollis asked what time.

Ray was in ahead of his team, at the house-ball rack between lanes five and six, squaring the balls lightest to heaviest. He took each one up in one hand and turned it until he had found whatever he looked for in its scuffs, and set it down in its place, and took up the next. Ellen came down the walkway and stood across the rack from him and watched his hands at it for longer than the job needed watching. They were big hands, and slow with the small heavy balls, and they set each one down without a sound.

He looked up. His eyes stopped at her vest, where the cuff of his own glove stood up out of the pocket, and stayed there for one look, and came away. Neither of them said a word about it.

"Next Tuesday after league," she said, "at the snack bar, I am asking Denise, Hollis and Verna two questions about Linda's name."

Ray set a ball down. He said nothing for so long that she thought he would let it go by, and took up the next ball and turned it in his hand. When his voice came it had gone short, and she heard that before she heard the words.

"Numbers said otherwise, then."

"Seven envelopes in ten days. Gil Arsenault is staying home."

He racked the ball. "So you're going round me."

"I'm going to them, with you in the room."

"Same thing, Mrs. Daly."

He did not stop. The next ball went into its place, and the one after it, lightest to heaviest, and his hands were as careful with them as they had been before she spoke.

Dale Hutchins had come to the rack for a house ball for the Highway Department and had stood there through the last two lines. He took his ball off the heavy end, looked at Ray, and then at her, and said it to her.

"Put me in that room too."

\* \* \*

Denise had the chairs up on every table but one for the mop, so six people came to a table built for four, and nobody could sit apart. Verna had the head of it, with no binder for once and her pocketbook on her knees. Hollis sat at her left with his elbows on the table. Across from him Dale Hutchins had his jacket zipped to leave and his truck keys on the table in front of him. Denise sat on her end stool, turned round with her back to the fryer, and the mop leaned on the counter at her side. One chair was left, at the foot, nearest the lanes. No chair was down for Ellen. She stood at the corner of the table nearest the counter, and she would have stood for this anyway.

Ray came in last, from coaching. He came up the walkway from lane ten with the league clipboard under his arm and the night's last string bowled behind him, looked at the five of them and at the one chair, and sat in it. He laid the clipboard flat on the table and kept his hand on it. His bowling bag went down at his feet. Hollis's eyes went to him and stayed there.

"I'd like the Memorial open to anyone who enters, on its own Saturday, with Linda's name on the cup. I have two questions. Whose is her name to give? And would Linda have given it?"

She said it in her voice for telling a stranger the way to Laconia, level and courteous, and under the voice she was taking in everything at once: the inch between Ray's fingers and the edge of the clipboard, how long the quiet had run already, and how many years longer than her every face at the table had known Linda.

Nobody answered.

Ray's hand stayed on the clipboard, the fingers spread on it as though a wind might take it. He did not look at her. His thumb moved once along the edge of the clip and stopped. Verna's thumbs moved once on the clasp of her pocketbook and were still. Dale looked at his keys. Hollis looked at Ray and went on looking. Out past the trophy case the lanes were still lit, all ten, with the pins standing at the far end of every one and nobody to throw at them, and nobody at the table said anything into the quiet, and nobody got up.

Denise came down off her stool. Her knees took it slowly, and she stood on the tile with the counter at her back.

"I told her don't you touch it. I told her at this counter. Then Gil Arsenault calls me up and asks was it Linda's, and I heard myself want him to drive over here and lose to me. Linda entered every open with a cup on it from here to Lewiston, and you know she did, Ray. I want it to mean something when I take it."

She said the last of it straight down the table at Ray, and he did not lift his eyes to take it. Hollis turned his head and looked at her, and turned it back. Denise stood a moment longer on the tile with her hands at her sides. Then she went back up on the stool and put her hand on the mop handle and kept it there.

Ellen had brought Denise for the other side, and Denise had come for her own.

Verna unfolded her hands. She had nothing on the table to open, and she looked at the middle of it anyway, as though the page were there, and spoke to that.

"The secretary recalls the first open this house ever ran, the winter of nineteen eighty-four. The first name on the sheet was Linda Bouchard's, as she was then."

Nobody at the table needed telling who Linda Bouchard had been. Denise's hand closed on the mop handle and opened again. Dale's jaw worked once. Verna folded her hands on the pocketbook and added nothing; the secretary had recalled what she recalled.

Hollis did not take his elbows off the table. He spoke to Ray down the length of it, in the voice he would give an ice depth.

"Did anybody ask Linda?"

Ray did not answer.

His hand lay on the clipboard, and the clipboard did not move, and Ellen watched it not move. Hollis waited with his eyes on him. Dale's keys lay where they lay. Verna's hands were folded. Denise sat by her mop. The lights went on burning over the empty lanes, and Ray looked at the table in front of him, at nothing on it, and the quiet went down the table and past the foot of it and out over all ten lanes and did not come back with anything.

She knew the sentence that would end it, and that it was hers to say, and she let the quiet run.

Dale looked at the clock over the vestibule door as though he had a shift to get to. Then he put one finger on his truck keys and spoke to the table.

"She ran league past my call and charged me for a snowstorm, and now she wants Linda's cup for the same reason."

He took the finger off his keys.

She had never given him her reason, that night or since. She had written herself up once in her life, on paper, for people she would never meet, and that had been easier than this, with Ray at the foot of the table. She gave it to Dale in the same level voice, and heard every word of it go on down the table past him.

"I had the three o'clock forecast and your call at ten past three. I went with the forecast, because for thirty-one years the forecast was what you went with. You were right. I was wrong."

Dale looked at her for the first time since he sat down.

"Still no."

He said it as he had said the rest, as a thing already on the record, and looked back down at his keys. They stayed where they were. Hollis's elbows stayed where they were. Ray's hand had not moved on the clipboard for any of it, and Denise's had not moved on the mop, and Ellen stood at her corner with it said, and the table was as it had been before she opened her mouth.

Verna cleared her throat.

"The secretary notes that nobody has asked the secretary to take minutes, and the secretary is grateful."

Hollis laughed. It came out of him once, out loud, a short bark that went across the snack bar and out over the lanes, and he put a hand flat on the table as though he could press it back in. Verna did not look at him. Nobody joined him. Ray did not laugh, and Ellen did not, and the laugh stopped where it was.

Verna waited until the table was quiet again. It did not take long. She lifted her chin the small distance she lifted it for a ruling.

"The secretary finds the Memorial's terms have been the coach's since the first one was bowled."

Then they looked at Ray, one at a time, in the order they sat. Verna looked first, down the length of the table over her folded hands. Hollis had been looking at him all along and did not stop now. Dale looked up from his keys and kept his face plain. Denise, on her stool by the mop, looked at him with her mouth pressed shut. Ray lifted his eyes off the clipboard and went round them as they sat, Verna and Hollis and Dale, and across to Denise on her stool, one face and then the next, taking each one as long as it took, and came last to Ellen, standing at her corner, and stayed on her.

Then he looked away from her, at all of them.

"Take it."

He said it to the table and the five of them at it and the lit lanes behind them. He was on his feet before anyone could answer, the chair going back on the tile, and the rest came in the same breath.

"And somebody else can coach it."

Nobody said anything. Verna's hands came apart on the pocketbook. Ray set the league clipboard on the table, square to the edge, and took his hand off it.

Ellen knew what was on that clipboard. It held the lane draw for Linda's Memorial, all ten lanes, drawn at Thanksgiving in his hand, and she had written it down off the radio herself. He had carried a clipboard into this building every league night for thirty years.

Hollis looked at the clipboard and did not pick it up.

Dale stood, took his truck keys off the table, and went out through the vestibule without a word. The outside door shut behind him. Verna's hands had gone back to the clasp of her pocketbook, and Hollis's elbows had not moved from the table.

Ray bent and took up his bag by its handles. He went down the walkway toward the lanes, past the trophy case and on into the light over the empty approaches, with the bag knocking once against his knee, and he did not look back, and nobody at the table said his name.

Denise came off her stool after him.

"Ray, I didn't—"

\* \* \*

She saw the last of them out. Denise went with her coat over her arm and her mouth shut, Verna went on Hollis's arm with the pocketbook under her other one, and Hollis held the outside door for her and looked back once at the table before he let it close.

Ellen went to the panel and put the lanes out.

She did it a pair at a time from the far end. Nine and ten went first, and the light went off the decks and the pins and the maple, and the settees at that end went dark. Then seven and eight, and the dark came a pair nearer. Then five and six, where the rack stood squared lightest to heaviest, and three and four, and one and two, and the last light came off the approaches and all ten lanes were dark. She had bought this building to hear all ten going at once, and nothing in it was going at all. The only light left was the one over the register.

Ray had not gone.

He stood at her counter with his back to it, in his jacket, zipped, facing down the dark lanes, and he had stood there the whole time she was putting them out. She came round from the panel and stood beside him and faced the way he faced. His shoulder was an inch from hers. Neither of them closed it. From where she stood she could see the snack-bar table at the edge of the register light, and the clipboard on it, square to the edge, where nobody had touched it.

"Thank you," she said.

She meant it. She said it calmly. Calm was how she got through anything, and it came out level and pleasant and finished, and under it she had everything running at once, his shoulder and the dark and Dale's keys going off the table and the clipboard and the sound his chair had made on the tile, and the voice she had just used, and what that voice would sound like to a man who had heard it before.

"There," Ray said. "Now you'll clear, and I'll lose the building, and you can stop wondering what my no was worth."

He said it down the dark lanes in one breath, joined up the way he joined everything, and it came out bitter at the far end.

"I never wondered."

It was a true sentence, and she had put nothing behind it.

He did not answer. Down the lanes nothing moved and nothing ran, and the building had gone so quiet that she could hear him breathing beside her, slow, like a man holding a sentence back.

She would have to ask before she told him. She had one voice for asking anything this hard, and it was the polite one.

"I would like to tell you what it cost me to ask you."

He did not stop her, and she went on.

"At your counter, that was the first time I have ever—"

"You sounded fine on the radio that night too, I bet."

He did not turn his head to say it. He said it quietly, to the dark, in a voice with nothing in it, and then he stood beside her and let it go on landing. His shoulder stayed where it had been, an inch away.

She had told him about that night herself, in the pit in the storm, with a pin in her hands. He had kept it since. He had kept it carefully, as he kept everything, and tonight he had taken it out and chosen it from everything else he might have said because it would land, and she could hear that he had chosen it; she had spent her working life hearing what was in a voice before she heard the words, and this one had found, without his knowing, the same morning Kate kept a name for.

She had lost separation once, at two in the morning, and nobody on the frequency could have told it from her voice, and tonight she had stood at that table while every face in the room turned to him and kept her voice exactly where it was.

*Understood.* The word came up in her ready, in the voice, and she heard it before she could say it, and she did not say it.

Ray took his keys out of his jacket pocket. He worked one off the ring with his thumbnail, turning it round the split steel until it came free, and it took him a while, because the ring was stiff and his nail slipped once: her front key, the one she had laid on his counter on the third of December, with no tag on it. Arlene's old key stayed on the ring with its soft orange tag, the key that had turned nothing in her door since the morning he rekeyed it. He turned round to the counter and laid hers on it, in the register light, and said nothing, as she had said nothing at his.

Then he went out the front.

Through the door glass she watched him on the granite step under the outside light, with his back to her and his breath going up white in the light. He stood there a moment as if he had forgotten something. She wanted him to stay on the step. She had wanted it the first night, with snow on his shoulders and an argument not half finished, and she wanted it now, one more time, long enough for her to find the next sentence. He went down the step and across the lot to his truck.

She picked up the key. It had been on his ring since December.

She looked at the hook by the register where the spares hung, each on its strip of her tape, and did not hang it there. She thought of the office desk drawer, which already had his signature in it, and did not go in. She opened the register drawer an inch and shut it again. Behind her, on the snack-bar table, the clipboard lay where he had squared it.

She put the key in her own vest pocket.
