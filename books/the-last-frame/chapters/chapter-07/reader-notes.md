# Reader Notes: Chapter 07, "Loss of Separation"

## Where I got bored

- Verna reading the 1987 minutes aloud: "Meeting of January thirteenth, 1987... Motion to rename the snack bar the Pin Stop. Moved, seconded, failed, fourteen to nine." It does set up the mid-season precedent. But I'm waiting to watch Ellen get torn apart, and the book pauses for a records search.
- The ninety-one's snack-bar rename motion ("who has now tried indoors"). It's a gag stacked on another gag, in the chapter that's meant to hurt most. Verna is fun. Here she lets the air out of the room right when it should be filling up.
- The opening paragraph of Ray's check speech ("Forty-eight of that pays Arlene's note down... Twenty-three thousand nine hundred fifty..."). He hands her a spreadsheet in place of a love scene. I skimmed it, then went back because I thought I was supposed to care about the numbers. I didn't.

## Where I got confused

- "Income, twenty-two. Cash, seventeen." Twenty-two of what, compared with which target? I had to dig back to Judy's two circles in Chapter 3 (thirty and twenty-five), and I'm still not confident I'm reading the gap right. The chapter's financial low point lands as shorthand.
- "Danny worked your income back from the cash you gave me and the interest you pay Arlene." What cash did she give Ray? Paying his invoices? I couldn't follow it.
- FEB 2, THEY SPLIT. Who bought that square, and how could they know? The breakup happened in a truck window about ninety minutes earlier. Then Denise says "He brought the sheet in Monday." I took "he" to mean Ray, which means the sheet is now at the lanes. That's a big gesture (he gave up his pool), and it's buried in a line of Denise's dialogue that I had to read three times.
- "The 2019 fuel-oil calendar lifted on its nail." I've never seen this calendar before, and it reads like something I should recognize.
- "Because you coach." Ray coaches Gagnon & Son? I'd half-registered it from Chapter 6. The phrase "the coach" in Verna's vote needed more groundwork.
- Chapter 6 ended on Hollis asking Ray "Purpose of visit," and nothing here picks that up.

## Where I stopped believing

- "We should stop. Effective now." Ellen ends a relationship as if she's handing over a position, and the narration says so: "She gave it to him in order." I believe she'd talk that way. I don't believe Ray takes it with "I've got a team." This is the man who shouted "I have been walking all over this town vouching for you!" one chapter ago. Now he folds the check into his coat and leaves without a word of protest.
- "If I take it, I'll never know whether I could have carried it. And you'll never know whether I wanted you or your money." It's too symmetrical. People don't produce a perfectly balanced two-clause thesis standing in a parking lot at fourteen degrees.
- The vote works out neatly: 11 to 8, Ray abstains, and exactly the two teams she'd already projected losing walk out. It feels arranged.

## Where it sounded like AI

- "He stood with that a long time." / "He let that go by." / "She kept her hands where they were." / "Her hands lay flat on the blotter. She looked at them to make sure, and they were fine." Every character has the same calm and the same habit of pausing.
- The key ring move comes back again: "turned the ring over once, so they rang against each other. Then he closed his fist on them." That's at least the third or fourth time. It's a tic now.
- "I want you. I want the alley. I've been trying since Sunday to pull those two apart, and I can't do it." It's on the nose. He says the theme out loud.
- "lit the two of them up like a lane." It's a bowling simile used only because the book is about bowling.
- The final line, headlights following her down the drive, is another quiet closing image. Every chapter ends with a car, a light, or a lock.

## What I want to know next

- Can the Open actually close a gap this size, now that two teams are gone?
- Did Ray really take the pool sheet off his wall, and why?
- Ray is still holding the right of first refusal. Does he want her to fail?
- Those questions do pull me forward. I'm less pulled toward the romance, because the breakup was so procedural that I don't feel anything needs repairing.

## Best moment

> Ray stood up. He came down the aisle between the settees toward the front, and the room watched him come, and Ellen did the arithmetic of a league without its coach before he was halfway. He walked on past the snack bar and the door and stopped at her left, on her side of the counter.
>
> "I don't agree with her," he said to the 112.

This is the best thing in the book so far. He disagrees with her and stands next to her anyway. It's earned, and nobody explains it.

The loss-of-separation backstory is a close second, especially "she couldn't get her key into the ignition. It took four minutes. She timed it."

## The three questions

**Does it feel dark?** Partly. The money is dark: she ends further from both of Judy's numbers than she started, and two teams are gone. The emotion isn't. The breakup is done in a briefing voice. Verna's comedy runs through the meeting. And the chapter ends with Ray protectively following her car, which is warm. I never felt Ellen hit the floor. The darkest passage (the near-miss at Logan) is backstory, told in summary.

**Does refusing Ray's money cost Ellen something I can feel?** I understand the cost. I don't feel it. The cost reaches me as a tape total ("Income, twenty-two. Cash, seventeen."), and I can barely read it. She gets no moment alone where turning down $55,000 and the man together catches up with her. The four-minute ignition story sets up exactly that moment, and then her car starts after a count of ten.

**Did the book finally raise its voice?** Yes, but through someone else. The Highway Department captain shouting "Nobody asked you to buy it!" from the top of a settee is the loudest and most alive moment in seven chapters. Ellen never raises hers. Her one emotional admission ("I ended it at your truck window like a shift briefing") is still a report.

## Verdict

The strongest set piece in the book, built around one great image of Ray standing at her left. But the dark moment is mostly arithmetic. The breakup is too tidy to hurt, the comic relief keeps undercutting the tension, and the stylistic tics (pauses, the key ring, closing images) are now plain to see. I'd keep reading for the plot. I'm not worried for either of them.
