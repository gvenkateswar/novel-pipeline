# Chapter 7: Loss of Separation

There was nothing for the Open in the mail. Ellen came in from the box at the road with the electric bill and a flyer for the Grange's bean supper, laid them by the register, and worked Ray's gloves down off her fingers and into her vest pocket.

The entry box sat at her elbow where it had sat since the derby committee said yes: a shoebox with a slot cut in the lid, and a strip of her tape across one end that said ENTRIES in black marker. She lifted the lid. Seven envelopes lay on the cardboard, the same seven, one of them addressed in pencil to Kettle Lanes, The Open, Ambrose, as though the town might have two. She knew the number without counting, and she put the lid back on.

Denise was at the register, breaking a roll of quarters against its edge, and she did not look up.

"Gil Arsenault called at two, from Pine Ridge, and was this the one Linda used to win, he wants to know, and I said no, it's a new cup with nobody on it yet, and he said then he'd stay home, thank you, Mrs. Lavoie, and did he sound sorry about it, he did not, and I said to him—"

She stopped. She reached past the register to the fryer and turned an empty basket on its hook until the handle faced the wall, and kept her hand on it.

Out on the ice, Hollis had said a cup with nobody on it would get the ones who were coming anyway, and there were seven of them.

"Next Tuesday, after league, I'd like you to stay," Ellen said. "I'm asking Hollis and Verna too. You were her friend longest."

Denise took her hand off the basket. "I know what you're going to ask me, and I already told you what I think, and I'll stay."

Verna, at the scorer's table on lane one with her binder already open to the night, said she would be available. Hollis, at the shoe cubbies, asked what time.

Ray was in ahead of his team, at the house-ball rack between five and six, putting the balls in order lightest to heaviest. He lifted each one off the rail, gave it a quarter turn under his thumb to see where it was worn, and set it down a place to the left or the right, letting the rail take it a half second before he let go. She stood at the end of the rack and watched his hands at it for longer than the job needed watching.

When he looked up, his eyes went to her vest. The cuff of his own glove stood up out of her pocket. He looked at it for as long as it took to set a ball down, and then he set one down.

"Next Tuesday, after league, at the snack bar," she said. "Denise, Hollis and Verna. I'm going to ask them two questions about Linda's name. I wanted you to hear it from me first."

He took the next ball off the rail, and turned it, and set it. He took the next. She waited through three of them, and on the third his thumb stayed on the ball after the rail had it.

"Numbers said otherwise, then."

"Seven envelopes in ten days. Gil Arsenault is staying home."

He set another one down. "So you're going round me."

"I'm going to them, with you in the room."

"Same thing, Mrs. Daly."

Dale Hutchins had come along the settees for a house ball for the Highway Department, still in his plow jacket, and he had been standing at the end of the rail for the last two lines. He took the heaviest ball out of Ray's new order, hefted it once, and looked from Ray to her.

"Put me in that room too."

\* \* \*

Denise had the chairs up on every table in the snack bar but one, legs in the air, the floor round them mopped and drying, so they all came to the one dry table, which was built for four, and nobody could sit apart from anybody. Ray came in last, from coaching, and took the chair at the foot, nearest the lanes, with his bowling bag at his feet and the league clipboard flat on the table under his hand. Past him all ten lanes were still lit, and nobody was on any of them.

There was no chair down for Ellen. She stood at the corner of the table nearest the counter, and she would have stood if there had been one. She had always given bad news standing.

"I'd like the Memorial open to anyone who enters, on its own Saturday, with Linda's name on the cup. I have two questions. Whose is her name to give? And would Linda have given it?"

She said it in the voice for giving a stranger directions, and under it she was watching the one face at the foot of the table and running ahead of it, to every answer he could give her and every one he might hold back, faster than she could keep up with herself.

Nobody answered. Ray's hand stayed on the clipboard, and he did not look up.

Denise was on the stool at the near end of her counter, her back to the fryer and her face to the table, the mop leaned on the counter beside her. She came down off it.

"I told her don't you touch it. I told her at this counter. Then Gil Arsenault calls me up and asks was it Linda's, and I heard myself want him to drive over here and lose to me. Linda entered every open with a cup on it from here to Lewiston, and you know she did, Ray. I want it to mean something when I take it."

Her voice had gone up at Lewiston and come down hard on his name. She got back up on the stool and took hold of the mop handle. Ray looked past all of them at the lit lanes.

Ellen had brought Denise for the other side, and Denise had come for her own.

Verna had the head of the table and, for once, no binder to open, and she did not look as if she needed one. Her hands lay folded on the clasp of the pocketbook on her knees. She spoke in the voice she kept for a ruling at the foul line.

"The secretary recalls the first open this house ever ran, the winter of nineteen eighty-four. The first name on the sheet was Linda Bouchard's, as she was then."

Across the room, in the case by lane one, Linda's card stood where it always stood, and nobody turned to look at it. Denise's hand tightened on the mop handle. Dale Hutchins sat with his plow jacket zipped to leave and looked down at the truck keys on the table in front of him.

Hollis sat across from Dale, at Verna's left, with his elbows on the table. He had not taken his eyes off Ray since Ray sat down, and he did not take them off him now, or change his voice to ask it.

"Did anybody ask Linda?"

Ray's eyes went to the lanes, to all that empty lit maple, and stayed there. He did not answer. Ellen watched his hand on the clipboard, and the clipboard did not move. One sentence from her, about the forecast or the derby or the price of fries, would have ended it for him. The quiet went on, and she let it.

Dale picked his keys up off the table and set them down again in the same place.

"She ran league past my call and charged me for a snowstorm, and now she wants Linda's cup for the same reason."

He said it to Verna, for the record, in the voice she had heard on the telephone in December, plain and certain, and tired at the bottom of it. "Put me down as no."

Ellen turned to him.

"I had the three o'clock forecast and your call at ten past three. I went with the forecast, because for thirty-one years the forecast was what you went with. You were right. I was wrong."

She had written those last three words once before, on a form, on a midnight shift, before anybody asked her to. She had said hard things to Ray's face, on lane one at seven in the morning, with nobody else on the floor. She had never said them to another man with Ray in the room. She said them to Dale, and at the foot of the table Ray heard every one.

Dale looked at her a while. Nothing moved in his face, and nothing moved on the table.

"Still no."

He covered his keys with his hand. Denise let go of the mop. Verna spoke into the gap.

"The secretary notes that nobody has asked the secretary to take minutes, and the secretary is grateful."

Hollis laughed once, through his nose, and his eyes went straight back to Ray. Ray didn't. Ellen didn't.

Verna let it stand a moment. Then she unfolded her hands and folded them again the other way.

"The secretary finds the Memorial's terms have been the coach's since the first one was bowled."

They looked at him. Verna looked first, down the length of the table from its head. Hollis was already looking and had been all night. Dale turned in his chair, and his zipped jacket creaked with it. Denise looked from her stool with her hand gone back to the mop. Ray's eyes went round them in the same order, one face and then the next, and came last to Ellen at her corner, and stayed on her as long as it took her to see that they would not stay.

"Take it."

He said it to the room. The two words went down the table, and nobody reached for them.

In the same breath he was on his feet, and the chair went back on the dry floor.

"And somebody else can coach it."

He lifted the clipboard off the table and set it down again, square to the edge, and took his hand off it. Verna's hands came apart on the pocketbook.

Ellen knew what was on it. The lane draw for Linda's Memorial, all ten lanes, drawn at Thanksgiving in his hand, and she had written it down herself off the radio on a Saturday of birthday parties, with his voice coming out of the speaker on her counter. Under the clip lay thirty years of Tuesdays and Thursdays.

Hollis looked at the clipboard and did not pick it up. Dale stood, took his keys off the table, and went round Ellen at her corner with his eyes on the door, out through the vestibule without a word, and the outer door shut behind him.

Ray picked up his bag and went down the walkway toward the lanes, away from all of them, under all that light with nobody bowling in it.

Denise was off her stool and two steps after him, and behind her the mop slid along the counter and went over on the floor.

"Ray, I didn't—"

\* \* \*

The far pair went out first. Ellen pushed the two toggles down under her own black numbers, and the far end of the house went dark from the pin decks back to the foul lines, and the two words came up in her as though he had only now said them.

*Take it.*

He had said them to the room.

Denise went out past her with her coat on over the polo and her car keys in her fist, and said good night to nobody. Ellen put out the next pair. Verna went with her pocketbook under her arm, and Hollis held the vestibule door for her and looked back once at the snack-bar table before he let it close behind them.

Ray had not gone. He had come back up the walkway with his bag while she was at the toggles, and he stood on the near side of her counter in his canvas jacket, zipped, with the bag at his feet, and did not say what he was waiting for.

She put out the next pair, and the next. The dark came up the house toward her a pair at a time, and each time it took the settees and the scorer's tables and the ball returns with it, and stopped, and waited on her hand. The rack between five and six went, with every ball on it in his order.

Her thumb rested on the last toggle a moment before she pushed it.

Then there was only the light over the register, a yellow circle on the counter and on her hands, and past it ten lanes of nothing. She had bought the building for the sound of all ten at once, and there was no sound in it anywhere. At the edge of the light she could see the snack-bar table, with the chairs still legs-up on the tables round it, and the clipboard where he had left it, square to the edge.

She came out round the counter and stood beside him, and they faced down the dark lanes together. His shoulder was an inch from hers. She knew the inch exactly. She did not close it, and neither did he.

"Thank you," she said.

It came out level and pleasant, and she heard it come out that way. Under it she was back at the table, fast, watching his hand lift the clipboard and set it down again, and the chair go back on the dry floor, over and over.

For a while he said nothing at all. Then Ray spoke, without turning his head. "There."

The word went off down the dark and nothing sent it back. His shoulder did not move. She waited for the rest of it, because with him there was always the rest, coming the long way round with the heavy word last, and she started to say his name and kept it, and he went on.

"Now you'll clear, and I'll lose the building, and you can stop wondering what my no was worth."

He said it a piece at a time, with the *and*s carrying it, and the last word came down where he had aimed it. She took it the same way. *Now you'll clear.* She had an answer to that, about Judy's committee and every week left between here and the end of February, and she did not say it. *I'll lose the building.* She had an answer to that too, the house she had sold in Merrimack to stand where she was standing, and she did not say that either. The last piece she could answer with nothing in the sentence but the truth.

"I never wondered."

It was true. She had known at his counter what it cost him to say no to her, and she had gone to his people anyway.

He did not go.

In the cellar the boiler came on, a soft thump under the floor and then its long low run, and he stood through it with the bag at his feet. He turned his head toward the snack-bar table and the clipboard on it, and held it there; then he turned it toward her, and held it there. His hand came up out of his jacket pocket. She watched it come. It went to his own jaw, rubbed once along the gray there, and went back down.

The boiler ran. If he was still beside her when it shut off, she would tell him. It ran on, and he was still there, and for the length of it she let herself believe that he would stay through whatever she said next and answer it in his own time, as he had answered everything she had ever put to him.

The boiler shut off. In the quiet after it she could hear him breathe.

"I would like to tell you what it cost me to ask you."

Her voice had gone more polite, and she heard that too. He did not say no. She took that for leave, and went on.

"At your counter, that was the first time I have ever—"

"You sounded fine on the radio that night too, I bet."

He said it low, with his face to the lanes, in the good voice, the one she had liked from the first night, and he did not raise it and did not hurry it. His shoulder had not moved.

*On the radio.* His word, and he had chosen it. *I bet* was his own. It went in a long way, while neither of them moved, to the morning Kate kept a name for.

She had lost separation once, at two in the morning, and nobody on the frequency could have told it from her voice, and tonight she had stood at that table while every face in the room turned to him and kept her voice exactly where it was.

*Understood* came up in her, whole and ready, in the voice, and she heard how it would sound to him, and shut her teeth on it.

He took his keys out of his jacket pocket. The ring was heavy, and he went round it with his thumb in the register light until he came to hers, the plain front key she had laid on his counter on the third of December, under his clock, without a word. He set his thumbnail in the split of the ring and worked the key round the coil, one turn and then another, the steel biting at the nail, until it came free. The dead key stayed on, the one Arlene had given him in 'ninety-six, its orange tag gone soft. He put the ring back in his pocket with that one still on it.

He laid her key on her counter, in the circle of light, and took his fingers off it, and said nothing, the way she had said nothing at his.

It made a small sound on the counter, and it was the only sound in the building.

He picked up his bag and went. She heard the inner door, and then the outer one. Through the door glass she saw him on the granite step under the light over the door, his breath going up white, his back to her, and she wanted him to stay on the step one more time, as she had wanted it the first night, with the snow on his shoulders and her heat going out past her into the dark. He went down it to the truck.

His lights came across the glass block and went away down the drive.

She picked up the key. It was warm from his pocket.

There was the hook by the register, where the spares hung on their tape; she looked at it and closed her fingers on the key. There was the office, and the steel drawer of the desk, where his signature already lay by itself on a page; she did not go in.

Behind her, at the edge of the light, the clipboard was still on the snack-bar table.

She put the key in her own vest pocket.
