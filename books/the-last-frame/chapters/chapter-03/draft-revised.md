# Chapter 3: Ground Stop

At ten to nine Ellen stood at the front glass and watched Hollis Pike's plow sit crossways at the bottom of her drive with its blade down. Its yellow beacon went round. It swept the sign pole and the snow driving past it, climbed the drive, came in through the glass block and lit the room behind her, and went away and came round again. The sign was still lit. The three o'clock forecast had said all of this would be over by nine.

Hollis came up the drive on foot with the snow to his knees and stood in the vestibule without bothering to stamp.

"Front end's off the crown and the blade's dug in," he said. "That's a loader. Not tonight."

Dale Hutchins was stamping enough for both of them. He was Ambrose's road agent, the man every plow driver in town answered to, and he had driven up her hill a few minutes ahead of the plow to tell her to send people home, and had watched it go sideways below his pickup. He did not raise his voice, and the people nearest the counter heard every word of it.

"I called you at ten past three and told you it wasn't stopping."

"You did," Ellen said.

There was nothing to put after it, and she put nothing. She had run league on the three o'clock forecast, and at twenty to eight the band had stalled over the lake and gone on unloading where it sat, and by the time she looked up from the radar on her phone the drive was one white slope with two fence posts showing.

The room started deciding without her.

"I've got four-wheel drive," said a man at the cubbies, with his keys already out.

"My dog's been shut in since noon."

"It's not bad past the beach. It's only the hill."

The captain turned from the counter to answer them, in the voice a man uses to get a crew moving. "Hollis's rig'll be out of there inside the hour. I'll take you down behind my truck to the center, a few at a time."

Hollis said nothing, which from Hollis was a measurement.

Ellen counted the room. First, the ones already moving: the man with the keys, two women from the diner team zipping their coats, a boy in a hockey jacket halfway into the vestibule with his hood up. Next, the ones watching the door to see who went first, which was most of them. Next, the ones who could not go anywhere fast: the old couple on the one-and-two settee, a man with a cane hooked over the scorer's table, Denise behind the fryer with a basket up and dripping. Then Hollis by the door, and the captain, and Ray at her right shoulder, the one person in the building who was not looking at the door. Thirty-three besides herself, on a Thursday the forecast had already thinned, and every one of them about to do something reasonable, and she knew how a night came apart, one reasonable thing and then the next, and she had sounded perfectly fine the last time it happened to her.

At five to nine she spoke at the level she used on the PA, without the PA.

"Thirty-three people on Kettle Road in this is a sequence of accidents. Nobody leaves."

The vestibule stopped moving. The boy in the hockey jacket stepped back inside.

"Here is the order," she said, in the voice she used for lane assignments. "First, the settees, by pair, oldest nearest the counter, where the heat comes up from the cellar. Second, coats are blankets, and boots go by the cubbies so the floor stays dry. Third, the snack bar stays open until the fryer runs out. Fourth, anyone who would rather stay awake may bowl on all ten lanes until morning. Sleeping is free. Strings are not."

Her voice was level and kind and a little bored, and under it she was still counting.

"She's right, Dale," Ray said, not loudly.

Ellen sat down on the counter stool. Ray saw her sit. She saw him see it, and he looked off down the lanes and said nothing, and nobody else in the building noticed at all.

The captain stood a while longer with his hood dripping on her floor. Then he came along the counter to the register and laid a bill down.

"I can't sleep with my trucks out," he said. "Give me a lane."

She rang it and counted his change into his palm, and he closed his hand on it.

"Nobody ever charged me for a snowstorm."

\* \* \*

At a quarter past eleven Denise ran a first tape off the register with the marathon still crashing on behind her. The tape came curling up out of the machine and over her thumb. At the end of the counter the captain had the rotary phone's long cord stretched around the coffee urn and was telling a man named Pelkey that the cut past the town beach was over a pickup's bumper, and to drop his wing at the culvert and leave it dropped till he heard otherwise.

"You never did come ask me in the morning," Denise said.

"I read the tape at four a.m. You'd left it under the drawer."

Denise looked at her over the tape a second longer than the answer needed. Then she tore it off and held it out at arm's length to read.

"Eight hundred and change," she said, "and it's not done. First night since you came that this drawer's been on the right side of anything, and I'll give you that, and I'll also say the man who runs every plow in town is standing right there on your telephone, and Arlene, the winter of the ice storm, gave away every hot dog on that roller and the buns with them. You charged them for a snowstorm."

"I charged them for strings," Ellen said. "The snowstorm was free."

Denise gave her one long look and clipped the tape to her board and said nothing at all, which was rarer than the money.

Ray was down at the far pairs collecting settee cushions two at a time and carrying them up to the near pairs, tucking them in around the old couple, who were asleep sitting up with their coats buttoned. Ellen did not have to turn her head to know it. Everyone else in the building had a place she could put them: asleep, bowling, at the urn, on her telephone. Ray she could not put anywhere, and she had been trying since nine. He straightened up with a cushion in each hand and caught her at it. She held his eyes for a beat after she had any reason to, and then for one more.

Just before half past eleven the lights went. Every lane, the snack bar and the hum of the PA went at once, and the red exit signs over the doors were the only color left in the building. Somebody's ball was already on its way down lane six. It hit, and nobody saw what it got. Thirty-three people stopped talking at the same moment, and in the gap Ellen could hear the snow ticking on the glass block.

Then Ray's voice, at her elbow. "Hold still a second."

He had been across the room, and she had not heard him cross it. He turned his head down toward the floor and the cellar under it, listening for something she couldn't hear, and then whatever it was stopped for him too.

"Burner runs on the power, and so does the pump that moves the heat," he said. "That's the boiler."

\* \* \*

On her knees on the pin deck behind lane five, with every flashlight in the house aimed down the lane at her, Ellen set the head pin on its spot and took her hand away.

With the machines dead, somebody had to set pins, and the deck still had ten worn circles in the wood from the years when boys had knelt here and done it by hand. Ray had shown her once. She had it. He had picked five, the middle of the house, without asking anyone, and she filed that.

"Folks would sleep, you know," he said, "if you let them."

"They paid for three strings. They'll get three strings."

"Then I'd guess we're the machine."

They set ten and backed off the deck behind the curtain, and Denise bowled by flashlight, slowly, the way she did everything except talk. After her third ball they went out on their knees, cleared the wood, rolled the ball back up the gutter and set ten more. Between balls they sat with their backs against the kickback, where the motors had left a little warmth in the steel. Their shoulders were not quite touching, and then they were.

"Denise is getting her practice in," Ray said. "Last Saturday in February the league bowls Linda's Memorial, its championship, all ten lanes going the way Linda bowled it, and Denise has wanted that cup longer than she'll say."

On the fourth box a pin skidded off the deck and came rolling along the kickback straight at Ray. Ellen was standing up wood for the next rack and did not look at him.

"Traffic, twelve o'clock, one mile."

It bumped his knee. Ray laughed. It came out of him all at once, too big for the pit, with his head back against the steel, and she had never heard it from him before and wanted it again at once.

"What's so funny back there?" Denise called up the lane.

Neither of them answered.

"Locker fourteen has a padlock none of my keys fit," Ellen said.

Ray picked up the stray pin and stood it with the others. "I've got the key. I don't use it."

She waited. He picked up another pin and turned it in his hands.

"February of 'ninety-eight," he said. "The winter five's sweep motor went. I'd put a new one in her that afternoon, and I came back after supper to watch her run, and Linda was on five, and I sat about where you're sitting. Thing about this pit is you see every ball coming and you can't do a thing about any of them. She had a big string going into the last box, and I couldn't see her from here, only the ball, and it came down the middle, and I got up on my knees like a fool. And she finished it, and the motor held. A hundred and eighty-seven."

His thumb went round and round the neck of the pin he was holding. Then he stood it on the floor with the others and squared it, and squared it again.

"I've been trying all month to work out why anybody would buy a building that loses money every Thursday," he said, "and I can't, and I'm about out of guesses."

She looked down the lane at the row of flashlights. It was easier to say it to them.

"Two things," she said. "They retired me by statute, with a sheet cake. That isn't one of them."

He waited.

"First. I worked midnights a long time. One night, at two in the morning, I let two airplanes get closer together than I was allowed to." She squared the pin by her knee. "Separation is the space you keep between two airplanes. It's the whole job. Nobody was hurt. I sounded perfect. I listened to the tape afterward, and you couldn't hear a thing in my voice, and I had come apart the entire time. I wrote it up myself before anyone asked." She took her hand off the pin. "Since that night, when I sound calm, I don't know what it means."

Ray didn't say anything. His hands had stopped.

"Second. Last winter I couldn't sleep. I drove the lake road at night, and I came in under the sign on a league night and stood in the vestibule, and all ten lanes were going. It sounded like Friday-evening arrivals into Boston, forty people talking and every one of them getting through." She found a pin by feel and picked it up. "And a pin is standing or it's down, and the whole room can see which. Nobody can sound fine about a pin."

She stopped there. She had a third item, in order behind the other two, and she decided not to brief it.

Ray picked up a pin for the next rack and held it in both hands.

"Then I'll ask you one thing," he said, "and I haven't asked anybody for anything in a good while, so you'll bear with me. Linda's Memorial. I'd like it to stay the way it is."

"It stays," Ellen said. An answer was only true with its condition attached, and she gave him the condition at the same level. "Unless the numbers say otherwise."

Halfway through it Denise's ball came down out of the dark and took every pin on the deck, a strike, the first of the night. They went into the cushion in front of them with a crash that filled the pit, and up the lane somebody on the settees whooped, and a few boots stamped.

Ray hadn't heard it. She could see that in his face before he said a word, fifteen inches from hers. He was turning the pin in his hands and looking at her as though the answer had been yes and nothing after it.

*No readback.* She had never once let a clearance go out without hearing it come back, and she let this one go.

She had the sentence ready, and she did not say it again.

"Pins!" Denise was shouting. "Am I bowling or am I waiting?"

Ellen set them, spot by spot, from the head pin back, and when she sat back on her heels the pin she had held all through it was the ten pin, standing on its spot, and her hand was still closed around it.

"You can let go of that one," Ray said. "It's set."

\* \* \*

The windrow the town loader had left across the mouth of the drive came to Ellen's hip, and the sign pole was somewhere in it. She drove her shovel in where the pole ought to be and hit it on the third try.

"There," she said. "Down to the gravel all the way round, so his blade can see it. He's here at seven. They go home in order after that, oldest first, and the lot is empty by eight."

Hollis had said seven when he phoned at a quarter past six, on the office's rotary phone, which had gone on working once the lights went. He had been out since five, when the loader pulled his plow free, and had gone down Kettle Road behind it opening the road to the center. Ray dug on the lake side of the pole with a square-mouthed shovel from down cellar. Across the lake the far shore was coming back out of the dark.

"Two weeks I've had a key to your front door," Ray said, without stopping, "and I've carried it around and turned it over a good deal, and I'd about decided it was a no."

"It was a no to selling."

"And the rest of it?"

"The rest of it was a key."

He leaned on his shovel and looked at her, and she went on digging. She had wanted him able to walk into her building without her having to ask him in, because she did not ask, and a key was the way around that. She had laid it on his counter and left him to work out the rest.

Her gloves were in her vest pocket. She had taken them off for a better grip on the frozen handle and not thought of them since, and her hands had gone red and then white across the knuckles. Ray laid his shovel across the trench, came round the pole, took hers out of her hands and stood it in the snow, and held both her hands still between his. That was all he did.

She meant to tell him, and she had the briefing built: what she had said in the pit, the clause he had missed under Denise's strike, and that she had watched him miss it. Every mistake she had made in her working life she had written up herself before anyone asked, and every one had cost her something, a position or a rating or days off the boards, and she had paid each one the day it came due. This one would cost her him, his face the way it had been in the pit with the pin turning in his hands. Nobody had ever asked her to pay that, and she would not.

Up at the building the lights came on all at once behind the glass block, and over the two of them the sign caught, red script and the white candlepin with the first letter dark, and the red ran out across the new snow to their boots. Down under the building the boiler would be lighting. She had not given the sleepers a thought since she came down the drive, which had never once happened to her on a position, and she did not want to go up and look.

She ought to have been going up the drive, and she stood in the trench with her hands in his and thought of his laugh going off against the steel, and the frost on the shoulders of his canvas jacket, and Hollis at seven, and a sweep motor that held in 'ninety-eight, and his thumb going round the neck of a pin, and how far up she had to look from this close, and the sentence, and somewhere back around the motor the order had gone out of it, and she heard that it had, and she let it run.

"Ellen," he said.

She took one hand out of his and put it to his face. His cheek was rough under her palm, and so cold she could feel her own hand warming it. He did not move. She slid her hand round to the back of his neck, where his collar had let the wind in all night, and came up the last of the distance herself, and kissed him under the dead K.

For a second he stood as still as he had stood in the dark, listening for the boiler. Then he breathed out against her mouth, all at once, and kissed her back, and took no notice of the road at all. He let go of her other hand and brought his own up along her jaw, rough at the heel and cold at the fingertips. His other arm came round her shoulders and held on, and she stood in the trench in the red light with the snow to her shins and let him, and kept her hand at the back of his neck, where it was warm now.

When he lifted his head he did not go far. He found her free hand again and looked down at it.

"Your hands are warm now," he said. "That's all I was after."

Hollis's plow came off Kettle Road into the mouth of the drive with its blade up and its yellow light swinging across the two of them, across the pole and the two shovels in the snow, and stopped. Hollis sat in the cab and said nothing through the glass. Up the drive every face in the building was in the glass block, woken by the lights. The vestibule door opened, and Denise stood in it in her coat with her arms folded, and called down the drive.

"Honey."
