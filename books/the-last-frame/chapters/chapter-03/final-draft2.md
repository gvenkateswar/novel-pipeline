# Chapter Three: Deadwood

Judy Ostrowski wrote two numbers on a clean sheet of paper, uncapped a blue pen, and drew a circle around each one.

"Two numbers," she said. "I'll circle them so neither of us can pretend later. Sit down, Ellen."

Ellen stayed on her feet. Judy's office had glass on three sides, and through the glass the lobby was all marble and a calendar of loons. Out at the drive-up the pneumatic tube landed with a thunk. Judy waited with a butterscotch in her hand. When the waiting got her nowhere, she turned the sheet around so it faced the right way.

"Here's how the underwriter sees you. Your other nine months net out to about nothing. Spring leagues pay for summer and summer people pay for fall. It washes. So the winter is the year."

She tapped the paper. "The new loan costs about twenty-four thousand a year. The committee wants a quarter more than that coming in, so there's room for one bad thing. Thirty thousand, net operating income, December first through February twenty-eighth. Twenty-five thousand cash on the twenty-eighth, so one bad month doesn't sink you. That means on deposit here by close of business that day. Or taken in by then and documented, with register reports dated by then and a deposit slip."

"And if I come in at twenty-nine?"

"Then the committee says no, and I say I'm sorry, and I mean it." Judy started unwrapping the butterscotch. "I like you. The underwriter has never met you. He's met Arlene's last three winters."

Ellen looked at the circles. Thirteen weeks, and she was starting from three hundred dollars under zero.

"There's forty-one thousand in the account today," she said.

"Everybody passes the cash test in December," Judy said. "People fail it in February. You've got school vacation week at the end of it, our week, the last one. What have you got booked against it?"

"Nothing yet."

Behind Judy's head hung a framed photograph of Judy in a snowmobile suit, on ice, holding a lake trout nearly as long as her leg.

"How much did it weigh?"

Judy didn't turn around. "Eleven pounds, four ounces. On a certified scale."

"Which item has the longest lead time?"

"The appraisal. Then an environmental review, because you've got a 1958 building with an oil tank in the basement and the committee likes to know what's in the ground." Judy finally ate the butterscotch. "Want to close on March first? Order both now. Then the only thing still open on March first is your winter."

"Order them today."

Judy wrote APPRAISAL / ENVIRO / TODAY on a yellow pad and underlined the last word twice. "I'll call the appraiser before lunch. He'll want to see the boiler."

"Everybody does."

Ellen sat down then, for the four minutes it took to sign the engagement letters.

On her way out she stopped at the teller window and asked for a second business account. The teller wore a tie clip shaped like a loon. He slid the form under the glass.

"It's for money I'm only holding," Ellen said. "Meals tax from the snack bar. The league's prize fund."

"Would you like a nickname on the statement? A lot of people put 'Rainy Day.'"

She took the pen on its little chain and printed in block capitals: HELD. NOT MINE. She was sliding the form back under the glass when the drive-up tube landed behind him, and he turned away to get it.

* * *

The steel door by lane 10 gave on the second shove, and Ray came through it sideways with a carton under each arm.

"Place up past Plymouth closed in October," he said. "Fella's daughter was selling the machines for scrap. I got there Monday before the truck did."

He set the cartons on the workbench beside the coffee can of bolts. Half the parts in them already wore masking tape in his square capitals.

"How much?"

He told her. She wrote the check against the bench and held it out to him.

"Now the calls," she said. "You keep the machines running. I pay a fair rate per call. On an invoice."

"Never charged Arlene."

"That's the problem. Arlene's books look better than they were by exactly what you're worth. I'm not carrying a number I know is wrong." She waited. "Lane 7. Last Wednesday. Invoice me."

Ray took a pad of Gagnon & Son sales slips out of his jacket, licked the carpenter's pencil from behind his ear, and wrote on the bench. LABOR / LANE 7 CLUTCH. He wrote a number under it and turned the pad toward her.

"That's what you charge to cut a key," Ellen said.

"About what it took."

"It took two hours and forty minutes on a cold catwalk. I have your card." She took the pencil out of his hand, crossed out his number, and wrote one above it.

He looked at the new number and then at her. "That's too much."

"It's what the work costs."

He didn't argue it. He tore off the white copy, held it out between two fingers until she took it, and put the pad away.

She took her own key ring out, worked one back-door key off it with her thumbnail, and set it on the bench beside the coffee can.

From the same pocket she produced an index card and a pushpin and pinned the card to the pegboard beside the pit door, next to the electrician's work order. She had ruled it the night before. R.G. — AFTER HOURS. Under the heading, five columns: DATE / TIME IN / TIME OUT / LANE / REASON.

"Back door," she said. "Any night after close. I don't need to be here and you don't need to call first. You write it down. Every visit, in your own hand."

Ray looked at the card, then at the key, and didn't pick it up.

"Readback," Ellen said.

"Back door. After close. Write it on the card." He picked up the key. "Every time."

Complete, and in order. She nodded once.

He took out his ring, threaded the key on beside the brass one with Arlene's orange tag, and put the ring back in his jacket.

"These machines need somebody who knows them," he said. "I'll keep them running. Whoever has this building on March second is going to want them running."

"I know who you mean."

He tipped his head at the work order. It had the electrician's letterhead and a pencil drawing of the ten lanes, with a total at the bottom. "What's that?"

"Friday nights. Black lights, new LED lighting over the lanes, and a sound system. Fifty-four hundred dollars. He starts Thursday."

"Bank's money?"

"Mine. Out of cash."

He unpinned the order and read it. Six fixtures were marked in pencil, one over each pair of lanes, and the last one over the wall between lane 1 and the snack bar.

"That's the trophy case."

"That's the wall the trophy case is on."

He pinned the order back where it had been. Then he pulled the first carton toward him, slit the tape with his thumbnail, and started lifting out parts, reading his own tape on each one before he hung it on its nail.

* * *

On Thursday at two-thirty an electrician stood on a ladder over lane 6 with three screws in his mouth and a black-light fixture held up against the ceiling tile. Denise had the crossword folded on the counter.

"He says six o'clock, which means eight, which means tomorrow at four you're up that ladder yourself in your good shirt, and don't look at me, I don't do ladders. Roger did ladders. Roger's my ex-husband, he's got a dental hygienist in Laconia now, she can hold his ladder. Anyway. You can't bowl."

"Yes."

"The league knows it too. They watched your first ball go into lane 6, and they will not respect an owner who can't bowl. They'll be polite to her, which is worse."

"Who's going to teach me?"

"Honey, who do you think has been fixing your machines?"

Denise reached under the counter and slid a form across with the pen on top. LEAGUE SANCTION CARD. "Fill it out. So you're legal if they're ever short."

"Short of what?"

"Bowlers, in case anyone's counting." She glanced at the register. "Well."

Ellen filled it in. Denise turned it with one finger and inspected it the way she inspected rental shoes after the spray.

"You'll live."

"Testing," the electrician said, through the screws, and one fixture came on over lanes 5 and 6.

The light ran violet down the pair and out across the counter. On Denise's red polo a handprint appeared on the left shoulder, bright as paint.

"That's Roger," Denise said, still writing. "Fourth of July, 2011, mustard, I've washed that shirt forty times. Look at your menu board."

Between HOT DOG and FRIE, where the S had fallen out years ago, a clean ghost of an S glowed on the felt. Out in the vestibule every bean-supper flyer on the corkboard had lit up like a pharmacy sign.

Ellen turned to the strip board. Two office parties wanted lanes 1 through 4 at seven on Wednesday, one on top of the other.

"Office parties want five to seven," Denise said. "So the husbands and wives can come after work and the boss can leave before anybody gets loud. Arlene put them at seven and wondered why they never came back."

Ellen took down both strips, changed them to five o'clock, put the second party on lanes 7 through 10, and printed PER D. LAVOIE along the bottom of each. She pinned them back where Denise could read them.

Denise looked at the strips longer than she had looked at anything all afternoon.

"Your fives look like esses," she said.

Ellen's phone was in her vest. She typed with both thumbs. LESSONS 0700 MONDAY. RATE PER OUR AGREEMENT.

The reply came while the electrician was moving his ladder down to lane 1. I don't charge for bowling. Tell you my price Friday.

"Stand back," the electrician said, and threw the switch for the whole bank.

Down the length of the building the trophy case went violet. Fifty years of tarnish disappeared into the light, and in the center of the case the card in its frame glowed white. WOMEN'S HOUSE HIGH SINGLE. 187. LINDA GAGNON.

The electrician came over with a clipboard. "Who signs?"

Denise pointed her pen at Ellen and wrote in the next answer.

* * *

By ten to nine on Friday all ten lanes were running under black light, and the waiting list was on its own strip with four names on it. Denise worked the register and corrected it from memory twice.

"Snack bar's done a league night's business and it's not nine," she said. "Don't get excited. And the popcorn machine and the space heater can't both be on, so somebody's going to be cold or somebody's going to be hungry, and I've made my choice."

Ellen walked the floor from lane 1 to lane 10 and back. Fifty-two people bowling in a building that had refunded three lineages ten days ago.

At 9:40 lane 3's table started coming down late. She watched it twice to be sure, then switched it off, apologized to the birthday party on it, and moved them to the top of the waiting list with a free basket of fries.

"You calling Ray?" Denise said.

"No."

"Good. I texted him."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You didn't ask me not to. And he'd have heard it from the parking lot."

At 11:20, after close, the back door opened. Ray stood at the pegboard with his pencil and wrote on the card before he did anything else. 12/11. 2320. TIME OUT left empty. 03. TABLE LATE. Then he went through the steel door into the pit.

Ellen gave him five minutes and followed.

He had lane 3's table running on test. It dropped every seven seconds onto an empty deck and rose again, and on every drop the sweep came across behind it with a thunk she could feel through the catwalk boards.

The electrician had run one fixture over the masking at the lane ends, and it lit the catwalk violet through the machines. Ray's canvas jacket glowed, and the pencil behind his ear. Every strip of tape he had ever written on, twenty years of PIN ELEVATOR 4 and BELT, SPARE and DON'T, hung glowing on its nail along the wall.

He stood looking at them.

"Nobody told me the tape did that."

The sweep came through. Ellen waited it out.

"It's been doing it the whole time," she said. "You never had the lights for it."

"Those staying?"

"Fridays and Saturdays."

He thought about that with his hands in lane 3's table, and gave something a quarter turn. The table dropped. He listened to the sweep and gave the same thing another eighth.

"My price," he said, to the table. "I'll teach you. Mornings, before the store. And the championship stays the way it's always been. Last Saturday in February. All ten lanes. The Linda Gagnon Memorial. Nothing glowing."

Ellen spoke from the pit doorway, after the next sweep had cleared.

"You have my word. The championship stays as it is, the last Saturday in February, all ten lanes, unless the numbers say otherwise."

The table came down before she finished, and the sweep went across the deck. Ray was watching it, lips moving, counting the cycle.

"That's all I'm asking," he said to the machine.

He wiped his hand on a rag from his back pocket and held it out.

She had made him repeat a breaker setting, and the back-door rule, and the key. She could ask him now. She looked at his hand, gray with grease to the second knuckle, and at the tape glowing on its nails behind him, and took it.

"Monday, then," Ray said. "Seven."

"Seven."

* * *

At seven on Monday morning only lanes 5 and 6 had their lights on. The rest of the building ran off into the dark, and the baseboard ticked along the wall as the heat came up. Somebody's glow-night confetti still lay in the gutter of lane 6.

Ellen had a clipboard. On it was a diagram she had drawn the night before with a protractor, three lines of approach converging on the gap between the 1 pin and the 3. Her reading glasses hung on their cord.

Ray, in his own shoes, studied the clipboard.

"Quit figuring," he said. "Look at the pins."

"I was looking at the clipboard."

"That's what I mean."

He handed her a house ball. Four and a half inches across, no holes, weighing nothing she could feel through her wrist.

Her first throw sailed past the lob line and touched down twice more, at intervals she could have timed with a watch.

"Lob," Ray said. "Pins don't count."

He called the second one the same. The third also cleared the head pin and three others.

"Lob. None of those count either."

She wrote each one down.

"You writing down the misses?"

"The misses are the data."

Behind her the ball return rumbled the ball back.

"What's the maximum?" she said.

"Nobody's ever bowled a 300. Best anybody's done is 245."

She wrote both numbers at the top of the clipboard, 300 over 245.

He picked a ball off the rack and held it out. "You want me to show you or tell you?"

"Negative," Ellen said. Then she heard it. "Show me."

He came and stood at her right. He took her wrist, turned her hand until the palm faced the pins, and lowered it six inches.

"Low," he said. "Let the lane have it."

He let go first.

In her whole working life nobody had watched her be bad at anything. Her calm had been that good. The mistakes reached people afterward, typed up, with times on them. Now she was bad in plain sight, in the cold, with a man three feet away who knew what good looked like. She kept her face level. It cost her more than a Friday evening of Logan arrivals.

The next ball stayed on the lane. It took the front pins off in a clean chop and left two small groups standing out at the sides of the deck, nothing in the middle, and a fallen pin lying across the boards in front of the group on the right.

"Can we re-rack that?"

"The wood stays on the lane. You play what's left."

He pointed at the fallen pin. "Hit that. Let it do the work." He glanced at the leave. "That's a spread eagle. Don't let the name scare you."

She missed it. She wrote that down too.

Wednesday she left the clipboard in the car.

On Friday she brought it in and laid it face down on the orange settee. Her second ball went low and long, struck a fallen pin, and sent it skidding into the group on the right, and three pins went down with it. Two stood at the left, one behind the other. She took her third ball, let it go low, and took them both down.

Ray didn't say anything. He lifted the next ball off the return before it had stopped rolling and held it out.

* * *

At twenty to midnight on Saturday the Highway Department had gone home and the fryer was ticking as it cooled. Denise had her coat on and her hat with the earflaps in her hand, and she was not leaving. She had taken the R.G. card down off the pegboard by the pit door and was reading it aloud from the office doorway.

"Glow night, lane three, table late. Tuesday, lane nine, belt. Thursday, lane three, table late again, and he underlined again, which is a man with feelings about lane three. Last night, lane six." She held the card up to the light. "'Glow light bracket. Not mine. Checked anyway.'

"Linda used to say his handwriting looked like a fence somebody built to keep the letters in, and look at that, twenty-some years, still a fence."

"Put it back, please."

"I'm putting it back." She did not move. "Honey, somebody added a new row to the sheet at the store this week. They're betting later now. And no, Ray still hasn't bought a square. I offered him the first of March at cost and he looked at me like I'd tried to sell him his own hammer." She put her hat on and pulled the flaps down. "Don't let it go to your head."

The vestibule door banged, and then her car went down the drive.

Ellen pinned the card back by the pit door and sat at the steel desk with the adding machine. The appraiser had come Wednesday, walked the basement with a flashlight, and looked at the boiler for a long time without writing anything. She had paid him.

She ran the tape through Saturday. Net operating income to date: $5,600. Cash: $34,300.

A straight line from zero on December first to thirty thousand on February twenty-eighth put her at $6,333 tonight. She had eighty-eight percent of it, and December was the month with the office parties and the holiday weeks. January had nothing on the strip board yet.

Thirty-four thousand in the bank looked like plenty. Judy had said people thought that in December.

She ran the weeks forward. The league nights were the thinnest columns on the tape, Tuesdays and Thursdays both, thinner than a slow Wednesday afternoon. She didn't know why.

On the two shelves above the desk, Arlene's green ledgers ran from 1971 to this year without a gap. Ellen took a blank strip and printed JAN / RECONCILE / ARLENE / LEAGUE and pinned it to the board.

Then she wrote one more. 02/27 / 1800 / LGM / 10. She left REMARKS blank and pinned it at the far end of the board.
