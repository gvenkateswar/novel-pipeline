1. **The stranded count is thirty-three.** The brief and the orchestrator have settled it. Three other places still say thirty-one and are superseded: the bible ledger ("31 people, 31 cars"), Ellen's bible line 7 ("Thirty-one cars"), and the outline's ending ("Thirty-one witnesses"). The outline's thirty-one witnesses still works out (thirty-three, less Ray and Denise), but **no witness count goes on the page.** "Thirty-one" stays free for Ellen's career.
2. **The captain is on a Thursday.** The Highway Department bowls Tuesdays. Dale Hutchins is at Kettle Lanes because at about 8:15 he drove up to tell her to send people home. Hollis's plow then went sideways below his pickup, so the road agent is stranded with everyone else and runs his crews from her rotary phone all night. That keeps him there to remind her of the call and to be charged for a string. Nothing in the bible changes.
3. **Where Hollis is overnight.** At 8:50 his plow is sideways at the bottom of the drive. He walks up and is stranded too. At five in the morning the town loader the captain called for pulls the plow out, and Hollis goes down Kettle Road opening it to the center. At 7:05 he comes back up the drive with the blade up. (Rule 15: someone arranged it.)
4. **Bible rule 1 (Ellen's silence shown out of character, from inside) is placed in this chapter**, split in two. S3 carries the fact ("No readback": in thirty-one years she let none go by, and she let this one go by). S4 carries the why: every other error cost her a job or a rating, and this one would cost her him. **Ch 05 must not restate it.** It may echo it in one clause while Ray vouches.
5. **New facts for the log** (none conflict with the bible):

### Scene 1
**Setting:** Kettle Lanes, the counter and the front glass, Thursday, December 17, 8:50 to about 9:10 p.m. Sensory anchor: the plow's yellow beacon turning at the bottom of the drive, sweeping the sign pole and the snow and coming round again through the glass block.
**Characters:** Ellen, Dale Hutchins (the captain), Hollis, Ray, Denise. The Thursday division is present but unnamed.
**Want:** Ellen wants every one of the thirty-three to stay inside until daylight, and she wants it to be her call. Three things are in the way. The road agent, standing in her vestibule, is right that she caused this. The bowlers want to go home. And the captain, tired and decent, offers to lead them out behind his truck once Hollis's plow is shifted. The reader learns her want at the decision line.
**Surface action:**
1. Open in motion. Ellen at the front glass at 8:50 watches Hollis's plow sideways across the bottom of the drive with its blade down, under a sign still lit, in snow the three o'clock forecast said would be over by nine. Hollis comes up the drive on foot, snow to his knees, and gives one plain measurement-style line: the plow wants a loader, and not tonight.
2. Dale Hutchins stands in the vestibule stamping his boots. He drove up at quarter past eight to send people home, and now his pickup is above the plow with everyone else's. Establish him in one clause: Ambrose's road agent, the man the plow crews answer to. In front of the counter, not loud: "I called you at ten past three and told you it wasn't stopping."
3. She takes it. She gives no defense, no reason and no list. ("You did.") She ran league on the three o'clock forecast; when the band stalled over the lake at twenty to eight she looked at the radar, and by the time she looked up the drive was gone. That's one sentence of interior, the chapter's only weather procedure. **This exchange ends with her wrong (rule 14).**
4. The room starts deciding without her. Somebody has four-wheel drive. Somebody has a dog at home. The captain says Hollis's plow will be pulled within the hour and he'll take them down behind his truck to the center, a few at a time.
5. She counts the room, as a list in her interior and not as a reflex line: thirty-three besides herself, and where each one is standing.
6. **8:55, out loud, at PA level without the PA:** "Thirty-three cars on Kettle Road in this isn't a decision. It's a sequence of accidents. Nobody leaves."
7. The sequence, briskly, as items. Sleeping spots in order: the settees by lane pair, the oldest nearest the counter and the boiler's warmth, coats as blankets. A paid marathon on all ten lanes for anyone who'd rather stay awake. Sleeping is free, and strings are not. Then she sits down on the counter stool. (Bible: she sits only once something is decided.) Ray, at her shoulder and the only person in the room not looking at the door, sees her sit. One beat; nobody remarks on it.
8. Optional, one line: Ray to the room or to the captain, plain, backing her. ("She's right, Dale.") It foreshadows his public vouching in Ch 5, but keep it after her decision and never instead of it.
9. The captain comes to the register, lays down money for a string because he can't sleep with his trucks out, and says, "Nobody ever charged me for a snowstorm." Ellen rings it.
**Subtext:** She knows the captain is right, and she can't afford to say it in front of the people she's about to keep. Holding them is the only way left to be right tonight. Ray watches her take a hit in public without answering it.
**Texture budget:** (1) The stalled band over the lake, one sentence, told through what it did to the drive and not as meteorology. It shows she ran on a forecast against a man's warning. (2) Her sleeping order, as items, which shows her sequencing under fear: calm, polite, imperative.
**Point of view:** Ellen. Under fear she gets more polite and procedural, uses imperatives and the present tense, and briefs whoever is nearest. Show the gap from inside: the dialogue is calm while the interior runs fast.
**Ends on:** **Cut.** The captain's line, "Nobody ever charged me for a snowstorm," as the last line, with no reaction beat after it. (This is the chapter's only quip ending. Don't add another.)

### Scene 2
**Setting:** The counter, 11:15 to about 11:30 p.m. Sensory anchor: the register tape curling out under Denise's thumb, and the captain beside it on the rotary phone's long cord, talking to his crews in measurements.
**Characters:** Ellen, Denise, Ray, Dale Hutchins (one line at most, on the phone).
**Want:** Denise wants Ellen to admit to her face that she charged people stuck in her building, and wants to see what kind of owner says yes to that. Ellen wants the number, the first night in the black, read out exactly. What's in the way is Denise's judgment, delivered in earshot of the road agent.
**Surface action:**
1. Open in motion. Denise rings the tape at a quarter past eleven, as she always rings it, while the marathon crashes on behind them. The fryer is shut down for the night.
2. Payoff, first and fast: "You never did come ask me in the morning." / "I read the tape at four a.m. You'd left it under the drawer." Ellen is literal, and it's a little sad (she wakes at ten to four; don't say so). No night-one figure.
3. The money fact, Denise to Ellen: "Eleven hundred and change. You charged them for a snowstorm." / "I charged them for strings. The snowstorm was free." Denise gets the last look and no comeback. The double act: Denise in a paragraph, Ellen in one line. Denise doesn't say "honey" here.
4. Longing: Ellen knows without looking where Ray is in the room. He's moving the oldest sleepers' settee cushions nearer the counter, and he's the one position in the building she can't put in a slot. He looks up and catches her at it, and she keeps looking for one beat longer than she has a reason to.
5. Lights out at about 11:25: every lane, the snack bar and the PA hum all at once. The exit signs glow red over the doors. Thirty-three people stop talking at the same moment.
6. In the dark, Ray's voice is at her elbow (he was across the room; he crossed it). Then he turns his head toward the floor, at a sound she can't hear. A second later she can't hear it either. "That's the boiler," he says. The burner and circulator are both electric, in one clause from him. The building has started to cool.
**Subtext:** Denise is asking whether Ellen is the town's new Arlene or something worse. Ellen's exact answer is a defense she half believes. The power cut takes the one good number she's had in seventeen days and turns it into thirty-three people in a cooling building, which she caused.
**Texture budget:** (1) The register tape, which carries the one money fact and pays off night one. (2) A power cut stops an oil boiler, one clause in Ray's mouth, which shows his ear (he hears machines before she hears voices).
**Point of view:** Ellen.
**Ends on:** **Reversal.** The night she finally made money, the heat goes. The last beat is Ray's line, "That's the boiler," or one short clause after it in which the quiet under the floor registers. It's physical, not a quip. No sentence explains what it means.

### Scene 3
**Setting:** The pit behind lane five, about 12:45 to 1:30 a.m. Everyone is in parkas, the exit signs are fading off their batteries, and a row of flashlights along the settees is aimed down the lane at the deck. Sensory anchor: the motors' last warmth going out of the kickback at their backs, and the pins cold enough to feel through gloves.
**Characters:** Ellen, Ray, Denise (at the foul line, sixty feet away, hearing nothing they say).
**Want:**
- Ellen's: "They paid for three strings. They'll get three strings." She says it once, early, and it's the line where the reader learns the want. Underneath, she wants him to keep talking to her in the dark.
- Ray's: he wants to tell someone about the 187 out loud, and he wants one thing promised by the only person who can change it. Nobody has asked him anything in six years, and asking costs him.
- What's in the way: her own habit of telling things exactly, and the noise of the house.
**Surface action:**
1. Open in motion. She's already down in the pit with him, both of them on their knees on the deck behind the curtain. The decision to hand-set gets one clause: the machines are dead, pins aren't, and the deck has ten marked spots from the years when boys set them. He shows her once and she has it. Lane five is the middle of the house, so the flashlights reach it from both sides. If the writer takes the Linda fact (flag 5), Ellen notices that he chose five without discussion and files it. Optional, in one glimpse: her flashlight passes the bench and lands on the old clutch with the envelope under it, still sealed, and she says nothing.
2. Denise bowls by flashlight, slowly, as Denise does everything except talk. Pins come back at them out of the dark. They reset, sitting with their backs to the kickback between boxes, shoulders not quite touching and then touching.
3. **The laugh.** A pin rolls off the deck and into his lap. Ellen, squaring the ten pin without looking at him: "Traffic, twelve o'clock, one mile." Ray laughs out loud, a real laugh she has never heard from him. Denise shouts up the lane to ask what's funny back there. Neither answers. (One ATC phrase in her dialogue this scene, and this is it.)
4. Ellen gives a statement, not a question, because she doesn't ask: "Locker fourteen has a padlock none of my keys fit." Ray: "I've got the key. I don't use it."
5. **Linda's 187, in Ray's syntax.** Long, sideways, dated by what broke, the important word last. February of 'ninety-eight. If flag 5 is used, he'd just put five's sweep back and stayed in this pit to listen, and he watched her last box come at him from back here, the pins flying toward him. He tells it carefully. Ellen wants to put her hand over his mouth to save him the rest of it, and doesn't. Don't name grief; show it in what his hands do with the pin he's holding.
6. Ray asks why she bought a building that loses money every Thursday. (It's a question he doesn't know the answer to, which is rare for him.) **She answers in her own words, and the reader learns why she bought the alley here.** She speaks in items, short and complete, with the order holding and then going. Content:
   - She was retired by statute, at a sheet cake. That's one clause; no figure needed.
   - The 2 a.m. night. On a midnight shift, two airplanes got closer than she was allowed to let them. She defines "separation" once, plainly, as the space you keep between two airplanes, which is the whole job. Her voice on the frequency was perfect the entire time, and inside she came apart. She wrote it up herself before anyone asked. Since that night she has never trusted that her calm means what it sounds like. No acronyms, no procedure, no hours or miles.
   - Last winter, on a night she couldn't sleep, she drove the lake road and came in under the sign on a league night, and heard ten lanes going at once. It sounded like a full frequency on a good night, every voice in its slot. And a pin is either standing or it's down, and the whole room can see which. Nobody can sound fine about a pin.
   - She stops there, at two items, as if there were a third and she has decided not to brief it. **Write it concrete and exact; no sentence states the theme.** Her items must be her own; the bible's interior lines are models, not copy.
7. **Ray's ask**, with his hands on a pin (fear: something in his hands). It's the one thing he'll ask her: Linda's Memorial stays as Linda bowled it, the last Saturday in February, all ten lanes.
8. She answers exactly, as she always does. "It stays." Then, because a true answer has its condition attached: "Unless the numbers say otherwise." On the second half, Denise's ball comes down the lane out of the dark and takes all ten, a strike, rare and loud. Thirty-three people in parkas roar and stamp their boots on the settees. It's a noise, not applause.
9. She sees he didn't hear it. He's turning the pin in his hands and looking at her as if she has handed him something. She is fifteen inches from him and sees it in his face before he says a word.
10. In her head, once: *No readback.* In thirty-one years she had let none go by, and she let this one go by. That's the out-of-character fact, and it goes no further here. The why waits for S4.
11. She has the sentence ready and doesn't say it again. Denise is shouting for her pins. Ellen sets them. She realizes the ten pin is still in her hand.
**Subtext:** Each of them tells the other the thing they don't tell. She has just told him that her calm can hide anything, and within five minutes she uses her calm to hide something from him. The reader sees that, and Ellen half sees it.
**Texture budget:** (1) Hand-setting pins on the ten marked spots, which is work anyone can do and which puts their hands on the same objects in the dark. (2) Separation, defined once in plain words, which reveals the wound and plants Ch 5 and Ch 7.
**Point of view:** Ellen. The sentences lengthen as the scene goes on, and the confessions play in real time. Her dialogue stays short and complete, while the interior around it runs long.
**Ends on:** **Dialogue.** Ray, in his tender register (about what she's holding, plain word last): "You can let go of that one. It's set." The last line is his. Nothing follows it.
**Word target:** 950   **Word floor:** 800 (intimate)

### Scene 4
**Setting:** The bottom of the drive, at the sign pole, Friday, December 18, about 6:40 to 7:05 a.m. (sunrise 7:09). The sky over the lake goes from black to gray. Sensory anchor: the sign coming back on over them when the power returns, red light on the new snow, with the K dark.
**Characters:** Ellen, Ray. At the very end, Hollis (in the cab) and Denise (in the vestibule door at the top of the drive).
**Want:**
- Ellen's: the pole found and the drive mouth clear before Hollis's blade comes, so the thirty-three go home in order by eight. Underneath, she wants to tell him the sentence from the pit, and she wants him, and those two wants can't both be served.
- Ray's: he wants to know what the key meant.
- What's in the way: Ellen herself.
**Surface action:**
1. Open in motion: two people digging. Hollis phoned at a quarter past six. The rotary phone works without power. The town loader pulled his plow at five (Ellen heard its backup beeper from the counter, awake as always). He'll be up the drive at seven. The loader's windrow has buried the base of the sign pole, and a plow blade that can't see a pole finds it the hard way. That's her contingency, and it's why they're out here. Pick fresh props, not the sample's (flag 6): a coal shovel from the cellar, a dustpan, the snack bar's ice scoop, whatever is believable in that building.
2. Ray asks a question he half knows the answer to: two weeks he's had a key to her front door, and was that a no? Ellen gives an exact, deadpan true sentence: "It was a no to selling." "And the rest of it?" "The rest of it was a key." Then **her interior says plainly what it meant**, so the reader has it at last: she wasn't selling, she wanted him able to come in without her having to ask him, and she doesn't ask. It's one or two sentences, and it pays the Ch 2 reader's question. It's harmless, the same move as the pit in a smaller key, and the reader will feel the echo without being told.
3. Her hands are bare on the handle, which is the chapter's one bare-hands moment. He takes the shovel out of her hands and holds her hands still in both of his, nothing more. It's the ball in Ch 1 that neither let go of, and it's not narrated as a callback. Use no line from the sample.
4. **The why (bible rule 1, completed):** She means to tell him. She has an order for it: first, Hollis at seven; second, Denise awake before that; third, a sentence from the pit he didn't hear and needs to. Every error she has made in thirty-one years she wrote up herself before anyone asked, and every one cost her something, a position, a rating, two days off the boards. This one would cost her him, his face the way it was in the pit, and she has never once been asked to pay that, and she finds she won't. Give it one or two sentences, plainly. She sees it in herself, and the narration does not excuse it.
5. Power comes back. Up at the building the lights come on behind the glass block, and over them the sign comes on, red script and the white candlepin, with the first letter dark. The boiler is somewhere far off under them. The sleepers will be waking. She notices she hasn't checked on a single one of the thirty-three in twenty minutes (say it without numbers this time), and that frightens her more than the dark did.
6. **The desire register.** Her list breaks: one long sentence joined by *and*, out of order (his voice, low, all night over the dead machines; the snow in his eyebrows; the gray along his jaw; Linda's night told so carefully; Hollis; the sentence), and she knows it's out of order and leaves it that way. The tail must name the broken order, because that's what marks it as hers.
7. Ray says her first name, "Ellen," for the first time in the book. That's the only line he needs.
8. **The kiss.** She moves first (heat rule). Her hand goes to his face or the back of his neck; no part of either body below the collarbone. One embrace. Mouth named once at most. One sharp sensory detail per beat: cold, the smell of pin grease on his hand, the sound he makes. He kisses her back slowly, taking his time over it. Under the dead K, the one use of "dead K" this chapter. **The kiss beat (approach, kiss, first line after) runs at least 150 words in real time. Don't summarize it or cut away from it.**
9. The first line after is Ray's, about her hands, with the plain word last. Something like "Your hands are warm now. That's all I was after." It's a small lie that Ray tells easily about why he's helping. The writer and dialogue doctor may improve it; keep it his.
10. **Consequence.** Hollis's plow comes off Kettle Road into the drive with the blade up, its yellow light swinging across them, and stops. The cab is full of Hollis, who says nothing. Up the drive, every face is in the glass block, woken by the lights. The vestibule door opens and Denise stands in it.
**Subtext:** She kisses him knowing. Her wrong and her want arrive in the same minute, and the town sees only the want. He thinks he has her promise and her mouth both, and he has one and a half.
**Texture budget:** (1) The windrow and why a plow blade needs the pole found first, which is Ellen's contingency thinking and the reason the scene happens at all. The power coming back isn't trade; it's light.
**Point of view:** Ellen. This is the high-temperature register: slower and longer, in real time, with the list breaking.
**Ends on:** **Consequence.** The town has seen it. Cut on Denise's first word, called down the drive: "Honey." Nothing follows it. This sets up the chapter promise: what happens when he finds out what he didn't hear?
**Word target:** 900   **Word floor:** 800 (kiss scene; heat rule)
