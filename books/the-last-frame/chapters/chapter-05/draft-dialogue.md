# Chapter 5: Not Since Midnight

"Mom. Scale of one to ten."

Kate's voice came up off the steel desk fast and flat, the voice of a nurse on her break who meant to use all of it, with a microwave counting down somewhere behind her. It was Thursday. Ellen called on Sundays at seven, so a call on any other night was news.

January's book lay open under Ellen's left hand. Her printing ran down every Tuesday and every Thursday, league and league and league, and the Saturdays were blank from the top of the page to the bottom. Kate would hear whatever was in her voice, so there would be nothing in it.

"Two."

"You said two with your knee in a brace."

Through the shut office door the house came to her with the edges rubbed off it, the crash and the talk and the snack-bar fryer, Denise's glow night going full tilt.

"The rest is still the rest?"

"Still the rest."

"Good." Something tore at Kate's end, a wrapper. "I took nights through March. Charge nurse. Signed Tuesday."

There it was, all of it in three items: the decision, the date, done, and no space left anywhere in it for a question. Ellen had briefed people that way her whole working life, and she knew exactly whose kitchen table Kate had learned it at.

"Understood."

She squared Ray's invoice on the blotter. It was on a Gagnon & Son pad, ruled in her format, with the job and the hours and the rate and a column for the part, all of it in his square printing. *Lane three table, setting late at top. Retimed her.* On anybody else's paper Ellen would have put a line through that last word. She left it. Clipped behind the invoice was his quote for the K from the shop in Laconia, unsigned, and Christmas had come and gone with the K still dark at the road.

"I can hear paper," Kate said.

"An invoice."

Through the door one lane's worth of crash went out of the noise and stayed out, and she knew it was lane seven, and that he had the sweep down and was on the catwalk behind it with his sleeves pushed up, as surely as if he had walked past the door.

"You could ask me something sometime," Kate said. "Before."

"I'm listening."

"You've got the voice on. The car-in-the-driveway voice."

Ellen knew which morning she meant: the one after the bad midnight shift, when she had sat in the car in the driveway with the engine off until the school bus came, and her daughter had been keeping a name for it ever since.

The PA came through the door before she had an answer, Denise with the whole house under her.

"Fifteen minutes, folks. Get a ball in your hand and don't throw it till I say."

\* \* \*

"Nine," Denise said over the PA, and the house came in with her on eight.

It was her custom, every New Year's Eve since her first winter at the counter: a ball in every hand on every approach, and nobody throwing till she got to zero. The count came down the lanes and in under the masking, where her black lights leaked into the pit and turned everything white in it violet.

Ellen came through the steel door by lane ten with two paper cups of sparkling cider and went along the catwalk. The motors were warm, and the heat came off them into her face one machine at a time.

Ray was behind lane seven with the sweep back in and a rag in his hands, working the grease off them a finger at a time. Lane seven was set behind its curtain with the rest of the row. He had finished in time. He looked up and saw the two cups, and then he saw her.

"Lane seven took me right up to the last minute," he said, "and the last minute's on your bill."

"Five," said the house.

She stood beside him at the kickback and put her free hand flat on the steel, which was warm from the motors. This close she had to look a long way up to see his face, and she looked. He was taller than the catwalk had room for and stood in it a little bent at the neck, and he let her.

"Four."

The house drowned the rest of the count, and then Denise's voice came over the top of everything alone.

"Zero!"

On zero all ten lanes threw at once. She felt the throw before she heard it, a long low roll that came up through the catwalk into her boots, and then the decks went off down the row so close together they made one crash, wood on wood on wood, and the cushions took it, and the steel under her palm shook with it and went on shaking after. Pins went up into the violet light and came down shining. She stood in the middle of it with her hand on the steel and let it come all the way through her. On lane seven's deck beyond the curtain the ten pin was still standing in its corner with the fallen wood around it, white and glowing, and nobody came down the lane.

"That's what I bought it for," she said.

Ray dropped the rag on the workbench. "I know. You told my lock."

She had said it on her vestibule mat, the morning he talked his offer to her lock, with her flashlight on his hands.

She held out his cup.

"Is this on the invoice?"

He took the cup. "Not since midnight."

Then he didn't say Happy New Year. She watched his mouth come to the place where a man says it, and close on it, and the minute go by with it unsaid, and she was glad of every second it stayed that way. Past the masking the house was cheering, and somebody started the old song and lost the words after the first line, and the whole place laughed at him.

"I know the whole sentence now," Ray said, low, under all of it.

"I know you do."

She set her cup down on the kickback. He looked at it there, and then he set his beside it, the way he set down anything that might spill, and for a second the two paper cups stood on the steel together and neither of them moved. Then he did. He came the last step and took her face in both his hands, his palms along her cheeks and his fingers back in her hair, big hands that still smelled of grease, and he tipped her face up the long way to his and kissed her. He was slow about it. Her hand closed on the collar of his shirt and held on. Past the masking the house was still cheering, and at her back she could feel the row of machines giving off their heat, and his thumb moved once along her cheekbone and stopped there. She had always known the time. She had known it on midnight shifts with no clock in sight, and in a snowed-in building with the power out, and on her own front step, to the minute, without looking at anything, and somewhere in this it went. She did not know when. She did not go after it. He kissed her again, or went on kissing her, and she could not have said which, and once in the middle of it he said *Ellen*, so low that it went no farther than her, and she kept her hand shut on his collar as though somebody might call him away.

When he lifted his head, she still had hold of his collar.

"What time is it?"

\* \* \*

Denise had the Memorial cup out of the case and was polishing it at the snack-bar counter, pretending she was dusting. Behind her the fryer was coming up to heat for eleven, and the oil had begun to smell like itself. Her black-light tubes lay in their carton by the register, packed till next time. Ellen sat on the near stool with her coffee.

The cloth went down inside the bowl of the cup, working at nothing, and the names on the little plates round the base went by under Denise's thumb one at a time.

"Forty people watched the two of you come out of that pit a few minutes past twelve with your faces arranged," Denise said, "and who do you think told the Bench, I'll tell you who, all forty, and the Bench had it by breakfast yesterday. So there's your forecast. Clearing from the west, with a good chance of Ray."

Ellen turned her cup on the counter. "I ran league on the last forecast I trusted."

"You should've asked my knees," Denise said. "They had it by Tuesday."

She set the cup back in the case on its own shelf, beside the locker with the brass padlock.

"And here's one more thing, because nobody else in this town is going to say it to your face. Since Linda, he can't stand that house after supper. Half the nights he sits out in his own dooryard with the heater running and the Bruins on, because the truck makes some noise. This place was where he meant to go, once the store was Danny's. He thinks nobody knows. Everybody knows."

She ran the cloth down the locker door and round the padlock without touching it.

Ellen could see the cab without trying: the dash lit green in the dark dooryard, a hockey game on the radio she couldn't have followed, and the passenger side empty. She put herself in that seat, with the heater on her boots, and sat there long enough to know she would like it. Then she took herself out again.

"If Linda's name were on an open," Ellen said, "who would come?"

"Everybody she ever beat. Which is everybody."

Denise's cloth stopped on the case glass, and she looked at Ellen over it.

"Don't you touch Linda's night. And I say that as a woman who's been second to it four times, with my Lakeview Diner."

Ellen got up off the stool. January ran again in her head, league nights and no parties, and came out where it had come out on the twenty-second.

"You're standing," Denise said, with the cloth still in her hand. "What happened?"

"It's January."

\* \* \*

Danny was counting the drawer at the far register, laying the bills out in short stacks on the rubber mat with his lips moving. Ray stood at the near end of the counter with both hands flat on the wood and let him.

The Bench had gone home, and their three stools stood empty by the front window with Main Street dark behind them. Roof rakes hung in a row from the ceiling over the aisles, and the store smelled of kerosene and birdseed. Ellen's bulbs sat on the counter between her and Ray in a paper sack folded over at the top, a carton of them for the cages in the pit, rung up and paid for. She had not picked it up.

Danny looked over at his father, and went back to his stacks, and in a minute looked over again. Ray's right thumb was moving on the wood, a quarter inch out and a quarter inch back, the way a thumb goes over the corner of a bill.

"You're counting it in your head," Danny said.

"My hands are on the counter."

"Tell your thumb."

Ray's thumb stopped. He looked down at it on the wood, and at the far register Danny bent over his stacks again with his ears gone red. Ellen kept her face still, and it took some keeping.

She put both her hands on the edge of the counter, across the sack from his.

"I am asking you for Linda's name, on her day."

Ray's hands stayed where they were. At the far register Danny could have taken the drawer into the back. He laid another bill on a stack where he was, and another.

"You're asking me."

"I am."

She had asked, and he was holding it. She had picked his counter for it, under his store's lights, with his son at the far register, so that whatever he said to her would be said where he said things. She would not ask him in the pit, where he had kissed her.

He looked at her across the sack of bulbs, and she could see him go back over the sentence word by word.

"Go on and give me the rest of it," he said, "and I'll take it in one go."

Her voice when it came was level and courteous, the voice she would have used to read him a parts list across this same counter, and underneath it everything was going at once: his hands on the wood, the soft slap of bills down the counter, the dark window, and how long a man like him took to say no. She had never in her life said *I am asking* to anyone. The first time had gone to him, across a sack of light bulbs.

"The last Saturday in February is derby weekend, and the lake will be full of people with nowhere warm to be. I want her Memorial open to anyone who pays to enter, with her name on the cup, and the league bowls with everybody."

Ray said nothing. Down the counter Danny snapped a rubber band round a stack and laid it in the drawer.

"If the winter goes the way winters go here, I miss by about the whole of January."

His thumb stayed where his son had stopped it.

"Nothing else I can put on that weekend is the size of her name."

"If I miss, you buy it at appraisal, and I get back less than the house my daughter let me spend."

She watched that one reach him. He had heard her say *the house* into a telephone the morning he rekeyed her door, with her old pins in his shirt pocket, and now he knew which house. His face changed with it: his eyes went down to the sack of bulbs between them and stayed there, and his mouth set, and she had her answer before he gave it.

His hands had not moved the whole time she talked. They lay on the wood, big and nicked, with the old white seam along one finger, and she wanted them on her face, where they had been two nights ago.

He looked once over his shoulder at the old kitchen chair behind the counter that she had never seen anybody sit in, and then back at her.

"No."

He said it no louder than a price, and it carried to the far register anyway.

"It's my wife's name. I won't put it on a cup strangers win for money. Linda wouldn't have wanted her night sold."

He gave it to her in short pieces with nothing joining them, and every one of them was his own. She had an answer for each, ready and in order, and kept them. She wanted to pick up the sack and go. She left it where it was.

There was one more sentence that belonged in the room, and she had thought it would have to come from her. He got there first.

"And I know what my no's worth, coming from the man who buys if you miss. Nothing."

At the far register Danny's hands went still on the bills, flat on the mat like his father's on the wood. It was the thing Normie had meant with a plastic fork in his hand on her settee, and Ray had said it at his own counter, out loud, in front of his son, before anyone else could say it for him.

His right hand came up off the wood for the first time and started for his back pocket.

"I've got some put by—"

It was the one thing he knew how to hand across a counter. She came in over the end of it before he could put a figure to it, as gently as it would go.

"I asked Judy before Christmas: my own money lowers the line, slowly, and all of it wouldn't buy back half of January."

His hand stopped short of the pocket.

"No. Yours couldn't count if you gave it. Anything that counts has to have sat in my name since before I signed for the building. And yours is what pays Arlene if I miss."

His hand went back down onto the counter, flat, beside the other. He looked at her a while. Then he nodded, once.

Danny had stopped counting.

It was five past five. Her sense of the time had come back, and she no longer trusted it to the minute. Ray took his hands off the counter. He went to the hook behind it, took down his canvas jacket and put it on, one arm and then the other, and settled the collar she had held on to in the pit, and zipped the jacket, and came round the end of the counter past her and the sack of bulbs and down the center aisle to the door. With his hand on it he turned.

"Then I'll make my no cost me what yours costs you."

He went out to his supper.
