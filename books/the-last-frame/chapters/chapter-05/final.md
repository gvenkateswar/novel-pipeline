# Chapter Five: The Push

"Twenty-four," Ellen said under her breath. Then a boy in a Bruins hat ducked back out to the vestibule for a mitten, and two girls traded places so they could stand together, and she started again from the counter.

The line ran from the counter through the inner door and out past the vestibule into four degrees. Eleven-year-olds in parkas and socks. Their boots had overrun the boot tray and lay in a drift across the linoleum, covering the cigarette burn from 1974. Somebody had pinned Hollis's derby poster over half the lost mittens on the corkboard.

"Twenty-six," she said, and wrote it on the first strip.

Denise stood at the shoe cubbies with the spray can. Toe, heel. She handed the pair across.

"You'll live."

Toe, heel.

She held up a pair of nines, brown and white, cracked across the top. "Those were a seven in 2004. They're a four now."

"What's a seven?" a girl said.

Denise sprayed the next pair.

The radio on Ellen's collar was new. She had bought four on Thursday, with chargers, and every time she turned her head the antenna knocked against the cord of her reading glasses. Ray had looked at the box on the counter that afternoon and said a man could holler down a lane.

She keyed it. "Kettle Maintenance, say intentions."

The radio hissed. Then Ray's voice came from the far end of the building, through a cinderblock wall and ten machines, perfectly flat. "Intentions are to quit."

The birthday mother at the front of the line heard it come out of Ellen's collar and laughed out loud.

The radio clicked again, though she hadn't called him. "Four's table is fine. I can hear it from here."

Ellen pressed the key. "I need the radio to know you're there."

He didn't come back on.

Behind the counter the strip board was full from end to end. Three birthday parties, the youth hockey team, the Rotary, and the Ambrose Elementary lunch staff, retiring one of their own, stacked on every lane from ten o'clock to close.

Twenty-six children who would not stay in line, and six parties butted against each other to the minute. Nobody had needed her to put a room in order since the afternoon of the marble cake, and she had sold a house to buy one that would.

Lane 2 still had the nine o'clock walk-ins, two brothers in their forties who bowled as if they were paid by the box. The older one was on his tenth.

"You're on one and two," Ellen told the mother. "One is ready now. You're number two for lane two. Four minutes."

"Number two," the mother told the girl at her elbow, in the voice people use for a dentist appointment.

At lane 2 the older brother reached for his coat. Denise lifted a pair of size fives to the light and turned them over.

"These," she said.

Ellen keyed the radio before the brother had both arms in his sleeves.

* * *

The pizza place sent all fourteen at 12:05.

They stood in two towers on the snack bar counter, and grease from the bottom box was working along the ledge toward the menu board's plastic letters. The popcorn machine was off so the office heater could run. All ten lanes were going at once, a drawer of wooden spoons dumped on the floor and dumped again.

"I asked for them twenty minutes in trail," Ellen said. "Twelve. Twelve-twenty. Twelve-forty."

"That's Kevin Pelletier." Denise was already lifting the top box and reading the ticket taped to it. "Kevin is the only driver, and he drives an Impala with one headlight, and he doesn't like the hill. So Kevin makes one trip." She set the box on the hot-dog roller's warming shelf. "Bisson party's on top, they eat first. Durant's under. Lapointe goes last."

She had the shelf loaded in party order before she finished talking.

Ellen turned to the nearest parent, a father holding a gift bag. "Denise has your pizzas in order. That was her idea."

"Okay," the father said.

Denise wiped her hands on a napkin and didn't look at either of them.

The hockey coach came to the counter at 12:50 with a whistle on a lanyard he hadn't taken off since the rink. His team had lanes 1 and 2 at 1:15.

"Stand by," Ellen said.

"I've been standing by." He put both hands on the counter. "I've been standing by since half past twelve. The boys have been standing by. We've got a game in Gilford at four, and they've got to eat, and they've got to get changed. Look, you can't tell twelve twelve-year-olds to stand by. They don't stand."

On the bench by the vestibule, eleven of them were proving it.

At 1:12 the lunch staff still had lanes 1 and 2. The retiree was in her ninth box. She was in her early seventies, in a cardigan with a corsage pinned to it, and she took each ball off the return as if it had to be signed for. Her whole staff sat along the orange settee and watched every one.

Ellen walked over with the strip in her hand. "We have a team booked on these lanes at one-fifteen. I can give your party lane 9 at one-forty, with a string on the house."

The head of the lunch staff was in her sixties, in a sweatshirt that said AMBROSE ELEMENTARY CAFETERIA in iron-on letters. She didn't get up.

"Well. I don't believe we'll be doing that." She folded her hands in her lap. "She's in her ninth box. She worked that line forty-one years and fed every one of those boys over there, and their coach before them. She will be finishing her string on the lane she started it on. Then there's cake."

The retiree threw her third ball in the middle of this and didn't look around.

The woman in the sweatshirt had not raised her voice once. Ellen looked at the strip in her hand and found nothing on it that settled either of them.

"Finish your string," she said.

She gave the hockey team lanes 9 and 10 early. That bumped the Rotary twenty-five minutes, and the twenty-five minutes ate the 4:30 walk-in slot she had penciled at the bottom of the board. Denise came past with two pizzas on her forearm.

"Honey, you don't move the lunch ladies."

The coach paid at the register and went down to 9 and 10 without saying thank you.

At 1:20 the lunch staff cut the cake on the lane 1 scorer's table. Ellen watched them from the counter and keyed the radio before she had decided to.

"Kettle Maintenance. Cake on lane one."

The radio hissed. "What kind?"

"Marble."

Nothing came back for a while. Then: "Save me a corner."

At 1:25 lane 8's ball return jammed for the third time, and Ray came up front with a screwdriver in his back pocket. He had put that return back together after the Highway Department took it apart on storm night, and a morning of children had found the one piece he hadn't replaced. He knelt beside it and pulled the cover.

The Rotary man had been waiting out his bump on the lane 7 settee. He leaned over.

"Ray. The championship still the last Saturday in February?"

Ray kept the screwdriver under the bracket. "Last Saturday in February. All ten lanes." He turned it. "She gave her word."

Ellen was three feet away at the lane 7 scorer's table with a pencil, writing the Rotary's new time at the top of their sheet. The Rotary man looked over at her.

She knew exactly what she had said on December eleventh, and where the sweep had come across it. She had never let a thing go unconfirmed on purpose until a handshake in her own pit.

"Your lanes will be ready in twenty-five minutes," she said.

Ray set the cover back on and went back through the pit door.

The Rotary man followed her to the counter. "And the banquet, same night? My wife wants to know. She'll need the start time for her calendar."

Ellen unpinned his card and moved it down twenty-five minutes.

* * *

At 10:52 Ellen penciled the day onto the pace sheet.

It was graph paper, taped to the counter beside the register. A straight line ran from zero on December first to Judy's circled number at the end of February. Under it ran the season, a day at a time in pencil, and since the first week of December the season had stayed below the line. Tonight's point sat on it.

She didn't say anything.

Denise had her coat on and her hat with the earflaps. She read the sheet over the register. "Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to frame it." Denise pulled the flaps down and went out the front with her keys in her hand.

Ellen walked the lanes and switched off the scorer's lamps a pair at a time. At lane 10 the steel door scraped, and Ray came through it wiping his hands. He stopped at the pegboard. She had booked him for the whole day on a work order Thursday, at his per-call rate, and he had signed in on the card at nine-thirty that morning anyway. He finished the line while she stood there. 01/16 / 0930 / 2255 / ALL / PUSH.

He unclipped the radio from his jacket and held it out, and she took it.

She put on her coat, took the flashlight off its hook, and went out for the sandwich board. He went out to his truck. It was on the shoulder of Kettle Road at the foot of the drive. He had moved it there at eleven, when the lot filled, to free a space for a party. They walked down at the same pace and didn't talk.

The night was clear and still, about two degrees. The sign on its pole by the road gave more light than she needed. She kept the flashlight on.

At the scope she had known men by their voices. Two words in her headset and she had the airline, the man, and whether he'd slept. She could have passed any of them in a terminal and never known it. All day she had had Ray's voice on her collar and his face somewhere behind ten machines, and she wanted to check the one against the other.

At the pole he stopped and tipped his head toward the sign.

"Tube on the K's fine. It's the transformer. Hear it?"

She listened. "It's on my list."

"It was on Arlene's list."

The sandwich board stood in the snowbank where she had put it at noon. FULL. NEXT LANE 7:40. She pulled it out.

"Why did you come up front at one twenty-five?" she said. "You could have talked a sixteen-year-old through that ball return on the radio."

"Fella I knew in Meredith—"

"No."

He stood there. Across the road the town beach lay flat and white out to the edge of the ice. He put his hands in his jacket pockets and took them out again.

"Couple things," he said. "You should have the first one before the other." He looked at the board in her hands. "Bank extended my letter. Through March thirty-first. I asked them December fourteenth."

"The morning of my first lesson."

"Yes."

He didn't add to it. She didn't ask him to.

"I'm keeping the letter," he said.

He looked across at the beach for a while, and back at her.

"And I'd like to kiss you, if you're agreeable."

A truck went along the far shore, a pair of headlights a mile off.

"I'm aware of the letter," Ellen said. "And that you asked."

She leaned the sandwich board against the pole.

She put her mitten flat on the stitching over his pocket, GAGNON & SON, with the G under her thumb. He had to stoop. She hadn't allowed for the seven inches, and she went up on her toes on the bad knee and didn't mind it. She kissed him once.

The flashlight stayed on in her other hand, aimed at the snowbank, lighting a circle of plow ruts. Over them the sign buzzed at the pitch it took below ten degrees. His hand rested on her shoulder blade, over the coat, and stayed there.

When he straightened up she looked down at the beam.

"I've had that on the ditch the whole time."

"I saw."

Headlights came over the top of the drive. Denise's car rolled down the hill slowly, stopped beside them, and the window came down.

"Defrost takes nine minutes," she said, and kept her foot on the brake. "Goodnight, Ray. Lock the front. The front sticks."

The window went up. She drove off toward town.

Ray watched the taillights go. "She's going to say something."

Ellen bent for the sandwich board. Ray reached for it at the same time.

* * *

At 11:40 Ellen sat down at the steel desk with the adding machine.

The strip had been pinned above the desk since December nineteenth. JAN / RECONCILE / ARLENE / LEAGUE. She had scheduled it for the night the first half of January closed, and January had closed on the push. At closing Arlene's lawyer had sent three years of summary statements, one line for lanes and one for the snack bar, and Ellen had bought the building on those. The green books had stayed on their shelf. The space heater ran under the desk. Behind the closed lanes the baseboard ticked as it cooled.

She took down three of Arlene's ledgers and laid them open side by side. 1979. 2004. 2025.

She lined up her own December and January by night of the week against Arlene's last three winters. Tuesday and Thursday came out the thinnest columns again. This time she went looking for the reason, with one remark of Ray's from storm night still in her head.

The 2004 book, the September page, in green ink: *Lineage stays $7.50. Per league.*

She took down the books after it. 2009. 2014. 2019. The same figure every fall, entered without comment.

The 1979 book, October: *N. says house adds to the prize fund. Good for the league.* N. was Normand, Arlene's husband. After that came a line every few years, a quarter here, fifty cents there, until the house was putting in more than the bowlers did.

Every spring the same three entries. Banquet. Trophies. Free strings, secretary and captains. Paid by the house.

Down the margins, year after year, in a smaller hand: *R.G., no charge, wouldn't take it. R.G., no charge, wouldn't take it.* In March of 2011: *R.G., no charge, tried to give him a ham.*

Beside the 2004 lineage line, smaller still, Arlene had written *Voyons donc*, her mother's French for *oh, come on*.

Ellen read it twice. Arlene had known what the league cost her, every fall for twenty years.

She pulled the adding machine over and built one week of league nights the way Arlene never had. Lineage and snack bar in. Staff, heat and lights until eleven, the prize money, the banquet, machine wear, and Ray's invoices at the rate she had written in over his. The tape curled out with a minus at the bottom. Seven hundred dollars a week. She ran it again with a lower heat figure and got nearly the same number, and got to her feet.

February went on the tape standing up. Six league weeks left. The seven hundred was already in her actuals. In December and January the parties and the holiday weeks had covered it. February had school vacation week, and nothing booked against it yet. She ran the season out to the end of February, and the tape said about $26,000 against Judy's circle.

The hole in her winter had two days of the week on it, Tuesday and Thursday. It was his league.

She worked the alternatives one at a time. Glow nights were already full. She had called every office on the Rotary list on Tuesday and booked one baby shower. The prize fund was the league's, and so was the lineage, and she ran a higher rate anyway and the tape came up short. One tape came within reach of the circle. It had the last Saturday in February on it, all ten lanes, sold to anyone who would pay.

She reached into the box for a blank index card and put it back.

*Unless the numbers say otherwise.* The numbers said otherwise now, in green ink and on a curl of paper, and she had said those words out loud in her own pit. She also knew which half he had heard, and that she had not asked for the readback. This afternoon he had said the first half to a customer, and she had let it stand.

She tore the tape off the adding machine and folded it once. At the counter she found the strip at the far end of the board. 02/27 / 1800 / LGM / 10. She uncapped her Sharpie over the empty REMARKS box, held it there, and put the cap back on.

* * *

At ten past one the counter lamp was the only light on. The lanes ran back from it into the dark, ten pinsetters stopped, the settees empty in their pairs. Through the front windows the sign was still lit at the road; she had forgotten the switch. Two radios sat in their chargers, each with a green light.

She sat down on the counter stool.

On the back of a score sheet she wrote the conversation out in order, the way she used to write up a shift change. Ledger. Seven hundred a week. Twenty-six. The promise, both halves.

Leading with the promise sounded like an excuse. She crossed it out.

The third began *Mr. Gagnon*, and she drew a line through it before she reached the comma.

What she sent said: *Sunday 1400. Here. About the league.*

She set the phone face up on the score sheet. It lit forty seconds later, at 1:14 in the morning.

*ok*

Ellen turned the phone face down, took the flashlight off its hook, and went out to the vestibule to find the switch for the sign.
