## 2. Where I got bored

1. **Scene 1, about 12% in:** "She squared Ray's invoice on the blotter. It was on a Gagnon & Son pad, ruled in her format, with the job and the hours and the rate and a column for the part, all of it in his square printing." Her daughter has called on the wrong night with news, and I'm reading a description of an invoice form. I know the book wants me to find the invoice tender. I just wanted the phone call.
2. **Scene 3 opening, about 55%:** "Behind her the fryer was coming up to heat for eleven, and the oil had begun to smell like itself. Her black-light tubes lay in their carton by the register, packed till next time." They have just kissed in front of forty people (or near enough). I want the fallout, and I'm handed fryer oil and a carton.
3. **Scene 4 opening, about 70%:** "Roof rakes hung in a row from the ceiling over the aisles, and the store smelled of kerosene and birdseed." I've been in this store twice already, and it smelled of birdseed the first time. Re-establishing it slows the run-up to the ask.
4. **Scene 4, about 92%:** "I asked Judy before Christmas: my own money lowers the line, slowly, and all of it wouldn't buy back half of January." My eyes slid off this. It's a banking rule, offered just as the emotional scene peaks, and I had to go back to Ch 2 to remember who Judy is.

Bigger boredom, at book level: by the end of Ch 5 I can state the entire plot in one sentence, and the chapter didn't add a single surprise to it. She'll miss by January unless she gets Linda's name, and he said no. The no was predictable from Ch 3.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **When is the phone call?** "On a Thursday night a call from Kate was news." Only at "Fifteen minutes, folks" and in the next scene do I learn it's New Year's Eve. The last chapter ended before Christmas, and the time jump is handled in one subordinate clause ("Christmas had come and gone with the K still dark").
- **"Scale of one to ten."** Scale of what? Pain? Money? Trouble? "You said two with your knee in a brace" means I'm meant to infer it's how bad things are, but I'm guessing.
- **"The rest is still the rest?"** This only means something if I remember "The house." / "Not the rest." / "I swear." from Ch 2. I didn't fully remember. It only resolves in the last scene ("the house my daughter let me spend").
- **Why did Kate take nights through March?** The page doesn't say, and Ellen pointedly doesn't ask. I understand that not asking is the point, but I don't know whether it matters to the plot (is Kate coming north? Freeing her days?). Now it's a dangling thread.
- **"came out where it had come out on the twenty-second."** What happened on the 22nd? I assume she ran the numbers then, but it reads like a reference to something I missed.
- **The money rules.** In Ch 2 the bar was that the winter clears $30K "with every dollar it made still sitting in the account." Here, "my own money lowers the line, slowly," and "Anything that counts has to have sat in my name since before I signed." What line, and lowered by what ratio? I came away knowing only "his money can't help, hers can't help enough."
- **"Ray had said it first."** Normie said it first, in Ch 4. I think the sentence means Ray said it before Ellen had to, but as written it contradicts the previous chapter.
- **The old kitchen chair behind the counter "that she had never seen anybody sit in."** He glances at it before saying no, so it is clearly loaded. Whose chair? His father's? Linda's? I have no handle on it, and Ellen has only been in the store a couple of times, so "never seen anybody sit in" carries little weight.
- **"derby weekend."** As far as I can find, this is its first mention in the book.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **Derby weekend appears exactly when she needs it.** A lake full of visitors with "nowhere warm to be" on the very Saturday of Linda's Memorial is the whole basis of her plan, and the book never mentioned it before this page. It reads like the solution was built for the scene.
- **The bank rule closing Ray's money.** The obvious safer option is that the man who loves her and has "some put by" helps. The page does close it, which is good. But it closes it with a rule announced for the first time in the same breath ("Anything that counts has to have sat in my name since before I signed for the building"). It sounds like a door the author locked, not one the lender locked.
- **Ellen didn't see January coming until the 22nd?** She's a retired controller who plans for everything ("she had planned for most things"), bought on a single winter's revenue, and Arlene's ledgers exist ("It's in her ledgers"). That she only now finds the January hole strains belief. If she knew all along, the page should say so.
- **"Nothing else I can put on that weekend is the size of her name."** That's asserted, not shown. Why not run a separate open on derby weekend with some other name on it? I assume because the Memorial takes all ten lanes, but the page leaves me to work that out, and I only half do.
- **Ray naming his own conflict of interest:** "And I know what my no's worth, coming from the man who buys if you miss. Nothing." It's in character for a fair-dealing man. But it's so tidy, the exact sentence the scene needs, that it reads like the author speaking through him. People under that much pressure rarely say the thesis aloud.
- **Denise as delivery service.** "Since Linda, he can't stand that house after supper... He thinks nobody knows. Everybody knows." This is the third chapter in which Denise walks in to tell Ellen (and me) what something means. The information is good. The device is showing.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **The same climax device on every kiss or near-kiss.** Ch 1: "she knew it had all come out of order and left it that way." Ch 3: "somewhere back around the motor the order had gone out of it, and she heard that it had, and she let it run." Ch 5: "She had always known the time... and somewhere in this it went. She did not know when. She did not go after it." It's the same move three times. By the third, I saw it coming.
- **Feeling explained after the gesture:**
  - "Three items, with no space left in them for a question: the decision, the date, done. Ellen had briefed people that way her whole working life, and she knew whose kitchen table Kate had learned it at."
  - "She had never in her life said *I am asking* to anyone. The first time had gone to him, across a sack of light bulbs." The callback to "I don't ask" in Ch 1 is good. Spelling it out kills it.
  - "Money was the one thing he knew how to hand across a counter."
  - "He gave it to her in short pieces with nothing joining them." Here the narrator is describing the dialogue I have just read.
  - "One more sentence belonged in the room, and she had thought it would have to come from her. He got there first."
- **Every scene ends on a button:** "don't throw it till I say." / "What time is it?" / "It's January." / "He went out to his supper." That's four for four, and every chapter so far ends the same way ("Ray won't." "That's the bell." "Honey." "Net thirty.").
- **Recycled details:** "the old white seam along one finger" and "big and nicked" (Ch 1, near verbatim). Motor heat in the face (Ch 2 "the warmth came off the motor into his face", Ch 4 heat along the catwalk, Ch 5 "each one she passed put its heat into her face"). Birdseed in the store (Ch 2). Roof rakes (Ch 4). His collar (Ch 3, used again here twice).
- **Everyone does weather jokes.** Denise: "Clearing from the west, with a good chance of Ray." / "You should've asked my knees." Ellen: "I ran league on the last forecast I trusted." Denise is borrowing Ellen's register, and the joke feels written rather than said.
- **Kate and Ellen talk alike:** "I took nights through March. Charge nurse. Signed Tuesday." / "Understood." The page says this is deliberate (she learned it at Ellen's table). Even so, on the page it's two of the same voice.
- **"the oil had begun to smell like itself"** is a writer's phrase, not Ellen's.
- **"He said it no louder than a price"** is a shopkeeper's comparison in Ellen's POV.

## 6. What I think is going on (prediction, committed)

What the page has already given me: Ellen sold her house, with Kate's consent, to buy the alley, and swore to Kate she wouldn't spend "the rest." Her loan needs the winter to clear $30K in the account. The December parties got her close, but January (league only, Saturdays blank) sinks her "by about the whole of January." The one thing big enough to fix it is turning Linda's Memorial (the last Saturday in February) into an open on the lake's derby weekend, and Ray has refused, knowing his refusal profits him. Ray's backup contract is what lets Arlene keep her place at The Pines, so he can't simply tear it up. Ray meant the alley to be where he went in the evenings once the store was Danny's, and since Linda died he sits in his truck in the dooryard rather than in the house, the same thing his father did in '74. Locker 14 is still padlocked, and Ray has the key and "doesn't use it." The K quote is still unsigned.

My prediction for Ch 6 (about 60% sure): Ray does something that costs him on the scale her house cost her. Most likely he goes to Arlene and finds a way to secure her Pines place without the backup contract, by putting the store up a second time and breaking the oath he swore over his father in the truck, so that his no stops paying him. Second guess (about 25%): he works openly to get her through January without giving up Linda's name (Legion, the Highway crew, derby people), billing her for every hour. Third (about 15%): locker 14 opens. I also expect Kate to show up in person before March, and that her nights-through-March is what makes that possible.

The ending: locker 14 holds something of Linda's (her ball, her shoes, an entry for some open) that tells Ray she'd have wanted strangers bowling her night. He gives the name. The Memorial runs as an open on derby weekend with all ten lanes going. Denise finally wins the cup, or comes second a fifth time and laughs about it. Ellen clears the line by a margin small enough to count aloud, or misses and Ray declines to buy. They end together, and the last image is the K lit on the sign: Kettle Lanes, whole.

## 7. The listener pass

- **Opening line has no speaker.** "Mom. Scale of one to ten." A listener has no idea who this is until the next sentence. Then comes a run of untagged one-liners ("Two." / "You said two with your knee in a brace." / "The rest is still the rest?" / "Still the rest.") where by ear I lose who is who.
- **"the far register" five or six times in scene 4:** "Danny was counting the drawer at the far register," "at the far register Danny bent," "At the far register Danny could have taken the drawer," "it carried to the far register anyway," "At the far register Danny's hands went still." Aloud it's a drumbeat.
- **"the sack of bulbs" about seven times in scene 4:** "paper sack," "across the sack from his," "across the sack of bulbs," "a sack of light bulbs," "down to the sack of bulbs," "pick up the sack," "past her and the sack of bulbs."
- **Hands, constantly, in scene 4:** "both hands on the wood," "My hands are on the counter," "She put both her hands," "Ray's hands stayed," "looked at her hands," "His hands had not moved," "His right hand came up," "His hand stopped short," "His hand went back down onto the counter beside the other," "Ray took his hands off the counter." I heard "hands" and "counter" more than anything anyone said.
- **"the steel" four times and "Past the masking" twice in scene 2**, plus "cheering" twice in close succession.
- **The kiss paragraph** is roughly 250 words in a single breath, ending in a list ("on midnight shifts with no clock in sight, in a snowed-in building with the power out, on her own front step, to the minute"). Aloud, the kiss gets lost in the clauses.
- **The colon sentence:** "I asked Judy before Christmas: my own money lowers the line, slowly, and all of it wouldn't buy back half of January." A voice can't perform the colon. The listener hears a stranger's name (Judy), then "lowers the line, slowly," and is lost.
- **Repeated sentence shape**, "He looked at X, and then at Y": "Ray looked at her hands on the edge of the counter, and then at her." / "He looked once over his shoulder at the old kitchen chair..., and then back at her." / "He looked up, saw the two cups, and then saw her." / "He looked at it there, and then he set his beside it."
- **"the voice" as a label:** "the voice of a nurse on her break," "The car-in-the-driveway voice," "the voice for giving a stranger directions." This continues a habit from every earlier chapter.
- **Works aloud:** the countdown ("Nine," "Five," "Four," "Zero!") and Denise's run-on "who do you think told the Bench, I'll tell you who, all forty." Both play well spoken.
