# Reader Notes: Chapter Five, "The Push"

## Where I got bored

- **"Honey, that's Kevin Pelletier."** This is Denise's pizza-driver speech: the brother-in-law at Market Basket, the one-headlight Impala, the car on the ice, Hollis with the rope. By now I can tell when a Denise aria is starting, and I skimmed it. It's the same move as Roger and the ladder, Roger and the mustard, and Bev Dubois. The comic small-town backstory monologue has turned into wallpaper.
- **The lunch-staff speech ("Well. I don't believe we'll be doing that...").** It's well written, but it's a third long scripted monologue in one chapter, after Denise's and before Denise's car speech. Everyone in this town delivers paragraphs.
- **"February went on the tape standing up."** In this paragraph and the alternatives paragraph after it, my eyes glazed over. The paragraph has a semicolon-joined accounting clause ("The seven hundred was already in her actuals; what December and January had were...") that I had to read twice. I got the point (about $26,000 against $30,000) and stopped following the working.
- **"You'll live" three times in the first ten lines.** The first one was funny. The third felt like the book poking me in the ribs.
- **The trophy-glass knuckle touch.** It happens again here, and I've lost count of how many times. It stopped meaning anything around chapter 3.

## Where I got confused

- **"Voyons donc."** It's untranslated French, and nothing told me Arlene was Franco-American. I could tell it meant something like "oh, come on," but the line I was meant to linger on was the one I understood least.
- **"N. says house adds to the prize fund."** Who is N.? A husband? Is it the "1979" Verna mentioned in Ch 1? I don't know if I was supposed to recognize the name.
- **"Arlene had known."** Known what, exactly: that the league lost money, or that Ray was carrying it? I got there, but only after I'd read the next paragraph.
- **"Tube on the K's fine."** The K has been dark since page one. Is he saying the bulb is fine and something else is broken? This should be a small, warm detail, and it went right past me.
- **The radio.** Ray's second transmission ("Four's table is fine") came after "Nobody had called it." For a moment I couldn't tell whether that meant he was listening in on her or the radio was broken.

## Where I stopped believing

- **Ellen finds the league hole in mid-January.** This is a woman who timed pinsetter cycles on night one, read the fryer manual twice, and noticed in Ch 3 that league nights were the thinnest columns. She waited a month to cost out her two league nights per week, and she bought the building without reading Arlene's ledgers. The book plays this as a twist. I read it as a convenient blind spot in a character who supposedly has none.
- **Denise's "I have nothing to report" speech from the car.** It's cute, but staged. Nobody sits nine minutes waiting on a defrost and then delivers a closing statement through the window.
- **"I'm keeping the letter. And I'd like to kiss you, if you're agreeable."** I believed Ray would say each half. I didn't believe he'd put them in one breath with nothing between them, and I didn't believe she'd answer with three "I'm aware"s and a kiss.

## Where it sounded like AI

- **"Her hands were steady on the pencil. She noted it the way she'd note the vestibule thermometer, and kept writing."** Narration labeling her calm for me.
- **"Two hours ago she had been the calmest person in the building, her pencil point resting on the straight line. The number had been two shelves above her head the whole time."** This is the neat summary line, the one that tells me how to feel about the scene I just read.
- **The three crossed-out drafts, then typing and deleting twice.** It's the stock "she struggles to write the message" sequence. I've seen it in a hundred books and shows.
- **Every scene ends on a placed object or a held gesture.** The Sharpie cap goes back on, the thumb stays over the keyboard, both of them reach for the sandwich board. The rhythm is very regular by now.
- **The tics.** Ellen's ATC phrases ("say intentions," "in trail," "stand by"), Ray's "Fella I knew," Denise's "Honey," "You'll live." Each character is a set of signature lines. They're consistent, but you can see the machinery.
- **Talk all in one register.** The lunch lady, the coach, and Denise all speak in the same polished, rhythmic small-town cadence.

## What I want to know next

- What happens at Sunday 1400? Does she tell him about the championship before he hears it from someone else?
- Will she sell the last Saturday in February? Will she use the "unless the numbers say otherwise" half of the promise she knows he never heard?
- Does Ray's extended letter mean he's waiting for her to fail even now, after the kiss?
- Hollis's derby is on the same weekend. Is that the out, or the collision?
- What was she about to type at the end?

These questions pull hard. This is the first chapter where the money plot and the romance plot are aimed straight at each other.

## Best moment

> Down the margins, year after year, in a smaller hand: *R.G., no charge, wouldn't take it. R.G., no charge, wouldn't take it.* In March of 2011: *R.G., no charge, tried to give him a ham.*

The ham is perfect. In one line it tells me who Ray is, who Arlene was, and what Ellen is about to have to take away from him. A close second is "Kettle Maintenance, say intentions." / "Intentions are to quit."

## Did the kiss feel earned?

Half earned. The pit scene in Ch 4 earned it: the 187 told from under the table, the marble cake, his laugh at "Traffic, twelve o'clock." This chapter didn't add to it. They share a radio, one exchange over a ball return, and a walk to the road. The paragraph about her wanting to check his voice against his face is the narration doing the courtship for them. The kiss itself is good and restrained: the mitten on the stitching, the flashlight on the ditch, "I saw." The lead-in feels scheduled.

## Does the ledger discovery land as a real blow?

The arithmetic doesn't land. I was told in Ch 3 that league nights were thin, and in Ch 4 that "Arlene never charged the league for a lot of things," so $700 a week was no surprise. What lands is the promise. She knows which half of her sentence he heard, she let it stand at 1:25 that afternoon, and the only fix is his wife's championship, an hour after she kissed him. That's a real blow, and it's the best structural choice in the book so far.

## Do I want to keep going?

Yes, more than after any earlier chapter.

## Verdict

This is the strongest chapter yet structurally, because the kiss and the ledger fall on the same night. It's padded by side-character monologues and repeated tics, and it's weakened by Ellen implausibly missing her own league math until now. I'd keep reading.
