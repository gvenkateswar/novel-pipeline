# Chapter 5: Midnight

"Mom. Scale of one to ten."

Ellen called her daughter on Sundays at seven. This was Thursday, so the call was news before Kate said a word, and she was making it on a break from the emergency department in Manchester, with a monitor going behind her that nobody was hurrying for.

The office door was shut on the house, and the noise came through the wood anyway, ten lanes and the whole glow-night crowd brought down to one low, steady roar, which was how Ellen liked a full house best, with a wall between her and it. Her phone lay face up on the steel desk. Her left hand lay flat on January's book: the league nights down the page in her printing, Tuesdays and Thursdays from the fifth on, and between them the Saturdays, white from margin to margin.

"Two."

"You said two with your knee in a brace."

Ellen took her hand off the Saturdays.

"The rest is still the rest?"

"Still the rest."

Something clanged behind Kate, and she let it. "I took nights through March. I'm charge on nights, and I signed Tuesday."

Ellen heard it come down the line in order, the decision and then the date and then nothing, because nothing was left in it for anyone to weigh. She knew the shape well enough. She had briefed a household in it for most of Kate's life, outcomes at the kitchen counter and at the foot of the stairs, the shift changed, the knee a two, the retirement and its date, and Kate had taken down nearly every one of them. Kate had held this one two days, until it was finished and could be reported, and then called on a Thursday from work, when nothing anyone said could reach it. Ellen had taught her that, and she liked it no better from this end.

"Understood."

She drew Ray's invoice out from under the book and squared it to the blotter. It was on a Gagnon & Son pad, in her format to the letter, rate and hours and part, in a square printing pressed hard enough to come through to the sheet beneath, and on the line for the work he had written *Lane three. Table setting late at the top. Trued her up.* On anybody else's paper she would have struck the *her* and initialed the change, and on his she let it stand. Clipped behind it was the shop's quote for the K, unsigned, and Christmas had come and gone with the K still dark at the road.

Through the door, under the roar, seven's sweep stopped partway through its stroke and did not come round again. He had taken it down to have it done by midnight. So he was in the pit behind seven, under the one caged bulb over seven and eight, and she could have walked to him in the dark.

"You could ask me something sometime," Kate said. "Before."

"I'm listening."

Kate was quiet long enough for the monitor behind her to go twice. "You've got the voice on. The car-in-the-driveway voice."

Ellen knew which morning Kate meant, the one after the two a.m. shift, when she had driven home and sat in the car in the driveway with the engine off until the school bus came, and Kate had kept that morning all this time and given it a name, and Ellen was hearing the name for the first time.

Before she could answer either of them, Denise came through the door on the PA. "Fifteen minutes, folks. Get a ball in your hand and don't throw it till I say."

\* \* \*

"Ten," Denise said on the PA, and the house said it back to her, as it had every New Year's Eve since her first winter behind the counter, every bowler in the building up on an approach with a ball in hand and nobody throwing.

Ellen came through the steel door by lane ten with a paper cup of sparkling cider in each hand and went along the catwalk in the dark. Out past the masking, Denise's black lights had turned every pin in the house white, and the glow came in under the masking along the floor of the pit, a pale band at each deck, enough to walk by. The motors gave off their warmth as she passed them, a little heat off each machine in turn, the one warm thing along that cold cinderblock wall.

"Six," said Denise. "Five."

Ray was behind seven with the sweep back in, wiping his hands on a rag under the caged bulb. He had heard the steel door, and he was already looking up the catwalk for her when she came into the light. He had finished in time, so seven would throw with the others, and his invoice would stop at midnight; she would have put money on both.

She set his cup on the kickback beside his rag, since his hands were not fit for it yet, and laid her free palm flat on the steel, which still held the warmth of the motors. He looked at the cup and then at her. Standing in a pit with the bulb over his head he was a good deal of man to look up at, and she did not mind it at all.

"Two," said Denise. "One."

On zero every lane in the house threw.

The balls came down all ten at once, and from the pit she heard them the whole length of the maple before they arrived, one low roll coming at her from everywhere together, and then the decks took them. The crash broke on both sides of her and straight ahead, ten racks going over inside a single breath, wood into wood and wood into the cushions, and the steel under her palm jumped once with all of it. Behind it the machines came down together to sweep, and the noise went rolling along the catwalk long after the first of it was spent, and she stood in the middle of it and let it go on as long as it wanted.

"That's what I bought it for."

"I know. You told my lock."

She had said it at her own front door with the flashlight on his hands, the morning he did all his talking to her lock.

She picked his cup up off the kickback and gave it to him. "Is this on the invoice?"

"Not since midnight."

He held the cup and did not drink from it. Past the masking the house was cheering, and somebody on the near lanes had a noisemaker, and Denise was saying something on the PA that nobody could hear. He did not say Happy New Year. Ellen watched his mouth not say it, and she did not know what he was keeping it for, and she began to count the minute he kept it, because she counted things. On seven's deck in front of them the ten pin stood alone in its wood, glowing white, and nobody went to clear it.

"I know the whole sentence now," he said.

"I know you do."

She set her cup on the kickback. He set his beside it, and that was all the deciding either of them did out loud, two paper cups side by side on the warm steel with the cider still working in them. Then he moved first, and put his hands on both sides of her face, slowly, as if he expected to be given the procedure. His palms were warm from the motor and smelled of the rag, and he had to come a long way down to reach her. She had known he was tall. She had known it across a counter and down sixty feet of lane, and now she knew it from a few inches off, with the bulb behind his head and his thumbs at the edges of her cheekbones, and it was a different fact at this distance. He kissed her, and she closed her hand on his collar, on the canvas gone soft at the fold, and held on and kissed him back. The minute went out from under her somewhere in there. She noticed only afterward that it had gone, a count she had been holding and had set down without deciding to, and she could not have said when. He said her name once, low, the way he talked in a loud room, so that it came under all that noise to her and went nowhere else. Then he kissed her again, and past the masking the house cheered on for as long as it liked.

When he lifted his head she still had his collar in her hand.

"What time is it?"

\* \* \*

"Forty people," Denise said, with the Memorial cup in one hand and the cloth going round it in the other, "watched the two of you come out of that pit a few minutes past twelve with your faces arranged, and did a single one of the forty go home and keep it to themselves? They did not. The Bench had it by breakfast. Verna had it in writing, and she's carried a hundred-and-one average since Carter, so you know she's careful. You both looked very respectable, I'll give you that, for two people coming out from behind the machines. You want my forecast? Clearing from the west, with a good chance of Ray."

"I ran league on the last forecast I trusted."

Denise gave that the look it had coming and turned the cup to do the other side.

The fryer was coming up to heat for eleven, and the snack bar smelled of it, the oil warming in the well with nothing in it yet. Ellen sat on the end stool with her coffee. Denise's black-light tubes lay in their carton by the register, her own rig, packed to go home in her trunk, and the trophy case stood open beside the locker with the brass padlock. The Memorial cup was the newest thing in the case, with Linda's name across the front and the winners' little plates around the base, none of them Denise's, and Denise had been dusting it since she came in, if anybody asked her.

Denise set the cup back on its square of clean glass and kept the cloth.

"Since Linda, he can't stand that house after supper. Half the nights he sits out in his own dooryard with the heater running and the Bruins on, because the truck makes some noise. This place was where he meant to go, once the store was Danny's. He thinks nobody knows. Everybody knows."

Her cloth went once round the brass padlock on the locker and came away without touching it.

Ellen saw his truck in his dooryard on School Street with the dash lit and the windshield fogging at the corners, and the passenger side empty beside him, and a hockey game going on the radio that she would not have followed past the first whistle. She put herself in that seat, with her hands in her vest pockets and the heater blowing on her shins, and sat there long enough to know she would like it. Then she took herself out again.

"If Linda's name were on an open, who would come?"

"Everybody she ever beat. Which is everybody."

Denise's cloth stopped on the glass, and she looked at Ellen across the counter for a while, the way she read a register tape before she tore it off, and Ellen watched her follow the question to where it went.

"Don't you touch Linda's night. And I say that as a woman who's been second to it four times, with my Lakeview Diner."

Ellen got up off the stool. January ran again in her head, the league nights and the white Saturdays, and came out where it had come out on the twenty-second.

"You're standing. What happened?"

"It's January."

\* \* \*

Danny was counting the drawer at the far register, and his father stood at the near one with both hands flat on the counter and let him. Ellen had been in the store long enough to know where everyone in it was, which was at the two registers and nowhere else: the clerks had gone home, and the Bench had gone at four, and their three stools stood in a row under the roof rakes with nobody on them. The store smelled of kerosene and birdseed and the cold off the door glass, and Main Street had been dark for half an hour. Her carton of bulbs for the pit's cages sat on the counter between her and Ray, rung up and bagged. She had not picked it up.

Danny looked over once, and his father's hands stayed where they were. When Danny looked over a second time, Ray's right thumb was moving on the wood, a small, even stroke and back again, keeping time with the bills going down at the other end of the counter.

"You're counting it in your head," Danny said.

"My hands are on the counter."

"Tell your thumb."

Ray's thumb stopped, and Danny went back to his drawer, and Ray kept his hand still on the wood with what looked to Ellen like real effort.

"I am asking you for Linda's name, on her day."

Ray looked up from his hands to her face, and kept looking, as if he wanted to hear it a second time and would not ask for it.

"You're asking me."

"I am."

She had chosen this counter for it, under his store's lights, with his son at the far register, so that whatever he answered would be answered where he answered things. She had not been willing to ask him in the pit, where she had kissed him.

"The last Saturday in February is derby weekend, and the lake will be full of people with nowhere warm to be. I want her Memorial open to anyone who pays to enter, with her name on the cup, and the league bowls with everybody."

Ray said nothing. His hands lay flat on the wood, big and nicked, with the old white seam along one finger, and he kept them there.

"If the winter goes the way winters go here, I miss by about the whole of January."

She heard her own voice go across the counter, level and courteous and in no hurry, the voice for telling a stranger about weather he would have to go through, and she listened to it the way she listened to anyone's and could find nothing wrong with it. That had never once been a comfort to her. Underneath it everything was running at once. The door was at her back, and she knew how many steps it was, and which board by the salt pallet would give. The count at the far register had slowed. Ray's breathing had lengthened, the way a man's did when he had made up his mind to let someone finish. And under all of that, arriving late, was the plain fact that she had never in her life said *I am asking* to anyone, at work or at home, and the first time she had said it, she had said it to him.

"Nothing else I can put on that weekend is the size of her name."

"If I miss, you buy it at appraisal, and I get back less than the house my daughter let me spend."

At *the house* Ray's face changed, and stayed changed. He had been on his knees at her front door when she said those two words into the phone to Kate, and now he had the rest of them.

His hands stayed flat on the counter the whole time she talked. He let her finish, and she wanted those hands off the wood and on her face, where they had been two nights ago with the whole house throwing behind the masking, and she stood with her own in her vest pockets and wanted it.

"No."

He let that stand by itself before he gave her the rest, and he looked at her the whole time.

"It's my wife's name. I won't put it on a cup strangers win for money. Linda wouldn't have wanted her night sold."

It was a good reason, and it was his own, and he had given it to her plainly, with nothing round it. She had known before she came in that he would have one.

She had the next fact ready, and he said it before she could.

"And I know what my no's worth, coming from the man who buys if you miss. Nothing."

At the far register Danny's hands went still on the bills. It was what Normie had said at the party, near enough, and Ray had taken it off Normie and said it himself, at his own counter, in front of his son.

Ray's right hand came up off the wood. "I've got some put by—"

"I asked Judy before Christmas: my own money lowers the line, slowly, and all of it wouldn't buy back half of January." She kept her voice exactly where it had been. "Yours couldn't count if you gave it, because it has to have sat in my name since before I signed for the building, and it's what pays Arlene if I miss."

Ray's hand went back down flat. He looked at her across the carton of bulbs for a long moment, with nothing left that either of them could put on the counter, and then down the counter at his son.

At the far register Danny had stopped counting altogether.

It was five past five. She knew it the way she always knew the time, and he knew it the way he knew his supper. He took his canvas jacket down off the hook by the door and put it on, one arm and then the other, and settled the collar she had held on to in the pit, and stood with his hand on the door.

"Then I'll make my no cost me what yours costs you."

He went out to his supper.
