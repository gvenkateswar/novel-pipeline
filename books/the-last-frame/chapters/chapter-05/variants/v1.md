# Chapter 5: On Zero

"Mom. Scale of one to ten."

Ellen had the phone face up on the steel desk with Kate on speaker and the office door shut on the lanes. She called her daughter on Sundays at seven, so a call on a Thursday night, New Year's Eve, from a break room in Manchester with a monitor chiming somewhere behind it, was news before anybody said what it was. It was glow night, and the house came through the door muffled, ten lanes and the crowd on top of them, and under that, if she listened for it, lane seven's sweep coming down a little late each time. Ray had been behind lane seven with it since eleven.

January's book lay open under her right hand. She had ruled it herself, a line for every night of the month, and the league nights were filled in down the page in her printing, Tuesdays and Thursdays, and every Saturday was a line she had ruled and left blank. She kept her hand flat on the Saturdays.

"Two."

"You said two with your knee in a brace."

Kate did not wait for an answer to that. She took her mother's vitals in the order she'd been trained to take them.

"The rest is still the rest?"

"Still the rest."

"Okay. Good. So." Kate's voice went level, and Ellen heard the level before she heard the news. "I took nights through March. Charge on nights. I signed Tuesday."

It came in order: the decision, the date, and nothing left open for anyone to weigh in on. Ellen had handed her daughter every piece of news in that shape since Kate was old enough to be handed news, finished before it was said. Kate had learned it at the kitchen table, from the woman on the other end of this call.

"Understood."

She squared Ray's invoice on the blotter. It was the first one he had written her, and it was in her format, the date and the machine and his hours, the part and the rate and the total down the right-hand side, in the same square printing he put on the tags of his spare parts. *Lane three, table setting late at the top. Set her up right.* On anybody else's paper Ellen would have struck out that *her* and written the correct word above it. She left his where it was. Clipped behind it was the Laconia shop's quote for the K, in his hand too, and still unsigned, because Christmas had come and gone with the K dark out at the road.

Through the door, lane seven's sweep came partway down and stopped, and did not go back up. He had it out, then. He would be on his knees on the catwalk behind lane seven, a few feet in from the steel door, with the caged bulb over his hands, and she could have walked to him in the dark without touching a wall.

"You could ask me something sometime," Kate said. "Before."

"I'm listening."

"You've got the voice on. The car-in-the-driveway voice."

Ellen knew which morning Kate meant: the one after the two a.m. shift, when she had sat in the car in the driveway with the engine off until the school bus came, and until tonight she had not known that her daughter kept a name for it.

The PA came on past the door, and Denise's voice came through the wood.

"Fifteen minutes, folks. Get a ball in your hand and don't throw it till I say."

\* \* \*

"Ten," Denise said on the PA, and the whole house said it with her.

Every bowler in the building was up on an approach with a ball in hand, every lane full, because at midnight on New Year's Eve Denise counted the house down and nobody threw till zero, and nobody had since her first winter at the counter.

"Nine."

Ellen came through the steel door by lane ten with a paper cup of sparkling cider in each hand and went along the catwalk behind the machines. Denise's black lights hung over the lanes on the far side of the masking, and what got under it into the pit was a low violet stripe along the floor that turned the paper cups white as chalk and lit the rolled sleeves of her shirt. The motors were warm. She felt each one go by on her right as she passed it, the warmth coming off them along the catwalk the way it came off a row of stoves.

"Eight."

Ray was behind lane seven, getting up off his knees with a rag in his hands, and the sweep was back in the machine. He had been on it since eleven and had it running again with the count already started, and his sheet for tonight would say eleven to twelve, in her format.

He looked at the two cups, and then at the machine beside him.

"Lane seven throws with the rest of them," he said.

"Four," the house said. Ellen set his cup on the kickback and put her free palm flat on the steel beside it, where the motors had left their warmth, and stood next to him.

"Two. One."

"Zero."

They threw, all ten at once. She heard the balls before anything else, ten of them on the maple together, a low roll she felt in the steel under her hand before it reached her ears, and then the decks took them almost in the same instant, crash on crash so close together that for a second it was one sound, and then it broke into every one of its parts, wood on wood and wood on the side boards and the cushions, pins spinning off into the pit and knocking against the steel, every lane in the house going down within a breath of the others. It came up through the kickback into her palm and along her arm. The ball returns took it over under the floor, ten of them rumbling back toward the people who had thrown, and past the masking the house began to shout. She stood in the pit with her hand on the steel and let the whole of it go through her.

"That's what I bought it for."

"I know," Ray said. "You told my lock."

She had, at her own front door, holding the flashlight on his hands while he talked to the lock.

She picked his cup up off the steel and gave it to him. "Is this on the invoice?"

"Not since midnight."

He didn't say Happy New Year. She gave him the minute to say it in, and watched his mouth not say it, and he used the whole of the minute that way, holding a cider he hadn't tasted, with the rag still in his other hand. Past the masking the house was cheering, whistles and stamping and Denise's voice over the top of it with no words in it that Ellen could make out. Under the masking she could see lane seven's deck, bright under Denise's lights, the wood lying every which way, and the ten pin standing up out of it in the back corner, glowing. Nobody came to clear it.

"I know the whole sentence now," he said.

"I know you do."

She set her cup down on the kickback. He looked at it there, and then he set his own down beside it, carefully, the way he would set a part on the bench when he meant to come back for it, and then both their hands were empty, and neither of them had moved yet, and both of them were choosing it.

He moved first. He brought his hands up, both of them, and took her face between them, slowly enough that she could have stepped out from between them at any point. His palms were rough and smelled of grease and the rag, and up this close he was taller than she kept him in her head between times, so that she had to tip her head back into his hands to see him at all. He said her name once, low, under all that noise, and she heard every letter of it. Then he kissed her. She took hold of his collar, the canvas stiff in her fist, and kissed him back, and pulled, a little, so he would know she meant it, and he came down the rest of the way to her. Past the masking Denise was saying something on the PA, and the house was laughing at it. She had been keeping the minute since Denise said ten. She kept minutes the way other people kept their hands in their pockets, without deciding to. Somewhere between his hands and his collar the count went out of her, and she let it go, and when he lifted his head and his hands stayed where they were, she had no idea how long it had been.

"What time is it?"

\* \* \*

"Forty people," Denise said. She had the Memorial cup out of the trophy case and was going round it with a cloth at the counter, pretending she was dusting. "Forty, and who counted them, I did, watched the two of you come out of that pit a few minutes past twelve with your faces arranged, and you looked very nice, I'll give you that, for a woman who'd just come out of a pit, and did one of them say a word to you, no, they did not, they went home and said it to each other, and the Bench had it by breakfast, on New Year's Day, with the store shut. So you want a forecast, I'll give you a forecast. Clearing from the west, with a good chance of Ray."

It was ten in the morning, an hour before open bowling, and behind her the fryer was coming up to heat for eleven, the oil just beginning to smell like itself. Her black-light tubes, her own rig, sat in their carton by the register, waiting to go home in her car. Ellen sat on a stool with her coffee. From there she could see the whole length of the house, every lane dark, down to the steel door by lane ten, shut, with the machines cold behind it.

"I ran league on the last forecast I trusted," Ellen said.

Denise looked at her over the cup a while. Then she set it back on its shelf in the case and squared it there, and when she talked again she talked like Linda's oldest friend, which she was.

"Since Linda, he can't stand that house after supper. Half the nights he sits out in his own dooryard with the heater running and the Bruins on, because the truck makes some noise. This place was where he meant to go, once the store was Danny's. He thinks nobody knows. Everybody knows."

Her cloth went on round the brass padlock on the locker beside the case, close, and never touched it.

Ellen looked down the house at the steel door. She could see the cab as plainly as she could see the door: the dash lit, the heater going, a hockey game on the radio that she would not have been able to follow, and the passenger side empty. She put herself in that seat, with her coffee and her boots on his floor mat and the game going on without either of them listening, and sat there for one second. Then she took herself out again.

"If Linda's name were on an open, who would come?"

"Everybody she ever beat. Which is everybody." Denise said it straight off, and liked it, and then her cloth stopped on the glass.

"Don't you touch Linda's night. And I say that as a woman who's been second to it four times, with my Lakeview Diner."

Ellen got up off the stool. January ran again in her head, the way it had run every night since she ruled the book, league nights and open bowling and not one party on a single Saturday, and it came out where it had come out on the twenty-second.

"You're standing," Denise said. "What happened?"

"It's January."

\* \* \*

Danny was counting the drawer at the far register, and his father was standing at the main counter with both hands flat on the wood, letting him.

The Bench had gone home, and their three stools stood empty by the window. It was dark on Main Street already, and under the roof rakes hanging from the ceiling the store smelled of kerosene and birdseed. Ellen stood across the counter from Ray with her carton of caged bulbs for the pit between them, in a paper bag with the receipt stapled over the fold. She had paid for it, and she had not picked it up.

Danny counted the way she would have wanted a drawer counted, every bill face up and turned the same way, and his father stood and watched him do it right. Danny looked over at him once, and went back to the bills, and looked over again. Ray's right thumb was moving on the wood, a small, even tap, keeping a count of something nobody else could see.

"You're counting it in your head," Danny said.

"My hands are on the counter."

"Tell your thumb."

Ray's thumb stopped. Danny went back to the drawer without smiling, which was how she knew he was pleased.

"I am asking you for Linda's name, on her day."

Ray lifted his eyes from his hands. At the far register Danny's count slowed. Ray looked at her a good while before he said anything.

"You're asking me."

"I am."

She had chosen his counter for it, under his own lights, with his son at the far register, so that whatever he said would be said where he said things; she would not ask him in the pit, where she had kissed him.

"The last Saturday in February is derby weekend, and the lake will be full of people with nowhere warm to be. I want her Memorial open to anyone who pays to enter, with her name on the cup, and the league bowls with everybody."

That weekend every winter, Hollis's fishing derby put half the county out on the ice of Great Kettle, trucks and bob-houses and children in snowsuits, from first light until dark. Ray's hands stayed flat on the wood. She went on in the same voice.

"If the winter goes the way winters go here, I miss by about the whole of January."

She heard her own voice go out across the counter level and pleasant, the voice she used on a man at the cubbies who wanted a shoe size she didn't stock, and under it she was going as fast as she had ever gone on a bad night: what he would say, and in what order, and what she would answer to each, and which of her answers she could not give in front of Danny. She had the whole of it laid out before she came to the end of the sentence. Her voice did not hurry for any of it.

"Nothing else I can put on that weekend is the size of her name."

His thumb did not move this time.

"If I miss, you buy it at appraisal, and I get back less than the house my daughter let me spend."

Ray's hands had stayed flat on the wood through all of it, and he had let her finish. Two nights ago those hands had held her face in the dark behind lane seven, and she wanted them there now, across his counter, with his son ten feet away.

He took his time. He looked down at his hands, and over at Danny, who was counting with his head down, and at the empty chair behind the counter that she had never once seen anybody sit in, and back at her. Whatever he was going to say, he had said it to himself already somewhere, in the truck maybe, more than once.

"No."

She waited, because there would be more of it, and there was.

"It's my wife's name. I won't put it on a cup strangers win for money. Linda wouldn't have wanted her night sold."

He said them one at a time, short, with room after each, and every one of them was his own. She would have said the same about anything of hers with a name on it, and she knew that, and she needed it anyway.

She had one more true sentence ready, the one that would cost him most to hear, and she drew the breath for it.

"And I know what my no's worth, coming from the man who buys if you miss. Nothing."

At the far register Danny's hands went still on the bills. It was Normie's sentence from the staff party, and Ray had picked it up himself and said it out loud at his own counter, under his own lights, where his son could hear every word of it. He had looked at her to say it, with the same face that had said no.

Ray's right hand came up off the wood for the first time since she started, reaching for the one kind of help he knew how to hand across a counter.

"I've got some put by—"

She had that answer in order too, and she gave it to him in two sentences, in the same voice, so that he would have all of it.

"I asked Judy before Christmas: my own money lowers the line, slowly, and all of it wouldn't buy back half of January."

He heard it to the end.

"Yours couldn't count if you gave it, because it has to have sat in my name since before I signed for the building, and it's what pays Arlene if I miss."

Ray put his right hand back flat on the counter beside the other one. At the far register Danny had stopped counting.

It was five past five. Ellen knew it the way she knew where her own feet were. Ray came out from behind the counter, took his canvas jacket down off its hook by the door, and put it on, one arm and then the other. He stood with his hand on the door.

"Then I'll make my no cost me what yours costs you."

He went out to his supper.
