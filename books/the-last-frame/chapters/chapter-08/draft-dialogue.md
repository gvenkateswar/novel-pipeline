# Chapter 8: Position Relief

Gil Arsenault held the house ball out in a bowling glove he had no use for, his good wool overcoat still buttoned.

"House rules, Ray. Three strings, total pins, my house balls. You'll forgive the house balls; they're older than you."

Ray took it. It sat lighter in his hand than his own.

"I bowl with older every Tuesday, Gil, and he's standing right behind me."

"Five years," Hollis said.

Pine Ridge was warm clear to the back wall under screens nobody in this match looked up at, a building somebody could afford to heat, and Ray resented enjoying it.

"You called me for a match, and I like a match, and half your Tuesday division drove over to watch it and they're in my lounge eating pizza, so I've had my night whoever bowls. Terms. Ray takes it, the Highway Department and the Legion go home to Kettle Lanes on Tuesday at the season rate and bowl Linda's Memorial. My man takes it—"

"Ray pays our lineage here to the end of the season," Dale said, "and his team bowls for you."

"—and Gagnon and Son bowls for Pine Ridge," Gil said, as though nobody had spoken.

Dale Hutchins sat on the settee behind the table, coat zipped, his bag between his boots.

"I want it settled before Linda's night. Last time I took your word about her, the book was wrong. And it still says you paid a bank to buy her out if she's short." Dale looked at Ray's own checkbook, laid on the scorer's table. "Everything you put up, losing pays you."

"Then watch me bowl."

"And Linda's cup? My crew paid for it in September."

"You won't pay for it twice, Dale. I'll ask her to give the league its Memorial back."

Dale looked at him a while. "Bowl, then."

Denise had the scorer's table already, with a Kettle Lanes paper sheet and a pencil out of Ellen's cigar box, and nobody said a word about Tuesday. Gil looked at her sheet and up at his own screens.

"I'll take Mrs. Lavoie's pencil over my screens. I watched her pick up a four-horse with her third ball eleven years back, in the spring doubles. I still think about it."

Ray had seen her make that leave twice in thirty years. Her pencil stopped over the sheet for a second, then went on. Ray opened his pocketknife and held out his hand, and Denise put the pencil in it. He shaved it to a point and gave it back blunt end first.

The house ball rode wrong off his fingers, and his first ball took three.

"Holy mackerel."

One of the Highway crew laughed and quit. Gil's anchor, a lineman half Ray's age, bowled clean and quick and took the first two strings going away.

Before the third string the man from the Bench's end stool leaned on the rail in his coat.

"Had a dollar on Gil's man, Ray. Nothing personal. Man that's buying the place come March don't bowl hard to fill it."

Denise, loud enough to carry, to nobody: "He's a hundred-and-four average with a hundred-and-forty heart. Don't tell him I said either."

Gil's man finished his third string on a spare. Denise ran both columns and said it: nine. Nine pins behind, with ten on the deck. He needed every pin, with three balls to get them, and on the settee Dale sat with his bag between his boots, watching to see whether a man who'd be paid for losing would try.

He threw the first one where he meant to. The front of the rack went with a crack, and one pin came down sideways and lay still across the deck in front of three still standing at the back, like a plank somebody had laid across a ditch. The second ball he threw too hard, because he was looking at that plank, and it went by the near end of it and took one of the three and left two at the back, with the plank still lying across their feet.

The third ball was small and heavier than it looked, worn dull in two places by other men's palms. Sixty feet of somebody else's maple lay between him and the pins. He set his eyes on the near end of the lying pin and let the ball go easy, and it went the whole length without a wobble and touched the end of that pin, just touched it, and the pin came up off the deck end over end into the two at the back, and all three went over together with a sound like a bundle of shovel handles going over in a back room. Denise had her pencil moving before the last pin lay still.

Gil keyed his wireless mic, and two words came out of the ceiling.

"Kettle Lanes."

Dale stood and picked his bag up off the carpet. "Tuesday, then."

The lot was cold enough to ache in his teeth, and Hollis came out with him. The man from the end stool was at Ray's truck ahead of them, and he put a folded dollar into Hollis's glove without a word. Hollis put it in his waterproof notebook.

"She wrote the league a letter," Hollis said. "Verna read it out to the Thursday division, every word, and copied it in the book, and gave me the page for the committee. You read it yet?"

"No."

Hollis waited with the notebook open. "You read it yet?"

Ray held out his hand.

It was a yellow sheet off her legal pad with the gum still along its top edge. At the head of it, underlined once, was the date he had watched her write at her counter, the night he worked her key off his ring: the third of February. Her printing, every letter standing separate.

*The silence was mine, and so was the name. Mr. Gagnon took the fault for it on himself in this room, and I ask the secretary to strike that from the minutes.*

He folded it in three and put it inside his jacket, beside the blank envelope.

"You anchor Tuesday."

"Where'll you be?"

\* \* \*

He had his hand on the bolt before he knew it. She stood outside the door glass in the dark, hatless, with her own gloves pushed into her vest pocket and her hands bare. They were red across the knuckles. He had never turned that bolt before opening in his life, and he turned it.

Her car had been at the curb, engine off and dark inside, when he put the first light on. The store smelled of kerosene and cut brass and the Bench's pot, which he had put on before he unlocked anything. She came to the counter and stood with her weight on both feet, and he went back to the drawer Danny had already counted.

The Bench would be at the door at opening, and she had come before it.

"Dale's crew and the Legion came in my door last night. Dale paid me in cash and asked for a receipt. He asked me where you were, and I told him I did not know."

He had stopped counting at her first word.

His gloves lay beside the drawer, where he had pulled them off to count. He squared them cuff to cuff and left them on his side. She had taken nothing from him that wasn't on an invoice since these same gloves on the ice, and those she had handed back folded one into the other, and he was the man the March paper said would buy her building.

"I came here after I knew it."

He didn't answer it. Inside his jacket the yellow page lay folded in three against the blank envelope, and the creases had gone soft since Friday from his opening it in the truck and reading it and folding it up again. The date was at the head of it in the hand on his counter. That hand and the other one lay side by side a foot from his gloves, chapped white at the joints, and she kept them still. He could have put two fingers inside his jacket and laid the page between them, and it would have been said. He kept his hand on the gloves.

The door opened behind her, and the Bench came in out of the dark, stamping snow onto the mat, three in a row, and stopped. The one off the end stool got as far as "And the—" and shut his mouth.

She didn't stop, or turn. "You have no reason to believe I would have come anyway, and I have no way to give you one."

The Bench stood inside the door in their coats with nothing to call.

Then the man from the end stool, who had paid Hollis a dollar in the Pine Ridge lot, left the other two standing and went past her to the pot with his coat still on, poured a cup, black, and held it out to her across the end of the counter.

"Coffee's on."

Her hand went out for it.

\* \* \*

Arlene set the plate down in front of him before his coat was over the chair: ham, a boiled potato split open, and a spoon of her own mustard on the rim. She didn't ask if he was hungry. The ham was hot and salty and cut thick, and the mustard went up behind his eyes, and he ate and let it.

Wintergreen and cough drops came up through her floorboards from the pharmacy under the smell of the ham, and the scanner talked low to itself by the bread box. Past the boxes she had marked for The Pines, her window looked down Main Street to the dock and Gagnon and Son at the bottom of it.

"Why'd you sell it to her, Arlene?"

"You'd have kept it a museum, Raymond. I wanted it to live."

He ate, and didn't argue it.

The scanner said a car was off the road by the town beach, nobody hurt, and a wrecker was on its way.

"That'll be the Pelletier girl. She takes that corner like it owes her money."

"I want off the March paper. I'll have your lawyer draw whatever it takes."

"Do you know what you're asking me?"

"Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain, because I'd rather hear it from you than work it out by myself in the truck."

She brought her copy of the contract out of a box with no lid on it yet and laid it on the oilcloth by his plate, three pages stapled at the corner, Ellen's printing on the last one and his scrawl under it.

"If you aren't standing there, nobody buys it. The Pines gives my room to the next name, and I go on the bottom of that list at eighty-four, and I may not live to the top of it."

"You'd be paid, though, wouldn't you?"

"Months on, dear, when my lawyer's done selling it out from under her, and that condo man has the lot for what the dirt is worth."

"I know it."

"Then hold it. You keep it in your drawer, she makes her number, you tear it up after, and I've had my bed the whole time."

Ray put the fork down square beside the plate. The oilcloth had split at the corner by his plate the winter her furnace quit, and he looked at the split while he said it.

"Every clutch I put in over there might be going into my own machine come March, and she knows it, and Dale Hutchins knows it, and I'm done standing at the bottom of her drive."

Arlene turned the contract face down on the oilcloth and laid two fingers on the back of the last page.

"Tuesday night your truck sat outside your own store from supper till the league let out, with the engine running. Thirty years I've known where you were on a Tuesday. The next morning her car was at your door before your light was. I made you both sign that paper so I'd be sure of my bed, and I've been sure of it all winter, and the two of you have been sitting in cars."

He kept his eyes on the split in the oilcloth.

"In nineteen seventy-one I held Normand to a building I wanted and he didn't."

"He ran it eighteen years."

"And never once said so. I won't hold you to one you've stopped wanting, Raymond."

"Then you'll sign it."

"First. And none of it stands without hers. It's hers to risk as much as mine."

He had come up her stair for a paper he could carry down it finished, and the last line on it was Ellen's, and he would have to put it in front of her and ask.

Arlene got up and took the phone off the wall by the bread box and dialed her lawyer from memory. "It's Arlene Fortier. Put him on."

"Draw it so I sign first and Raymond under me, to stand only if the third name follows, and leave a line for Ellen Daly. Raymond will carry it to her himself, and he'll ask her."

\* \* \*

The door glass rattled in its frame under his first rap and his second, and before his hand came down a third time the door swung in and she was standing behind it. Her hand closed on his raised wrist and brought him in out of the wind. The wind came in with him off the lake and ran across the vestibule tile, and she shut the door on it with her other hand and held on to his wrist a second past it, her fingers warm through the cold cuff of his jacket, and let go.

The vestibule smelled of wet wool and road salt off the Tuesday division's boots, from a room he had not stood in tonight. Down the building the lanes were dark, with one light left on over the counter, and the release in his jacket had its fold against his chest from the door to the counter.

At the counter he took it out and laid it on the wood between them, folded, unopened. "That's what I came for. I'll ask you for it last."

Ellen looked at the page and left it lie, and stayed on her feet. "Then say the others first."

"What I said about the radio. You told me that night in the pit with a pin in your hands, and I kept it, and when I wanted something that would land I went and got it."

"It did. I have heard it every time I have keyed that microphone since."

He had picked it because it would land. He had heard it land in this building, and he had driven home and sat in his dooryard with the heater running and the radio off and heard it go on landing.

"I'm sorry for it, Ellen."

She took it standing and let it stand, and he let her.

"Hollis gave me what you wrote." He touched his jacket over the pocket where the yellow page was. "I watched you put the date on it."

Her weight went off the bad knee and came back onto it.

"I told Dale I'd ask you to give the league its Memorial back. I'm asking. Linda's name comes off the Open."

"Go ahead."

"League bowls its Memorial at two on the last Saturday, on all ten lanes."

"And the Open."

"Gets the morning and the evening, and a cup of its own, with whatever name you like on it."

He took a pencil out of her cigar box and turned it end for end. "Hollis says his committee voted on an Open, and nobody voted on a name."

"Agreed." Her eyes went down the dark lanes and came back to him. "Nine teams entered for Linda's cup alone, and wrote her name on the form to say so. I will call all nine in the morning and offer their money back, and all nine will take it. That puts me a hundred and fifteen dollars under thirty thousand, with every lane of the Open full."

He offered her nothing. A hundred and fifteen dollars was a kids' birthday party on a Saturday with the fryer going, or most of one, and he had heard her swear to her daughter, the house and not the rest, and he put the pencil back in her box and his hands in his jacket pockets.

"Snack bar might find you some of it, Linda's night."

"In fries."

He laughed out loud, once, and it went down the dark lanes.

He unfolded the page and turned it round to face her. It was one sheet on Arlene's lawyer's letterhead, and it let the three of them go from the March paper once the third name was on it, with Arlene's signature at the top, and his under hers, and a line left at the bottom with nothing on it.

"I'm asking you to sign it."

She put on the reading glasses that hung from the black cord and read it once, top to bottom, and took them off again.

"You have asked an eighty-four-year-old woman to bet her bed on a hundred and fifteen dollars and a promise I made my daughter."

"I know what I asked. I asked it at her table, with her ham on my plate."

"You could keep it in your jacket until I have made the number, and tear it up then. Arlene would have her bed whatever I made."

"I could. I'd be fixing your machines for one of us all month, and I couldn't tell you which."

She looked at the empty line a while. "If I miss, Arlene loses her place, and it will be my name that put it there." She took a pen out of her vest. "If I ask you to stand beside me before this is done, I won't have you standing at the bottom of my drive as well."

The cap came off the pen, and she bent over the counter and put the point on the line, and he watched her hand do it. It went slow, slower than she wrote anything at her counter, one letter and then the next, every letter in her printing standing separate from the one before it, and the pen going over the paper was the one sound in the whole dark building, a small dry scratch he could have heard from the lot. She lifted the pen between the two names and set it down again. Her other hand held the page by its corner so it wouldn't creep, and the knuckles of it were still chapped from the cold, and she finished the last letter and lifted the pen and capped it. Arlene's name was at the top of the page, and his under it, and hers under his.

"I'll call Concord in the morning and let it go. They'd want ninety days to write it again."

"There are thirteen days."

He had not got as far as counting them, and she had.

"I called Laconia this morning. They're bending you a K."

She looked at him over the top of the signed page. "Thank you."

His key came out of her vest pocket, the one he had worked off his ring at this counter, and she held it across to him on her open palm. He looked at it.

"Not yet."
