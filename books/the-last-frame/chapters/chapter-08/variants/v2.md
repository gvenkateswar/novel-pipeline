# Chapter 8: Position Relief

Three's sweep motor hung half out of its bracket, and Ray had her brushes out and was working the second holder loose with the tip of a screwdriver while Hollis held the flashlight. The league clipboard was under Hollis's other arm. Hollis had phoned the store at five that Thursday and said, "Three's dropping late. She hasn't called anybody," and hung up, and at six he had opened the back door from the inside and stood out of the way. So here Ray was, two nights after he'd quit her league at a snack-bar table, still unable to leave one of her machines down on a league night, and he meant to be out that same back door before first ball with nobody the wiser but Hollis, who didn't count.

Down past the pin deck and sixty feet of empty maple, a woman's voice came to him along the lane from the counter phone. It carried as it always carried, without being raised.

"The Linda Gagnon Memorial Open. Saturday the twenty-seventh."

She said it as plainly as she'd give a man at the cubbies his lane.

"A team from Lewiston. Spell it for me?"

He had heard the name a good many ways in six years. He'd heard it low at the store, from people who meant well and quit halfway through it, and off the card in the trophy case every Tuesday and Thursday, and in his own kitchen after supper, from nobody. He had never heard it said whole, first name and last and Memorial after it, to a stranger on a telephone, and then again a few minutes later to somebody else, and again. Linda used to drive to Lewiston for opens with her ball on the seat beside her and come home past midnight smelling of somebody else's alley. His hands stopped on the bracket. After a while they went on.

"Mr. Arsenault," Ellen said, and waited while a man in Halsey Falls asked his question. "Yes. Her name is on it."

Ray could have said Gil's half for him. Gil had said it at every open since: "Linda beat me in the 'ninety-four open, and I'd like a second look."

"Then you're the fourteenth team. I'll put you in the morning."

She dialed again.

"Judy, the indoor day comes to Saturday with the Open, and the club's fee with it. It uses the snack bar and the settees, the weigh-in board and the raffle. It doesn't take a lane. Sunday stays empty."

Hollis, still holding the light, said, "She's had my yes since Tuesday."

Then he took the light off the motor and put it on Ray's face.

"You going to tell her what you said was wrong, or fix another one?"

Ray squinted into it. "Hold the light where I'm working, Hollis."

"I am."

The light stayed where it was. Ray found the bracket with his fingers and seated the motor by feel, in the dark below the beam, and ran the bolts down by feel, and she took them.

He wrote out the invoice on the bench, on the pad he kept in his bag for her: the hours, the Worcester man's rate, the part, a total and a line ruled under it, in her format and his square printing. Under the line he printed: Light held one hour by H. Pike. No charge.

He came up the walkway from the steel door by lane ten with the bag in one hand and the paper in the other. She was still on with Judy. She saw him coming the length of the house, and her eyes didn't leave him. He laid it on her counter. She read it with the phone against her shoulder.

"You've put Hollis on my bill."

"No charge. He's worth about that."

He was already turning for the vestibule. He had the inner door open when she said into the phone behind him, "Judy, I'll call you back."

\* \* \*

Ray rapped three times on the front door, and the glass rattled in its frame, as it had on the first night with the snow coming down on his shoulders. Kettle Lanes was shut Mondays and always had been. On this one, the eighth, he had turned the burner off under the pot at five, which made him late for his quarter past five, and so far he had billed her for none of those suppers.

Through the inner door's glass the light over the counter was on, and she was standing under it ruling squad sheets for the Open with a steel rule and a pen. She heard him. She kept her eyes on the sheet until she had taken the pen the length of the rule and lifted it off at the end, and he stood on the granite in the last of the daylight and watched her finish the line.

Then she capped the pen and came round the counter and through the inner door, into the vestibule with its corkboard of bean-supper flyers and somebody's lost mitten, and stopped on the other side of the glass. Her reading glasses hung on their cord. She didn't unlock the door, and he didn't ask her to.

He had come with one thing to say, and he meant to say all of it, the worst first, and put no reason on the end. He said it to the glass, loud enough to carry through it.

"I said you sounded fine on the radio that night. You'd told me about that night in the dark with a pin in your hands, and I picked it because it would land, and I used it on you. And I walked out on thirty years of people so you'd watch me go. And you were telling me what it cost you to ask me, and I didn't let you finish. I'm sorry."

She heard all of it with her eyes on his. At the last two words they went down once, to the push bar on her side of the door, and came back up.

"Understood."

She let a breath go.

"I'd like to hear whether it holds."

He had put a good many things back together in this building on a Monday and watched them let go by Thursday, and a man couldn't tell which kind he had by looking at it. He had nothing to say after his sentence that wouldn't be asking her for something, so he didn't say it. The cold of the granite came up through his boot soles. Behind him the lamp over the lot had come on above the drifts.

"I'd like to hear the rest of what you were saying."

She looked at him a while through the glass. Then her hand came up, bare, and lay flat on the inside of it at the height of his chest. He took his own out of his jacket pocket and put it up on the outside, level with hers. Her fingertips came to his second knuckles, and the whole of her palm sat inside the span of his. The glass was cold under his hand and stayed cold, and on her side her breath fogged a patch low on the pane that faded and came back, and faded, and came back.

"At your counter, that was the first time I have ever asked anybody for something I wanted. Then I took it to your people. At that table I let the quiet do my asking. One sentence from me would have ended it for you, and I didn't say it. I knew what it was costing you."

Her voice came off its level at "I knew," and he heard it come off.

"I let it cost you."

\* \* \*

Ray laid Gagnon & Son's entry for Linda's Open on her counter with the house filling up behind him for Tuesday league: four names in his own hand, his on top, and paid. He'd filled it in at the store that morning with Danny at the register saying nothing about it. Ellen put her glasses on from the cord and read it herself, every line, top to bottom, and took the glasses off again. She left it lying where he'd put it.

"I'd like to hear it again. Out there."

Her eyes went past him to the settees.

He turned round and faced the room. It had the noise of any Tuesday twenty minutes before first ball, shoes going on and the ball returns rumbling and the Highway crew at the snack bar with their hoods still up from the lot, and the noise began to drop off a table at a time as people saw him standing at her counter with his back to her. He knew which words she had heard at her door. He wouldn't say them in here. The radio line would hand this room her two in the morning, and that night was hers, given to him in the dark with nobody else listening. So he'd have to say it with nothing to stand behind. He had not asked anybody in this building for anything since Arlene put a key in his hand in 'ninety-six, a key he hadn't asked for either, and in his whole life he had asked the store once, and only the store.

The Legion man on lane three set his ball back on the rack. At the snack bar a hood came down. On lane one, Verna's hands closed the binder.

"I was cruel to Mrs. Daly on purpose, and I walked out on you to make her watch. I'd like back in. Verna, I'm asking."

Verna let it sit. Then, from the scorer's table on lane one:

"The secretary notes Mr. Gagnon resigned in front of witnesses and has asked in front of witnesses. He may bowl. Mr. Pike keeps the clipboard through the Memorial."

The room laughed at the secretary, as it had since nineteen seventy-nine. It would be the first last Saturday in February in thirty years that Ray didn't coach, and he'd watch Linda's day go by from a settee.

When he turned back, Ellen had his paper in her hand, and he watched her put it through the slot in the box.

He went down the walkway with his bag. Hollis was at the scorer's table on six with the clipboard, his golf pencil going, and he said it low, with his eyes on the sheet.

"You missed Sunday. Quitting my league doesn't quit you out of my bob-house. I scored it a forfeit."

"Holy mackerel."

"We're level."

First time ahead in eleven winters, and it had gone on a Sunday he never showed up for. Ray took a ball off the rack between five and six, the first one his hand came to, and stepped up on six's approach with Hollis behind him holding the clipboard. He turned it loose, and it went away from him down the maple, still rolling.

\* \* \*

The front door was unlocked because she'd said it would be. Ray let himself into the dark house at ten in the morning on Presidents' Day, a Monday, with the lanes unlit, and before he had his boots wiped he heard the steel drawer of the office desk open.

She brought it out to the counter: the release, with his January signature by itself on the first line and the two lines below it blank. It had lain in that drawer five weeks and done nothing by itself. She turned it to face him and stood behind the counter with her hands at her sides.

"If you come back, you come back beside me."

He had stood behind her since the day Arlene came down to the store and asked him to sign there, and every man on the Bench could have told you to the stool where he stood. He looked at his name on the line by itself, and then at her.

"I'll sign Arlene's paper, and I'll tell her myself."

He let that go by him. She wasn't done, and he could hear she wasn't.

"In January I'd have been signing her bed away on a winter I was losing. With Linda's name on it, it's a winter I'm winning, if every lane fills."

It was his own question answered, across this same counter, a month late, and she had put the if on the end of it herself, where he would have left it off.

"I gave you her name, and the building with it. That's not enough?"

Her eyes went past his shoulder to the snack bar, where the chairs were down on the floor now, and came back to him.

"You gave it angry. I need you to choose it."

He heard the chair go back on the dry floor again, on the second of February, and his two words go down the table with nobody reaching for them, and her at the corner thanking him after. He let all of it go through him once more. Then he answered her.

"Then I'll choose it, on a Monday, with nobody in the room. Linda's name on your Open, and my name off your paper. I'd like both."

She nodded once.

"The bank in Concord has held me a loan since December, to buy this place at appraisal if you missed," he said. "I'll phone them in the morning and let it go."

Ellen stood with that a moment. Then she folded the paper along Arlene's old creases, took her coat down off the hook behind the register, put the paper in its inside pocket, and came out round the counter.

She drove behind his truck in her own car, down Kettle Road and onto Main Street on a holiday, past the bank dark behind its granite and the Massachusetts plates parked up the hill, past Gagnon & Son lit at the bottom by the dock, with Danny alone at the counter and a line out to the salt pallet for ice cleats.

Arlene was at her table when they came up, with the boxes for The Pines taped shut along the wall and her coat over the back of her chair, as though the van might come early. Ellen had phoned ahead. Arlene looked at the two of them in her doorway and offered neither of them a chair, and the radiator under the window clanked and knocked. Ellen stood at the end of the table in her coat.

"With every lane of the Open full, I clear by six hundred. One storm takes that."

Arlene took her glasses off and polished them on the front of her cardigan, and put them back on to look at Ellen through them.

"If I sign this and I miss, it's a bank sale. You wait for your money and go back on the list, the condo man gets the lot for the land, and the house money my daughter let me spend goes with it."

At the condo man Arlene's chin came up, and stayed up.

Ray said it to Ellen. "If you miss, I'm not at the bank's sale either. I said I'd not buy your mistake cheap. That's cheaper."

Ellen turned her head and looked at him, and kept looking, and said nothing.

He turned to Arlene. "That costs you, Arlene. A sale with me in it pays you more."

Arlene looked at him over the tops of her glasses for as long as the radiator took to finish knocking.

"I'll take the condo man's money before I'll take yours off her mistake, Raymond."

She had called him by his whole name since he was a boy in her front lanes, and never once to let him off anything. He nodded to her, and let it stand.

"There's money I swore to my daughter," Ellen said. "It isn't in this."

Arlene gave that one nod.

"You ought to call Kate," Ray said.

Ellen looked at the telephone on Arlene's wall by the door, and stayed where she was.

"Kate gave me the house to lose. I'm not asking her to watch me lose it a second way. I'll tell her, after."

Ray had nothing to put against that, and didn't try.

Arlene reached over and pulled the table drawer open far enough to show them the inside of it: string, rubber bands, a ledger pencil.

"I'll sign it, dear, and keep it in my drawer till the count."

It was a fair offer and a safe one, and in January he'd have taken it for her and called it kindness.

"Then he's behind me till March with his hand on your drawer," Ellen said. "Today, or not at all."

Arlene pushed the drawer shut with two fingers and watched it go all the way.

"I said I'd sign when you did, dear. I didn't say I'd like it."

One corner of Ellen's mouth moved, and she put it back where it belonged.

Ellen took the paper out of her coat and opened it on the oilcloth and signed it standing, with her own pen, on the second line, her name square and plain under his. She turned it round and held out the pen. Arlene took it and signed last, once, sitting, the pen going slow across the third line, and capped it and handed it back to Ellen. Ellen folded the paper along its creases and gave it to Ray, and he put it inside his jacket. The radiator knocked under the window, and nobody said a word over it.

Ray went down first. Arlene's stair was steep and narrow under its one bulb, and with the pharmacy shut for the holiday the cold came up the treads to meet him. He was all the way out of her paper now. At six in the morning he would lay it beside Danny's envelope on the store counter, and go put the coffee on, and let his son read it with nobody watching. At eight this morning, from the store phone, he had ordered the K from the shop in Laconia at the quote he'd given her, and had them date their bill the first of March, so it would come off nothing she was counting. He hadn't told her that.

She came down behind him a tread at a time. She didn't say his name. He turned anyway, one stair below her, and for once her eyes were level with his. She had his gloves on against the cold of the stair, and they were big on her.

She took his key out of her vest pocket, the plain front key he had laid on her counter, and held it out to him on the palm of his glove.

"Not yet."

He went down the rest of the stair, and his knee clicked twice on the last tread. At the side door he lifted the latch and opened it on the cold of Main Street, and looked back up. She was still holding it out.
