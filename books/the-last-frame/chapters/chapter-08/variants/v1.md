# Chapter 8: Position Relief

Gil Arsenault held the house ball out on the palm of a bowling glove he had no need for, in his good wool overcoat.

"House rules, Ray. Three strings, total pins, my house balls. You'll forgive the house balls; they're older than you."

Ray took it. It sat lighter in his hand than his own. Pop music came down out of the ceiling, the screens overhead ran cartoons for nobody, and the carpet had planets on it. The building was warm into every corner, and Ray resented every degree of it and stood in it anyway.

"You called me for a match, and I like a match, and half your Tuesday division drove over to watch it and they're in my lounge eating pizza, so I've had my night whoever bowls," Gil said.

He gave the terms short, for the settee. If Ray took it, the Highway Department and the Legion went home to Kettle Lanes on Tuesday at the season rate and bowled Linda's Memorial. If Gil's man took it, Ray paid their lineage here to the end of the season, and Gagnon & Son bowled for Pine Ridge.

Dale Hutchins sat on the settee with his coat zipped to the chin and his bag between his boots.

"I want it settled before Linda's night. Last time I took your word about her, the book was wrong. And it still says you paid a bank to buy her out if she's short."

He looked at the stake Gil had just read out, and worked it to the end.

"Everything you put up, losing pays you."

"Then watch me bowl."

"And Linda's cup? My crew paid for it in September."

"You won't pay for it twice, Dale. I'll ask her to give the league its Memorial back."

Dale looked at him a while.

"Bowl, then."

Denise was already at the scorer's table with a Kettle Lanes paper sheet and a pencil out of Ellen's cigar box, which Ellen kept sorted points up so a scorer never had to reach twice. Nobody said a word about Tuesday. Gil looked from the sheet up to his screens.

"I'll take Mrs. Lavoie's pencil over my screens. I watched her pick up a four-horse with her third ball eleven years back, in the spring doubles. I still think about it."

Ray had seen her make that leave twice in thirty years. Her pencil stopped over the sheet a second and went on. He took out his pocketknife and held out his hand, and she put the pencil in it, and he shaved it to a point and gave it back blunt end first.

He started badly. The house ball rode wrong off his fingers, high and light, and took three off the right side.

"Holy mackerel."

One of the Highway crew laughed and quit. Gil's anchor was a lineman half Ray's age, and he bowled the first string clean and quick and never watched his own pins fall. He bowled the second the same, while Ray chased him two and three pins at a time.

Before the third string the man from the Bench's end stool leaned on the rail in his coat. "Had a dollar on your man here, Ray. Nothing personal. Man that's buying the place come March don't bowl hard to fill it."

Denise said it to her sheet, loud enough to carry to the lounge. "He's a hundred-and-four average with a hundred-and-forty heart. Don't tell him I said either."

The lineman finished on a spare and sat down. Ray was nine pins behind, with the tenth box to bowl and ten on the deck, and he needed every one of them.

His first ball took the front of the rack, and one pin went over sideways and lay across the deck in front of the three still standing at the back, like a plank laid across a ditch. The second went too hard, and he knew it as it left him, and it took one of the three and left two at the back with the plank still lying in front of them. The ball came back up the return and he picked it up, small and heavier than it looked and worn smooth by other men's palms, and stood at the line with it and looked down sixty feet of somebody else's maple to the near end of the lying pin, where its head touched the deck, and set his eyes there and kept them there and let the ball go easy, so easy somebody on the rail groaned, and it rolled the length of Gil's lane at a speed a child could have walked beside, and touched the near end of the pin, and the pin came up off the deck end over end into the two at the back, and all three went over together with a sound like a cupboard coming down. Denise's pencil was moving before the last one lay still.

Gil keyed his wireless mic, and two words came out of the ceiling over the music. "Kettle Lanes."

Dale got up off the settee. "Tuesday, then." He picked up his bag.

The lot was cold enough to ache in his teeth. The man from the end stool was at Ray's truck ahead of him, and he put a folded dollar into Hollis's glove without a word. Hollis put it in his waterproof notebook.

"She wrote the league a letter," Hollis said. "Verna read it out to the Thursday division, every word, and copied it in the book, and gave me the page for the committee. You read it yet?"

"No."

Hollis waited, and asked it again in exactly the same voice. "You read it yet?"

Ray held out his hand.

Hollis took it out from inside his coat and gave it over. It was a yellow sheet off her legal pad with the gum still on its top edge. At the head of it, underlined once, was the date he had watched her write while he worked her key off his ring at her counter: the third of February. Her printing went down the page every letter set apart from the next, so nobody tired could mistake one for another. He read the end of it twice.

*The silence was mine, and so was the name. Mr. Gagnon took the fault for it on himself in this room, and I ask the secretary to strike that from the minutes.*

He folded it in three and put it inside his jacket, against the envelope with nothing written on it.

"You anchor Tuesday."

"Where'll you be?"

\* \* \*

The bolt on the front door had never once been turned before opening in all Ray's years behind the counter, and he turned it, because she was standing outside the door glass in the dark. Her hands were bare and red at the knuckle.

The store was dark down every aisle but the one light over the counter, and it smelled of kerosene and cut brass and the Bench's pot, which he had put on before he unlocked anything. He went back to the drawer Danny had already counted, and Ellen came to the counter and stood at it with her weight on both feet.

He had got as far as the fives when she started, and he stopped and left them lying.

"Dale's crew and the Legion came in my door last night. Dale paid me in cash and asked for a receipt. He asked me where you were, and I told him I did not know."

His gloves lay beside the drawer where he had pulled them off to count. His hand went to them while she talked and squared them, cuff to cuff, and stayed there. She had taken nothing from him since the hood that wasn't on an invoice, nothing from the man the March paper said would buy her building, and he kept the gloves on his side of the counter.

"I came here after I knew it."

He didn't answer her. Inside his jacket the yellow page lay folded in three against the envelope with no name on it, and when he breathed he could feel the edge of it through his shirt, the page with the date at its head that he had watched her underline while his thumbnail worked at the ring. Her hands lay on his counter one over the other, red across the backs and white at the tips where the cold had got in first, an inch from his gloves. He had his answer in his pocket, and he kept his hand where it was.

The Bench came in out of the dark at opening, the three of them in a row, stamping snow onto the mat, and stopped when they saw who it was. One got as far as "And the—" and shut his mouth.

Ellen kept her eyes on Ray. "You have no reason to believe I would have come anyway, and I have no way to give you one."

Nobody said anything. The Bench stood inside the door in their coats with nothing to call.

Then the man from the end stool, who had paid Hollis a dollar in the Pine Ridge lot, went past her to the pot with his coat still on, poured a cup, black, and held it out to her across the end of the counter.

"Coffee's on."

Her hand went out for it.

\* \* \*

Arlene set the plate down in front of him before he had his coat off: ham, a boiled potato split open, a spoon of her mustard on the rim. She didn't ask was he hungry. The ham was salt and sweet at once, and the mustard went straight up the back of his nose, and he ate with his coat still hanging off one shoulder.

Wintergreen and cough drops came up through the floorboards from the pharmacy, under the smell of the ham. Boxes lettered for The Pines in her ledger hand stood along the wall. The scanner talked low to itself on the counter, and the window over her table looked down Main Street to the dock, to Gagnon & Son at the bottom of it.

"Why'd you sell it to her, Arlene?"

"You'd have kept it a museum, Raymond. I wanted it to live."

He ate, and didn't argue it.

The scanner spoke up: a car off the road by the town beach, nobody hurt, a wrecker on the way. Arlene didn't turn her head.

"That'll be the Pelletier girl. She takes that corner like it owes her money."

Ray finished the potato. "I want off the March paper. I'll have your lawyer draw whatever it takes."

"Do you know what you're asking me?"

"Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain, because I'd rather hear it from you than work it out by myself in the truck."

She got up and went to a box with no lid on it yet and came back with her copy of the contract, and laid it on the oilcloth beside his plate. Three pages stapled at the corner, and on the last of them Ellen's printing, small and level as if she had ruled the line herself before she trusted it, and his own scrawl under it running downhill.

"If she misses and you aren't standing there, nobody buys it. The Pines gives my room to the next name, and I go on the bottom of that list at eighty-four, and I may not live to the top of it. I'm paid months on, when my lawyer's done selling it out from under her, and that condo man gets the lot for what the dirt is worth."

"I know it."

"Then hold it. You keep it in your drawer, she makes her number, you tear it up after, and I've had my bed the whole time."

Ray put his fork down square beside the plate. The cupboard door over her sink had hung a quarter inch open since the winter its top hinge pulled loose, and he looked at the gap while he said it.

"Every clutch I put in over there might be going into my own machine come March, and she knows it, and Dale Hutchins knows it, and I'm done standing at the bottom of her drive."

Arlene turned the contract face down on the oilcloth.

"Tuesday night your truck sat outside your own store from supper till the league let out, with the engine running. I watched it from that window. Thirty years I've known where you were on a Tuesday night. And the next morning her car was at your door before your light was. That paper's mine. I made the pair of you sign it so I'd be sure of my bed, and I've been sure of it all winter, and the two of you have been sitting in cars."

He didn't answer it. He looked past her down Main to his own door at the bottom of the hill, and the stretch of curb in front of it, plowed to the pavement, with nobody parked there.

"In nineteen seventy-one I held Normand to a building I wanted and he didn't. He gave it eighteen years and never once said so. I won't hold you to one you've stopped wanting, Raymond."

"Then you'll sign it."

"When she has. It's hers to risk as much as mine."

He had come up her stair for a paper he could carry around finished, and she was sending him back down it to ask.

Arlene got up and took the phone off the wall by the bread box and dialed her lawyer from memory.

"It's Arlene Fortier. Put him on." She waited, looking out her window, until he came on. "Draw it so I sign first, and Raymond under me, and leave a line for Ellen Daly. He'll carry it to her himself, and he'll ask her."

\* \* \*

The door glass rattled in its frame under his second rap, and the door came open before he could give it a third. She had been standing on the other side of it.

His hand was still raised from knocking. Hers came out of the light and closed around his wrist above the cuff of his jacket, on the skin, warm from her building, four fingers and a thumb he could feel one at a time, and she brought him in off the step and out of the wind that came across the lot off the lake, one step and then a second, until the door had swung shut on the cold behind him. She held on a second past that. He stood in her vestibule with his arm still half raised and her hand around his wrist, and neither of them said a word of welcome, and then she let go.

The vestibule smelled of wet wool and road salt off the Tuesday division's boots, three hours of them in and out past the bean-supper flyers, from a league night he hadn't been in. The lanes were dark, and one light burned over the counter. Crossing to it, his eyes went to the padlock on locker fourteen and came back.

Ellen went around behind the counter, and he stayed on his side of it and took a single folded page out of his jacket and laid it on the wood between them, unopened.

"That's what I came for. I'll ask you for it last."

She stayed on her feet, square to him across the wood, and waited for whatever came first.

"What I said about the radio. You told me that night in the pit with a pin in your hands, and I kept it, and when I wanted something that would land I went and got it."

"It did. I have heard it every time I have keyed that microphone since."

He stood in her building and let it land.

"I'm sorry for it, Ellen."

She let it stand, and nobody said it was all right.

"Hollis gave me what you wrote. I watched you put the date on it."

Her hand went to the pocket of her vest and stopped on the outside of it, over something small, and stayed there.

"I told Dale I'd ask you to give the league its Memorial back. I'm asking."

He gave her the terms in three lines, one at a time, and let each of them go before the next.

"Linda's name comes off the Open."

"The league bowls its Memorial at two on the last Saturday, all ten lanes."

"Your Open gets the morning and the evening, and a cup of its own, with whatever name you like on it."

Then, because it was the one thing he had brought that wasn't his: "Hollis says the committee voted on an Open. Nobody voted on a name."

He watched her do the sum behind her eyes, the whole of it, down to the dollar, before she said a word.

"Agreed."

"Nine teams entered to bowl for Linda's cup. I will call all nine in the morning and offer their money back, and all nine will take it. That puts me a hundred and fifteen dollars under thirty thousand, with every lane of the Open full."

He offered her nothing for it. "Snack bar might find that, Linda's night."

"The snack bar sells fries in paper boats."

He unfolded the page and turned it round on the wood to face her. Arlene's lawyer had got it all onto one page: the three of them let go of the March paper, Arlene's name signed first in her old ledger hand, his under it, and a line at the bottom with nothing on it yet.

"I'm asking you to sign it."

She put on the reading glasses that hung on their black cord and read it once, top to bottom.

"You have asked an eighty-four-year-old woman to bet her bed on a hundred and fifteen dollars."

"I know what I asked."

"You could keep it in your jacket until I have made the number, and tear it up then. Arlene would have her bed whatever I made."

"I could. I'd be fixing your machines for one of us all month, and I couldn't tell you which."

She looked down at the page with Arlene's name on it, and then up at him over the glasses. "If I miss, Arlene loses her place, and it will be my name that put it there."

Her hand went into her vest for a pen. "If I ask you to stand beside me before this is done, I won't have you standing at the bottom of my drive as well."

Ray watched her hand bring the pen out and uncap it with the thumb and stand the cap on the counter. It squared the page to the edge of the wood with its side, and set the point at the start of the empty line, and held it there over the paper while the boiler came on down cellar and the floor took it up, and then it began. There was no other sound in the building. The pen made a small dry scratch and lifted, and scratched, and lifted, her printing going onto the line with every letter standing separate, the point coming clear of the paper between each one and coming down again exactly where she meant it to, steady as a good sweep setting down on its stop, the red gone out of her fingers now and the nails cut short and square, and he watched every letter of it, her first name and then the last, until the pen came up off the end of the line and stayed up. She capped it.

"I'll call Concord in the morning and let it go. They'd want ninety days to write it again."

She had them counted before he was done. "There are thirteen days."

"I called Laconia this morning. They're bending you a K."

She looked at him for some time. "Thank you."

Then her hand went into the vest pocket it had stopped on before, and brought out his key, the one he had worked off his ring at this counter, and held it across to him on her open palm. His own hand lay on the wood an inch from hers. The brass had gone warm in her pocket, and there was no tag on it, and her hand stayed where it was. He looked at it.

"Not yet."
