# Chapter 8: Position Relief

Three's sweep motor hung half out of its bracket with the brush caps off, and Ray worked the first brush loose with the tip of a screwdriver and found what he'd expected to find, a carbon worn to a stub and scored across its face. Hollis held the flashlight. The league clipboard rode under his other arm, pinned against his coat, and he hadn't said a word about it, and neither had Ray. Hollis had phoned the store at five, on a Thursday in February with first ball an hour and a half off, and said, "Three's dropping late. She hasn't called anybody," and hung up before Ray could remind him he'd quit, and at twenty past he'd opened the back door from inside, and Ray had left the truck round by the dumpster, where nobody would read anything into it. So here he was at five past six, two nights out of the league in front of witnesses, on his knees behind lane three, because he did not leave a machine down on league night, whoever owned it, and quitting hadn't changed that, though he'd had some hopes.

The house was lit end to end and nobody was bowling yet, and through the gap under the masking the lanes ran away bright and backward toward the counter. Down sixty feet of empty lane came a woman's voice, not raised, carrying the way it carried.

"The Linda Gagnon Memorial Open, Saturday the twenty-seventh."

She said it into the counter phone as plainly as she'd tell somebody lane four was open. Then, after a while: "A team from Lewiston. Spell it for me?"

He'd been on his knees here the better part of an hour, and she had said it four times, five with this one, and he had not meant to keep count. For six years nobody in Ambrose had said the whole of it. The town said the Memorial, or Linda's day, and the cup in the case said the rest on a brass plate where nobody had to read it out loud, and that had suited everybody, him first. Now it came down the lane every few minutes in Ellen's counter voice, the first name and the last and the rest behind them, to people in Lewiston who would want to know where to park. Linda had driven to Lewiston for opens, two hours each way with her ball on the seat beside her, and come home with the cup or the name of whoever'd beaten her. His hands stopped on the bracket. Then they went on, and he got the new carbon in and its spring behind it.

"Mr. Arsenault," Ellen said, from the far end of everything.

There was a pause about as long as it took a man in a good wool overcoat to come round to his point.

"Yes. Her name is on it."

Ray could have done Gil's half for him. Gil had said it at every open since, to anybody holding a pencil: "Linda beat me in the 'ninety-four open, and I'd like a second look."

"Then you're the fourteenth team," Ellen said. "I'll put you in the morning."

The receiver went down and came straight back up. "Judy, the indoor day comes to Saturday with the Open, and the club's fee with it. It uses the snack bar and the settees, the weigh-in board and the raffle. It doesn't take a lane. Sunday stays empty."

"She's had my yes since Tuesday," Hollis said. He chaired the derby, and he said it holding the light steady, in the voice he used for ice.

Then he took the light off the motor and put it on Ray's face.

"You going to tell her what you said was wrong, or fix another one?"

Ray squinted into it. "Hold the light where I'm working, Hollis."

"I am."

The light stayed where it was. Ray put the motor back up into its bracket in the dark, by feel, and found the bolt holes with his fingertips and ran the bolts in and snugged them down, and when he cycled her, three's sweep came down on time, grudging about it, and set ten clean.

On the bench by the steel door he wrote out the invoice on the pad from his jacket, in his square printing and in her format: the hours, the Worcester man's rate, the part, a total with a line ruled under it, and at the bottom, "Light held two hours by H. Pike. No charge."

He came up the walkway from the steel door by lane ten with the page folded once. The Thursday people were coming in out of the cold with their bags, and Ellen was at the counter with the receiver at her ear, and she saw him coming the length of the house and kept talking. He laid the page on her counter. She read it with the phone against her shoulder.

"You've put Hollis on my bill."

"No charge. He's worth about that."

He was already turning for the vestibule, and he had the inner door open when she said it into the phone behind him.

"Judy, I'll call you back."

\* \* \*

He knocked three times on the door glass, and it rattled in its frame, and the sound went into the dark vestibule and stopped. Kettle Lanes was shut Mondays. The sun had gone off the lake behind him a few minutes since, and it was a quarter past five on the eighth of February, and up on School Street the pot sat on a cold burner with nothing in it, because at five o'clock he had turned the burner off and walked out of his own kitchen like a man with somewhere to be. That made him late for supper. All winter he had not once billed her for a supper, and he didn't mean to start tonight, but he felt somebody ought to know.

The closer on this door had been slamming since about Christmas, and he had a screwdriver in his jacket, and he left it alone.

Through the outer glass and the inner glass behind it, the counter light was on. She was at the counter with a ruler and a pen, ruling squad sheets for the Open, a line at a time across the page. Her head came up at the knock. Then it went back down, and she finished the line she was on, edge to edge, and laid the ruler across the sheet to hold her place. He watched her finish it.

She came through the inner door and across the vestibule and stopped at the glass, in her vest, with the light behind her. With it behind her he couldn't read her face, only the shape of her, square on both feet. She did not reach for the lock, and he did not look at it.

He had put it in order in the truck, and he said it in that order.

"I said you sounded fine on the radio that night. You'd told me about that night in the dark with a pin in your hands, and I picked it because it would land, and I used it on you. And I walked out on thirty years of people so you'd watch me go. And you were telling me what it cost you to ask me, and I didn't let you finish. I'm sorry."

His breath went up white and thinned on the glass. He had nothing to put after the last word, and he didn't go looking for anything.

"Understood." It came through the glass small and exact. She took a breath, and he saw the vest rise with it. "I'd like to hear whether it holds."

"I'd like to hear the rest of what you were saying."

Her hand came up and lay flat on the inside of the glass, at about the height of his heart. He put his own up on the outside, level with it. Her fingertips came to his second knuckles, and the rest of her hand sat inside the span of his with room to spare. The glass was cold under his palm and stayed cold, however long he left it there, and on her side her breath made a gray patch low on the pane that went while he watched it, and came back.

"At your counter, that was the first time I have ever asked anybody for something I wanted." She kept her eyes on him to say it. "Then I took it to your people. At that table I let the quiet do my asking. One sentence from me would have ended it for you, and I didn't say it. I knew what it was costing you."

Her voice came off its level on the word knew, and he heard it come off.

"I let it cost you."

\* \* \*

Ray laid the entry on her counter: Gagnon & Son, for the Linda Gagnon Memorial Open, four names in his own hand and the money paid, with first ball ten minutes off on a Tuesday and the Highway crew already down on their settee with their hoods up. Ellen put her glasses on from the cord and read it herself, every line of it, the team and the four names and the word paid, and took the glasses off and let them drop, and left the paper lying where he'd put it.

"I'd like to hear it again," she said. "Out there."

Her eyes went past him to the settees.

He turned round and faced the house. It was lit end to end, and the Tuesday people were in it doing what they did before first ball, lacing shoes and arguing handicaps and buying fries they'd be sorry about, and the noise of it was the noise of every Tuesday he'd had for thirty years. The words he'd said to her in the dark were about a night she had told him by flashlight with a pin in her hands, and that night was hers. If he said them out here, this room would have her two in the morning by the weekend, and the Bench would have it with their coffee by Monday, and every one of them would know where to aim if they ever had a mind to. He had done that to her once already, to her face, with nobody listening, and he was not going to do it again with forty-some on the settees. So he would have to say it with nothing to stand behind, no reason out in front of it and no machine to point at. He had not asked anybody in this building for anything since 'ninety-six, when Arlene put a key in his hand before he could ask. He had asked the store once in his life, and only the store.

The noise went off the room a table at a time while he stood there not starting. At the Highway crew's settee a hood came down. On lane three the Legion man set his ball back on the rack and kept his hand on it. At the scorer's table on lane one, Verna closed her binder.

"I was cruel to Mrs. Daly on purpose, and I walked out on you to make her watch. I'd like back in. Verna, I'm asking."

Verna answered from lane one.

"The secretary notes Mr. Gagnon resigned in front of witnesses and has asked in front of witnesses. He may bowl. Mr. Pike keeps the clipboard through the Memorial."

Somebody on the Legion settee laughed at the secretary, and then two or three more did, and the noise came back up off the tables. So the last Saturday in February would be the first since 'ninety-six he didn't coach, and Linda's day would go by him from a settee between his boxes, which was where he'd spent thirty years telling other men to sit still and pay attention.

When he looked back at the counter, Ellen had picked the paper up and was putting it in the box.

He went down the walkway with his bag. Hollis was at six's scorer's table with the clipboard in front of him and the golf pencil going, and he said it low, with his eyes on the sheet.

"You missed Sunday. Quitting my league doesn't quit you out of my bob-house. I scored it a forfeit."

First time ahead in eleven winters, and it had gone on a Sunday Ray hadn't shown up for.

"Holy mackerel," Ray said.

"We're level."

Ray took a ball off the rack between five and six, the first one under his hand, and stepped up onto six's approach with Hollis and the clipboard behind him, and turned it loose, and it went away from him down the maple, still going.

\* \* \*

The steel drawer of the office desk ran out on its rollers and banged at the end of its track, and Ellen came out to the counter with the paper and laid it down facing him. It was the release, his January signature alone on the first line and the two lines under it blank. The building was shut for Presidents' Day, nobody on the floor at ten in the morning, and the front door had been unlocked because she'd said it would be. The paper had sat in that drawer since January and done nothing by itself, which was about what he'd have expected of paper.

"If you come back, you come back beside me. I'll sign Arlene's paper, and I'll tell her myself."

He looked at the two blank lines, and she let him look.

"In January I'd have been signing her bed away on a winter I was losing. With Linda's name on it, it's a winter I'm winning, if every lane fills."

"I gave you her name, and the building with it. That's not enough?"

"You gave it angry. I need you to choose it."

He had given it on his feet, with the chair going back on the dry floor behind him on the second of February and every face at that table turned to him.

"Then I'll choose it, on a Monday, with nobody in the room. Linda's name on your Open, and my name off your paper. I'd like both."

There was one more thing, and it was his to say. "The bank in Concord has held me a loan since December, to buy this place at appraisal if you missed. I'll phone them in the morning and let it go."

Ellen picked the paper up and folded it once along the crease it already had, and went to the hook for her coat.

She drove down behind his truck in her own car, down Main Street on a holiday with the bank dark and Massachusetts plates parked all up the hill, past the store lit up with Danny alone at the counter and a line for ice cleats back to the salt, and parked behind him at the pharmacy's dark windows.

Arlene was at her table with her coat over the back of her chair, and the boxes along the wall were taped shut now, every one, and under the window the radiator was banging away like it had heard the news and had views on it. Ellen had phoned ahead. She didn't sit. She laid the paper on the table by Arlene's cup and stood over it, and Ray took the chair across from Arlene, because somebody ought to and Arlene had looked at it.

"With every lane of the Open full, I clear by six hundred. One storm takes that."

Arlene looked up at her and waited, and Ellen gave her the rest.

"If I sign this and I miss, it's a bank sale. You wait for your money and go back on the list, the condo man gets the lot for the land, and the house money my daughter let me spend goes with it."

Ray said it to Ellen first. "If you miss, I'm not at the bank's sale either. I said I'd not buy your mistake cheap. That's cheaper."

Then, across the table: "That costs you, Arlene. A sale with me in it pays you more."

Arlene set her cup down in its ring. "I'll take the condo man's money before I'll take yours off her mistake, Raymond."

"There's money I swore to my daughter," Ellen said. "It isn't in this."

"You ought to call Kate," Ray said.

"Kate gave me the house to lose. I'm not asking her to watch me lose it a second way. I'll tell her, after."

Arlene drew the paper toward her with one finger. "I'll sign it, dear, and keep it in my drawer till the count."

"Then he's behind me till March with his hand on your drawer. Today, or not at all."

The radiator banged once more and let it go.

"I said I'd sign when you did, dear. I didn't say I'd like it."

Ellen took her own pen out of her vest and signed standing, bent over the table, in the same plain hand she put on everything. Arlene signed last, once, sitting, and capped the pen and handed it back to Ellen. Ellen folded the paper in three and passed it across the table to Ray.

Ray went down first. Arlene's side stair was steep and narrow and lit by one bulb at the top, and with the pharmacy shut for the holiday no heat came up it at all, only the cold off the dark shop below, settling on the treads. He went down with a hand on the rail and the paper in his inside pocket.

He was all the way out of her paper now. At six tomorrow he would lay it on the store counter beside Danny's envelope, before the boy had his coat off, and let the two of them sit there together and see what Danny made of them. And at eight this morning, from the store phone, with Danny ringing up cleats at the other register and pretending not to listen, he had called the shop in Laconia and ordered the K on Kettle Lanes' account, at the quote he'd brought her before Christmas, and asked them to date the bill the first of March, so it would come off nothing she was counting. He hadn't told her. He had stood at her counter at ten meaning to, and then she'd said beside me, and it had seemed a poor time to bring up one letter of a sign.

"Ray."

He turned on the step below her, and with the one step between them her eyes were level with his, for once, and he stood a second and let them be. She was wearing his gloves. They were big on her still, and gone shiny at the fingertips from a month of her hands in them. She took his key out of her vest pocket and held it out to him on the palm of his own glove.

"Not yet," he said.

He went on down, and his knee clicked twice on the last of the treads. At the side door he got the latch up and looked back. She was still standing under the bulb holding it out to him, his key on the palm of his glove, and behind him the door came open on the cold.
