# Chapter 8: Position Relief

Gil Arsenault held out the house ball in a bowling glove he had no use for, under the cuff of a good wool overcoat he hadn't taken off.

"House rules, Ray. Three strings, total pins, my house balls. You'll forgive the house balls; they're older than you."

Ray took it. It sat lighter in his hand than his own.

Pine Ridge was sixteen lanes under scoring screens, warm to the back wall on an oil bill Gil could plainly pay, and Ray enjoyed it and held that against himself.

"You called me for a match, and I like a match, and half your Tuesday division drove over to watch it and they're in my lounge eating pizza, so I've had my night whoever bowls." Gil held up two gloved fingers. "You take it, the Highway Department and the Legion go home to Kettle Lanes Tuesday at the season rate, and they bowl Linda's Memorial. My man takes it, you pay their lineage here to the end of the season, and Gagnon and Son bowls for me."

Gil's man was a lineman half Ray's age who threw every practice ball as hard as the one before it, and a man who threw like that had nothing easy in his arm for a tenth box. Ray could take him. Gil had known it when he said yes.

Dale Hutchins sat on the settee with his coat zipped and his bag between his boots, and the Highway crew and the Legion stood along the rail behind him.

"I want it settled before Linda's night," Dale said. "Last time I took your word about her, the book was wrong. And it still says you paid a bank to buy her out if she's short."

Dale read the stake twice off the back of Gil's scorecard.

"Everything you put up, losing pays you."

It did. Lose, and her two teams stayed gone, and every dollar they took out of her winter moved her building a dollar nearer to him.

"Then watch me bowl."

"And Linda's cup? My crew paid for it in September."

"You won't pay for it twice, Dale. I'll ask her to give the league its Memorial back."

At the end of the settee Hollis wrote something in his notebook. Ray had not asked Ellen for one thing to her face all winter.

Dale looked at him a while. "Bowl, then."

Denise was at the scorer's table with a Kettle Lanes paper sheet and a pencil out of Ellen's cigar box, and nobody said a word about Tuesday.

"I'll take Mrs. Lavoie's pencil over my screens," Gil said. "I watched her pick up a four-horse with her third ball eleven years back, in the spring doubles. I still think about it."

Ray had seen her make that leave twice in thirty years. Her pencil stopped a second over the sheet and went on, and Ray opened his pocketknife and held out his hand for it, and shaved it to a point and gave it back blunt end first.

The house ball rode left off his hand, and his first ball took three.

"Holy mackerel."

One of the Highway crew laughed and stopped. The lineman bowled two strings clean and quick and sat down between boxes with a paper cup.

The man from the Bench's end stool leaned over the rail in his coat before the third string.

"Had a dollar on your man here, Ray. Nothing personal. Man that's buying the place come March don't bowl hard to fill it."

Hollis had taken the other side of that dollar in the lot.

"He's a hundred-and-four average with a hundred-and-forty heart," Denise said, loud enough for the rail, to nobody. "Don't tell him I said either."

The lineman finished on a spare, and Denise ran her pencil along both sheets, and Ray picked up the house ball nine pins behind with ten on the deck, needing every pin there was. He threw the first one where he meant to. The front of the rack went, and one pin spun and lay down across the deck in front of the three still standing at the back, like a plank somebody had laid over a ditch. He threw the second too hard and knew it in his hand, and it took one of the three and left two at the back with the plank across in front of them. The ball came back up the return. It was small and heavier than it looked, worn smooth in a band by other men's palms, and ahead of him lay sixty feet of somebody else's maple. He set his eyes on the near end of the lying pin and let the ball go easy, and it rolled a long time. The lying pin came up off the deck end over end into the two at the back, and all three went over together with a sound like a cupboard coming down. Then there was nothing on the deck.

Denise's pencil was moving before the last pin lay down. Gil keyed the mic on his lapel, and two words came out of the ceiling over the music.

"Kettle Lanes."

Nobody on the rail clapped. The end stool looked at the house ball coming back up the return as though it could tell him whether Ray had meant the first two strings.

Dale got up and looked at Ray a second longer. "Tuesday, then," he said, and picked up his bag.

The lot was cold enough to ache in the teeth. At Ray's truck the end stool put a folded dollar in Hollis's glove without a word, and Hollis put it in his waterproof notebook.

"She wrote the league a letter. Verna read it out to the Thursday division, every word, and copied it in the book, and gave me the page for the committee." Hollis let that sit. "You read it yet?"

"No," Ray said, with his hand on the door handle.

"You read it yet?"

Ray held out his hand.

It was a yellow sheet off her legal pad with the gum still along the top, and at the head of it, underlined once, was the date he had watched her write while he stood at her counter working her key off his ring: the third of February. Her printing, every letter standing separate. Halfway down it said:

*The silence was mine, and so was the name. Mr. Gagnon took the fault for it on himself in this room, and I ask the secretary to strike that from the minutes.*

He folded it in three and put it inside his jacket, against the blank envelope.

"You anchor Tuesday."

"Where'll you be?"

\* \* \*

The bolt was cold under his thumb, and Ellen had been on the other side of the door glass since before he put the counter light on, in the dark, with her hands bare and red across the backs. In forty years he had never turned that bolt before opening for anybody. He turned it.

The store smelled of kerosene and cut brass and the Bench's pot. She came to the counter and stood with her weight on both feet, and he went back to the drawer Danny had counted last night, which Ray was counting again.

He was on the fives when she started, and he stopped, and left them where they lay.

"Dale's crew and the Legion came in my door last night. Dale paid me in cash and asked for a receipt. He asked me where you were, and I told him I did not know."

His gloves lay beside the drawer where he had pulled them off to count. His hand went to them and squared them cuff to cuff, and left them on his side of the counter. Since the night at the hood she had taken nothing from him that was not on an invoice, and nothing at all from the man the March paper said would buy her building.

"I came here after I knew it."

He gave her nothing back. Inside his jacket, folded in three against the blank envelope, was her yellow page with the date at its head that he had watched her write, and she had written it before he ever called Gil, and it said in her printing what she was standing at his counter saying now. It was six inches from her, and he kept his jacket zipped over it. Her hands lay on his counter with the red going slowly out of them, the backs chapped, and his gloves lay a foot from them, squared, and the fives lay where he had stopped, and he left every one of those things where it was.

Then the door opened on the dark, because he had unbolted it, and the Bench came in out of it stamping snow onto the mat, three in a row. They stopped when they saw who was at the counter. The middle one got as far as "And the—" and shut his mouth.

She did not stop and did not turn. "You have no reason to believe I would have come anyway, and I have no way to give you one."

Nobody said anything. The Bench stood inside the door in their coats with nothing to call.

The man from the end stool, who had put a folded dollar in Hollis's glove in the Pine Ridge lot, went past her to the pot with his coat on and poured a cup, black, and held it out to her across the end of the counter.

"Coffee's on."

Her hand went out for it.

\* \* \*

Arlene put the plate down in front of him before his coat was over the back of the chair: ham off the bone, a boiled potato split with the steam coming out of it, and a spoon of her mustard on the rim. She did not ask if he was hungry. The ham was sweet at the edge and salt in the middle, and for the first few forkfuls he forgot what he had come to say.

Wintergreen and cough drops came up through the floorboards from the pharmacy under the smell of the ham, and the scanner muttered low on the counter. Boxes along the wall said The Pines in her ledger hand. Her front window looked down Main Street to the dock, to Gagnon & Son at the bottom of it.

She would say yes. He had known it coming up. She had made the paper and she could unmake it, and she had owed him something since the day she sold the alley to somebody else, and they both knew which way that ran.

"Why'd you sell it to her, Arlene?"

"You'd have kept it a museum, Raymond. I wanted it to live."

He ate, and didn't argue it.

The scanner broke in with a car off the road by the town beach and the wrecker called. Arlene did not turn her head.

"That'll be the Pelletier girl. She takes that corner like it owes her money."

"I want off the March paper. I'll have your lawyer draw whatever it takes."

Arlene sat down across from him and folded her hands on the oilcloth. "Do you know what you're asking me?"

"Tell me what happens to you if she misses, Arlene, and tell it plain, because I'd rather hear it from you than work it out by myself in the truck."

She got up and went to a box with no lid on it yet and came back with her copy of the contract and laid it on the oilcloth beside his plate, three pages stapled at the corner, with Ellen's printing on the last one and his scrawl under it.

"If she misses and you aren't standing there, nobody buys it. The Pines gives my room to the next name, and I go on the bottom of that list at eighty-four, and I may not live to the top of it. I'm paid months on, when my lawyer's done selling it out from under her, and that condo man gets the lot for what the dirt is worth."

"I know it."

He had done that sum in the truck and come up anyway.

"Then hold it. You keep it in your drawer, she makes her number, you tear it up after, and I've had my bed the whole time."

He put his fork down beside the plate and squared it with the edge. Over her sink a cupboard door hung a quarter inch low off its top hinge, and he looked at the gap while he said it.

"Every clutch I put in over there might be going into my own machine come March, and she knows it, and Dale Hutchins knows it, and I'm done standing at the bottom of her drive."

Arlene turned the contract face down.

"Tuesday night your truck sat outside your own store from supper till the league let out, with the engine running. I watched it from that window. Thirty years I've known where you were on a Tuesday night. And the next morning her car was at your door before your light was. That paper's mine. I made the pair of you sign it so I'd be sure of my bed, and I've been sure of it all winter, and the two of you have been sitting in cars."

He didn't answer it. The scanner said something about the wrecker and went quiet.

"In nineteen seventy-one I held Normand to a building I wanted and he didn't. He gave it eighteen years and never once said so. I won't hold you to one you've stopped wanting, Raymond."

"Then you'll sign it."

"When she has. It's hers to risk as much as mine."

He had come up her stair for a paper he could carry home finished.

Arlene got up and took the phone off the wall by the bread box and dialed from memory. "It's Arlene Fortier," she said, and waited while they found him. "Draw it so I sign first, and Raymond under me, and leave a line for Ellen Daly. He'll carry it to her himself, and he'll ask her."

\* \* \*

The glass in the front door rattled in its frame under his first rap and his second, and before the third the door came open, because she had been on the other side of it. His hand was still up. Hers closed on his wrist and brought him in out of the wind, and held on a second past the inner door, and let go. Her fingers were warm from the building and went most of the way around his wrist.

The vestibule smelled of wet wool and road salt off the Tuesday division's boots, from a league night he had sat out in the truck. Past it the lanes were dark down to the pins, with one light over the counter, and on his way to it his eyes went to the padlock on locker fourteen and came away.

He took the folded page out of his jacket and laid it on the counter between them, and did not open it.

"That's what I came for. I'll ask you for it last."

She stayed on her feet behind the counter, which was how she took bad news, and he said it to her standing.

"What I said about the radio. You told me that night in the pit with a pin in your hands, and I kept it, and when I wanted something that would land I went and got it."

"It did. I have heard it every time I have keyed that microphone since."

The gooseneck stood at her elbow, pulled down and switched off. He looked at it, and then at her.

"I'm sorry for it, Ellen."

She let it stand there between them, and gave him nothing for it.

"Hollis gave me what you wrote. I watched you put the date on it."

She had no answer for that in any order. Her right hand went to the pocket of her vest and stopped there, over whatever was in it.

"I told Dale I'd ask you to give the league its Memorial back. I'm asking."

"Linda's name comes off the Open."

"The league bowls its Memorial at two on the last Saturday, all ten lanes."

"Your Open gets the morning and the evening, and a cup of its own, with whatever name you like on it."

"Hollis says the committee voted on an Open. Nobody voted on a name."

He watched her run it. It took her less time than it took him to see she was doing it.

"Agreed." She said the rest without stopping. "Nine teams entered to bowl for Linda's cup. I will call all nine in the morning and offer their money back, and all nine will take it. That puts me a hundred and fifteen dollars under thirty thousand, with every lane of the Open full."

Ray offered her nothing. Anything he put on that counter the bank would count as his, and she would know it before he had it out of his pocket.

He unfolded the page and turned it round to face her. It was one page from Arlene's lawyer, and it let the three of them go from the March paper, with Arlene's signature first and his under it and a line left at the bottom.

"I'm asking you to sign it."

She put on the reading glasses that hung on the black cord and read it once, down to the line, and took them off again.

"You have asked an eighty-four-year-old woman to bet her bed on a hundred and fifteen dollars."

"I know what I asked."

He had heard the price of it at Arlene's table, in her words, and carried it out Kettle Road in his jacket.

"You could keep it in your jacket until I have made the number, and tear it up then. Arlene would have her bed whatever I made."

"I could. I'd be fixing your machines for one of us all month, and I couldn't tell you which."

She looked at him over the page, and then down at it. "If I miss, Arlene loses her place, and it will be my name that put it there." She took a pen out of her vest. "If I ask you to stand beside me before this is done, I won't have you standing at the bottom of my drive as well."

She uncapped the pen and set the cap upright on the counter and bent to the line. There was no other sound in the building. The pinsetters were off and the fryer was cold and the heat had gone down for the night, and he could hear the point go into the paper and drag, a small dry sound, and he watched her hand make the E and lift and go on, every letter standing separate, the same printing that had come down the yellow page under the lot light at Pine Ridge, each one finished before she began the next, with no hurry anywhere in it, and Arlene's bed going down onto the paper with every stroke, and his own name above hers coming loose from her building as she went. She wrote Daly, and capped the pen.

"I'll call Concord in the morning and let it go. They'd want ninety days to write it again."

"There are thirteen days." She had counted them before he could.

"I called Laconia this morning. They're bending you a K."

She looked at him a while. "Thank you."

Then her hand went into the vest pocket it had stopped at, and brought out his key, the one he had worked off his ring with his thumbnail and left beside her pad, and she held it across the counter to him on her open palm.

He looked at it, lying in the middle of her palm with no tag on it.

"Not yet."
