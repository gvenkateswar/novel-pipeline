# Chapter 8: Position Relief

Three's sweep motor hung half out of its bracket, and Ray had the brushes worked loose and was blowing the dust off them, with Hollis holding the flashlight and the league clipboard clamped under Hollis's other arm. It was Thursday, the fourth of February, a few minutes past six, and past the masking the house was lit end to end with nobody bowling in it yet. Hollis had phoned the store at five ("Three's dropping late. She hasn't called anybody.") and let him in the back from inside, and that was all the asking there had been on either side. Ray had quit her league on Tuesday, in front of everybody who mattered to it. He still didn't leave a machine down on a league night, whoever's it was, and Hollis knew it, and had dialed accordingly.

Her voice came down sixty feet of empty lane to him through the gap under the masking, from the phone at the counter. It carried without being raised. It always had.

"The Linda Gagnon Memorial Open, Saturday the twenty-seventh."

She said it to the caller as plainly as she'd give out a lane number. A pencil went, he'd guess, and then: "A team from Lewiston. Spell it for me?"

The phone went down and rang again before he had the second brush out, and she said it again, the whole of it, Linda's first name and her married one and the word Memorial after them, to somebody from away who would write it on whatever was handy and drive in from Maine to bowl under it. She had been saying it every few minutes since he came in the back. He had not said his wife's whole name out loud in one breath since the funeral home, where a fella in a gray suit had needed it for a form, and here it came down lane three in that voice, with the next caller already waiting. Linda had driven to Lewiston for opens in a Buick with a heater that worked when it felt like it, and come home with cups. His hands stopped on the bracket. Hollis's light stayed where it was, on the brushes. His hands started again.

He got the second brush out and laid both on the bench, worn down short on the one side, and the phone rang.

"Mr. Arsenault," she said. Then nothing, for long enough that Ray knew what was being said to her. "Yes. Her name is on it."

He could have given her Gil's half himself. Gil had said it at every open since, in a good wool coat, to anybody standing near enough: "Linda beat me in the 'ninety-four open, and I'd like a second look."

"Then you're the fourteenth team," Ellen said. "I'll put you in the morning."

The receiver went down and came straight back up. "Judy, the indoor day comes to Saturday with the Open, and the club's fee with it. It uses the snack bar and the settees, the weigh-in board and the raffle. It doesn't take a lane. Sunday stays empty."

Hollis chaired the derby. He kept the light on the brushes. "She's had my yes since Tuesday."

Then he took the light off the motor and put it in Ray's face.

"You going to tell her what you said was wrong, or fix another one?"

Ray squinted into it. "Hold the light where I'm working, Hollis."

"I am."

The light stayed on his face. Ray found the bracket with his left hand and the motor's foot with his right and seated her by feel, with the beam in his eyes and the rest of the pit gone black around it, and ran the two bolts down snug and a quarter turn past.

On the bench he made out the invoice on the pad he'd brought from the store: his hours, the Worcester man's rate, two brushes, a total with a line ruled under it, in her format, in his square printing. At the bottom he wrote, Light held two hours by H. Pike. No charge.

He came up the walkway from the steel door by lane ten with his toolbag, past the empty settees, and she saw him coming before he was halfway. She was still on with Judy. He laid the paper on her counter, and she read it with the receiver held against her shoulder, every line of it, down to the last.

"You've put Hollis on my bill."

"No charge. He's worth about that."

He was already turning for the vestibule. He had the inner door open, and the cold off the outer glass on his face, when she said into the phone behind him, "Judy, I'll call you back."

\* \* \*

Ray rapped three times on the front door glass, and the glass rattled in its frame. It was Monday, the eighth, the building shut to the public as it was every Monday, and the sun just down behind the lot; at five he had turned the burner off under the pot on School Street, which made him late for his own supper at a quarter past five, and so far he hadn't billed her for a single supper, though he had the line worked out. Through the glass she was at the counter under the one light, ruling squad sheets for the Open with a straightedge. She looked up at the knock. She finished the line before she came. He stood on the granite step and watched her finish it, the straightedge held down under four fingers and the pencil drawn along it to the edge of the sheet, and then the pencil laid down square beside it.

She came out through the inner door into the vestibule and stopped at the glass, with the corkboard of lost mittens at her shoulder and the counter light behind her, and she didn't touch the lock.

Behind him his truck ticked in the empty lot. The door's closer hissed when it shut and wanted a turn on its adjusting screw; he had heard it the first night and every night since, and his hand wanted the screwdriver in his jacket. He kept his hand where it was.

In the truck, every way he'd tried it had a reason in it somewhere toward the middle, and he'd taken the reasons out one at a time coming up Kettle Road, and what was left was short, and all of it his.

"I said you sounded fine on the radio that night."

He had put the worst of it first, so he couldn't stop short of it.

She took it standing. Her chin came up a degree and stayed there.

"You'd told me about that night in the dark with a pin in your hands, and I picked it because it would land, and I used it on you."

Her eyes stayed on his. She folded her arms across her vest, and unfolded them, and let her hands hang.

He went on before he could tidy it.

"And I walked out on thirty years of people so you'd watch me go."

She looked past him once, down the drive to the road where his lights had gone that night, and brought her eyes back.

"And you were telling me what it cost you to ask me, and I didn't let you finish."

That one she took at the mouth. It pressed shut and held.

"I'm sorry."

He stopped there. There was nothing to put after it, and he had made sure of that in the truck.

"Understood."

It was one word, and he let it be one word.

She let a breath go. "I'd like to hear whether it holds."

"I'd like to hear the rest of what you were saying."

He said it through the glass to her face, with his hands in his jacket pockets, and waited on her.

"At your counter," she said, "that was the first time I have ever asked anybody for something I wanted."

Her right hand came up and lay flat on the inside of the glass, about the height of his heart. He took his own out of his pocket and put it up on the outside, level with hers, palm to her palm with the glass between. Her fingertips came to his second knuckles. The glass was cold under his palm and stayed cold whatever his hand did to it, and on her side her breath made a gray patch low on the pane that faded while he watched and came back when she breathed again.

"Then I took it to your people."

Her hand stayed on the glass. So did his.

"At that table I let the quiet do my asking."

He had sat at the foot of that table and heard the quiet go on, and taken it for the room's.

"One sentence from me would have ended it for you, and I didn't say it."

The patch on the pane faded and came back.

"I knew what it was costing you."

Her voice came off its level on the first two words. He heard it come off.

"I let it cost you."

Ray laid Gagnon & Son's entry for Linda's Open on her counter on Tuesday the ninth, about twenty past six, with first ball ten minutes off and the house filling behind him: four names in his own hand, his at the top, and paid. Ellen put her glasses on from the cord and read it herself, every line of it, the names and the squad and his signature at the foot. Then she took the glasses off and left it lying where he'd put it.

"I'd like to hear it again," she said. "Out there."

Her eyes went past him to the settees.

He turned round with his back to her counter and faced the house. League night meant the Highway crew with their hoods still up from the trucks, and a Legion man on lane three heckling his own warm-up ball, and the settees full of wives and coats and somebody's grandson asleep across a bowling bag, and Verna at the scorer's table on lane one with her binder open, and down at six, Hollis, with the clipboard. The noise was a whole house warming up, and as he stood there it began to come off a table at a time, nearest first, as people saw who was standing with his back to Ellen's counter.

He had the words he'd said to her at the door, exact. They were the short way through this, and they would hand this room her two in the morning, which was hers, and which he had spent once already. So he'd have to say it plain, with nothing in front of it to stand behind. He had stood up in this building a good many times and told it what the league thought, and what the old teams wanted, and what Linda would have liked, and every time there'd been somebody else's name for him to stand behind. He hadn't asked anybody in this building for anything since 'ninety-six. He'd asked the store once, and the store only. And the word was cruel. Short, he could have said, or sore, or out of line, and the room would have taken any of those and handed him a ball. Cruel they'd have to sit with. He'd have to hear it in his own voice, to people he'd coached, about a woman behind him who would hear it too, and he had looked for a softer word that was true, and there wasn't one.

"I was cruel to Mrs. Daly on purpose," he said.

One of the Highway crew pushed his hood back off his head and left it down.

"And I walked out on you to make her watch."

The Legion man had a ball up by his chin. He set it back on the rack and took his hand away.

"I'd like back in."

A ball came up the Highway crew's return and sat there, and nobody reached for it. Their whole lane had gone still, four men in plow jackets standing where they'd stopped.

"Verna, I'm asking."

Verna closed the binder with both hands. Down at six, Hollis's pencil went on moving on the clipboard, and his head stayed down.

Verna spoke from her table without getting up.

"The secretary notes Mr. Gagnon resigned in front of witnesses and has asked in front of witnesses. He may bowl. Mr. Pike keeps the clipboard through the Memorial."

The laugh started on the settees at the secretary and went down the lanes, and the Legion man took his ball back off the rack, and somewhere at the far end a ball went down and took its wood, and the noise of a Tuesday came back up over all of it, a table at a time.

The room had it well before Ray did. Mr. Pike kept the clipboard. The last Saturday in February would be the first since 'ninety-six he didn't coach, and Linda's day would go by with him on a settee, waiting his turn like anybody.

Behind him paper moved. When he turned, Ellen had his paper in her hand, and he watched her put it in the box at her elbow with the others.

He took his bag down the walkway to six. Hollis was at the scorer's table with the clipboard flat in front of him and the golf pencil going, and he spoke low, with his eyes on the sheet.

"You missed Sunday. Quitting my league doesn't quit you out of my bob-house. I scored it a forfeit."

First time ahead in eleven winters, and it had gone on a Sunday he'd spent at his own kitchen table.

"Holy mackerel," Ray said.

"We're level."

Ray set his bag down by the settee. He took a ball off the rack between five and six, the first one his hand came to, and stepped up on six's approach with Hollis and the clipboard behind him, and turned it loose, and it was still going down the maple.

The steel drawer of the office desk ran out on its rollers, and Ellen came back through to the counter with the paper and laid it between them, at ten on Presidents' Day morning, with the building shut and the front door left unlocked because she'd said it would be. It was the release. His signature from January sat alone on the first line, and the two lines under it were blank. It had lain in her drawer since then and done nothing by itself.

"If you come back, you come back beside me. I'll sign Arlene's paper, and I'll tell her myself."

"In January I'd have been signing her bed away on a winter I was losing. With Linda's name on it, it's a winter I'm winning, if every lane fills."

"I gave you her name, and the building with it. That's not enough?"

"You gave it angry. I need you to choose it."

He heard the chair go back on the dry floor, the second of February, and his own voice over the top of it.

"Then I'll choose it, on a Monday, with nobody in the room. Linda's name on your Open, and my name off your paper. I'd like both."

"The bank in Concord has held me a loan since December, to buy this place at appraisal if you missed. I'll phone them in the morning and let it go."

Ellen picked up the counter phone and dialed Arlene's number from memory, and said into it, "We're on our way down," and hung up.

She drove down behind his truck in her own car. Main Street was keeping the holiday: Granite Lakes dark, Massachusetts plates parked up the hill, and at the bottom Gagnon & Son lit, with Danny alone behind the counter and a line for ice cleats out past the salt.

Arlene was at her table, the boxes taped shut along the wall and her coat over the back of her chair, as though the coat meant to leave first. The radiator under the window clanked and settled. Ellen didn't sit. She put the paper down in front of Arlene and stood with her hands at her sides.

"With every lane of the Open full, I clear by six hundred. One storm takes that."

Arlene looked out the window at the lake, and back.

"If I sign this and I miss, it's a bank sale. You wait for your money and go back on the list, the condo man gets the lot for the land, and the house money my daughter let me spend goes with it."

Arlene's eyes went to Ray and stayed there.

"If you miss, I'm not at the bank's sale either," he said to Ellen. "I said I'd not buy your mistake cheap. That's cheaper."

He turned to Arlene. "That costs you, Arlene. A sale with me in it pays you more."

"I'll take the condo man's money before I'll take yours off her mistake, Raymond."

"There's money I swore to my daughter," Ellen said. "It isn't in this."

"You ought to call Kate."

"Kate gave me the house to lose. I'm not asking her to watch me lose it a second way. I'll tell her, after."

Arlene drew the paper across to her side of the table. "I'll sign it, dear, and keep it in my drawer till the count."

"Then he's behind me till March with his hand on your drawer. Today, or not at all."

Arlene looked at her, and then at Ray, who added nothing.

"I said I'd sign when you did, dear. I didn't say I'd like it."

Ellen took her own pen out of her vest and signed standing, on the second line. Arlene signed last, once, sitting, and capped the pen and handed it back to Ellen. Ellen folded the paper on its creases and put it in Ray's hand.

Ray went down first. The stair was steep and narrow under one bulb, and with the pharmacy shut for the holiday the cold came up it from the side door at the bottom.

He was all the way out of her paper now. It was in his inside pocket, and at six tomorrow he would lay it on the store counter beside Danny's envelope.

At eight this morning, from the store phone, he had ordered the K from the shop in Laconia at the quote he'd given her, on Kettle Lanes' account, and asked them to date the bill the first of March, so it would come off nothing she was counting. He hadn't told her.

Halfway down he heard her stop above him, and he turned on his step. For once her eyes were level with his. She was wearing his gloves. They were big on her, the fingers creased where hers ran out. She took his key out of her vest pocket and held it out to him on the palm of his glove.

"Not yet," he said.

He went down the rest of the stair, and his right knee clicked twice on the way. At the side door he put his shoulder to it, and it opened on the cold, and he looked back up. She was still holding it out, the key on the palm of his glove, under the one bulb.
