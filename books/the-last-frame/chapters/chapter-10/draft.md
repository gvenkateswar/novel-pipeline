# Chapter 10: Ice-Out

The pocketknife took the cedar off in long curls, and they dropped one over another onto the counter beside her cigar box. There were ten lanes of sheets to put pencils to for Linda's day, and most of the box still to go. On the radio over the fryer the Laconia station had Hollis on.

"Four inches of slush on top at the public access, and water up through every hole I cut. Derby's off for Sunday."

She had let him in at half past six, and the whole time she turned the bolt his key was in her vest pocket. He'd heard it tick against her pen. Since then she had filled the urn and set out the sheets and said nothing about Manchester, and the pencil in his hand had come to a point and gone on past it, and it was getting short.

Her phone went off in her vest. She took it out and looked at the name. Then she laid it face up on the counter between them, beside the cigar box, and touched the speaker.

An engine idled at the other end, and somewhere near it a car door shut.

"Am I on speaker?"

"You are. Mr. Gagnon is here."

"Mr. Gagnon."

"Ray."

"Ray," Kate said. She had left her turn signal on, and it ticked under her. She sounded like her mother run faster.

"Okay. I said I'd sleep on it, and I slept on it, four hours in this garage with the seat back, which counts, I've had worse on nights—" The signal stopped. "I did the subtraction anyway. Without the dollars. It's harder than it sounds."

Ellen had both hands flat on the counter, one on either side of the phone.

The engine cut out at Kate's end. "Then do it."

Ellen's hands stayed where they were.

Keys came out of an ignition. "And call me first next time."

Kate stopped there. He could hear her listening to their end: the fryer ticking, the radio gone on to the price of heating oil, and him with the knife in his hand and nothing to do with it. Nobody in the alley moved.

"Like this."

He set the knife down by the shavings. "I'd have said no."

Her car door slammed. "You weren't asked."

Ellen turned her face toward the shoe cubbies, and he watched the corner of her mouth hold out against it.

"I'm going in. They put you on the board if you're late, and the charge nurse reads the board out loud, it's medieval." A pair of doors opened on a hospital, and a page went out a long way down a hall. "Mom."

Ellen leaned down to the phone.

"Is he the one who followed you out as far as the plow had been?"

The call ended, and the screen went dark between them.

\* \* \*

The small brass key came off his ring hard. He stood at the customer side of her counter and worked a thumbnail into the split end and walked the key round, while the house filled up behind him with the thaw tracked in on its feet. The Highway crew came in off the night's plowing with their eyes red, Dale Hutchins among them.

Ellen was at the sheets with her pen.

"Kettle Lanes," he said, "this is Gagnon. Request."

She finished the name she was on. Then she set down her pen. "Gagnon, say request."

He laid the brass key on the counter between them. "That's locker fourteen's. It's on your wall. I'd like it open, and you there when it is."

She looked at it a while, and picked it up, and came round the counter.

At the case Denise had a cloth on the Memorial cup, because it was Sunday, and went on pretending to dust it. The heat was coming up, and the case glass ticked a foot from his ear.

Ellen put the key in the padlock and turned it, and nothing turned. Six winters it had hung there without a hand on it, and it was not going to start for hers. She set her wrist and tried again. He put his hand over hers on the key. It went under the two of them, stiff and then sudden, and the shackle dropped.

She hung the padlock on the hasp and opened the door.

Linda's bag was on the floor of the locker with her shoes on top of it, laces tucked in, and her ball sat on the shelf above where she had left it. There was one thing more. A league sheet, folded in four, stood up out of the bag's side pocket. Ellen read any paper in her building. She drew it out and read it, and folded it once, and gave it to him without a word.

It was the last Tuesday Linda had bowled. Three strings in her own hand, and none of the three anything anybody would put in a frame. She'd had a poor box in the middle string and written it down as careful as the good ones.

He folded the sheet into his shirt pocket.

Then he lifted the ball off the shelf. It was cold from the wall and heavier than it looked, and he had carried it out to the car for her more Tuesdays than he had ever counted.

Denise put down her cloth. "I lost to that ball twice, Ray Gagnon, and the second time was worse, because she was nice about it, she told me it was the best she'd ever seen me bowl, which it was, and I lost anyway, so don't you go bowling it sentimental on me today, because I'll beat you regardless and then I'll have to feel bad about it."

Ellen had gone back ahead of him. When he came back past the counter with the ball, the gooseneck was already bent across to him. He leaned to it.

"From next year Linda's name goes on the Open, and anybody that pays the entry can win it, because Linda would've entered on day one."

Hollis looked up from the settee at lane five. "Committee never voted on a name."

Then he opened his waterproof notebook on his knee and wrote it down.

Ray's first box with Linda's ball was on lane five. His thumb went looking for the place hers had worn into it, a dull patch about the size of a dime, and found it, and was too big for it. He bowled ordinary strings with it. Each time he looked up, with all ten lanes going at once, Ellen was behind the counter with her eyes shut.

At the end it came down to lanes five and six, Lakeview Diner against Gagnon and Son, with the other eight lanes finished and their bowlers along the rail. Denise threw the last ball. Verna turned a page in her binder.

"The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins."

Denise sat down on the settee and said nothing at all.

The room came up onto its feet. It started at the far settees, with the Highway crew getting up in their town jackets, and went down the building from there, the Legion along the back and Judy from the bank in the standing room with her pocketbook on her arm, until every one of them was up and the noise came back off the low ceiling. At the counter Ellen's hand was on the gooseneck. She didn't key it.

Denise got up after a while and went to the case she had dusted every Sunday for six years, and took the cup out with both hands. There was no ring on the glass where it had stood.

Gil Arsenault was at the rail in his good overcoat, come over from Halsey Falls for Linda's day.

"Mrs. Lavoie." He held out the spring-doubles flyer.

Denise took the flyer and folded it into her pocket. Then she turned round to Ray with the cup against her.

"Gil's short a partner for the spring doubles. I'm thinking about it."

He didn't answer it. He lifted Linda's ball off the return on lane five and carried it past the open locker, through a room still on its feet, to the house rack between lanes four and five. He set it on the shelf among the house balls, by Ellen's tape on the steel upright, and gave it a quarter-turn until it sat, with Ellen watching from the counter and his hand still on it.

\* \* \*

His fingers were in the double knot of his bowling shoe, up on the end stool, and three strings had pulled it tight. Past the stool the building was dark but for the light over the counter. Across the room locker fourteen stood open with its padlock hung on the hasp, and Linda's ball sat on the house rack among the others. Her sheet was still folded in his shirt pocket.

Ellen came out of the office with her keys. "I'm going to look at seven."

"Seven's fine."

She went through the steel door by lane ten anyway. He got the other knot undone and his boots on, and gave it a minute, and went after her with the laces loose.

She was sitting on the iron step behind seven, where she had sat at New Year's, with nothing in her hands. He sat down beside her on the cold iron.

The machines had been off an hour. Down the catwalk the motors were giving up the day's heat, ticking as they cooled, one close by and then another farther off, while the warmth went out of the kickback steel at his back and her breathing came slow beside him. He let them tick. At New Year's she had carried a cup of cider down here and never drunk it. The curtain at the end of seven hung still, with the deck behind it set for nobody, and the grease and belt smell had gone thin in the cold. Her bad knee was an inch from his. Both her hands were on it.

"You asked me whether New Year's too. It was. I knew, and I came down here at midnight so you'd find me."

"I'd about worked that out, in the truck."

A motor ticked, farther down.

"Kate asked me something this morning, with you standing there."

"I heard her."

"I watched your lights turn round at the end of the plowing. I watched them all the way back to town."

"Left one's been aimed at the ditch since Christmas," he said. "You'd have known it was me."

He looked at her hands. She kept them where they were. Her watch was going on her wrist, quicker than any motor on the catwalk, a small steel tick he had never once heard over a full house, and he sat on the cold iron and listened to it and did not hurry her.

"The night of the storm," she said, and stopped, and started it again from the top. "The night of the storm I told you two things and kept the third, because it wasn't in order."

The motor nearest them gave one more tick and quit. On the other side of the masking the whole building had gone still, and he could hear the urn at the snack bar cooling, a long way off, and a car go by on Kettle Road with its tires in the slush.

She turned on the iron step to face him, and her bad knee came against his.

"It is now. Third." She stopped on it, a heading with nothing yet under it, and the watch went on. "I love you."

He took her hands off her knee in both of his.

"Third item," he said, and heard her words come out in his voice, and went on with it. "You love me."

Her hands went still inside his. He let a breath go.

"And one you left off. I've loved you since about the first night, when you kept me knocking at your door to finish your tape."

Ellen came up off the iron to reach him. Her reading glasses swung out on their black cord and caught on the pull of his jacket zipper. The cord stopped her short, an inch from him. She came the last inch anyway, with the cord drawn tight at the back of her neck.

Ray laughed against her mouth.

Then Ellen was kissing him, and he was kissing her back. Her hand went to his collar and took a fistful of the canvas. Her other hand came up along his jaw, over a day's gray stubble, and stayed there. He put his hand at the back of her head, in the short hair, and held her to him. The cord ran taut between them, and neither of them saw to it. Down the catwalk one more motor ticked and quit. Ellen took her time. He had never once seen her take it over anything, and she took it now, slow and plain, with her thumb on the hinge of his jaw. When she sat back the cord drew tight again, and the glasses hung off his jacket between them.

She unhooked them from his zipper with two fingers. "Twice behind lane seven and once under the sign. I've kept count."

Her hand went to her vest pocket, where his key was, and lay flat on it, and came away with nothing in it.

\* \* \*

"You're in the other chair."

Her voice came from a little way off the phone, and under it heels went across marble, and the drive-up tube came down with a thunk. She had set her phone face up on the chair beside her at Judy's desk. The committee had sat at two, and Judy was in it.

The Bench sat on their stools with their cups halfway up. Behind the register the Bench's old gray sheet hung on its nail, every square pencilled in but the last. He had kept to his own counter all day so that nobody on Main Street could say whose money it was.

A door opened at the far end of the line. Heels came across, and Judy's chair creaked round.

"Committee approved it."

His hand went flat on the counter. Nobody on the Bench drank.

Down the line, cellophane.

"She's got the butterscotch out," he said to the Bench, low.

"I can hear you, Ray. It's approved subject to one thing. Your contract with Arlene is on record at the county, and the release from February was never notarized, so title won't insure over it." A drawer slid shut. "I like you, Ray. The underwriter has never met you. I need you at this desk, signing it again in front of me, before the wire room shuts at four, or Arlene's money sits till tomorrow."

He was already moving. The Pines held her room through the first. The envelope came out of his jacket, where it had ridden all month, and he put it in Danny's hand. Danny didn't open it.

"Go on. I've got the counter."

His jacket hung flat on that side going up Main Street. He went past the barber's and up to the pharmacy, where a van stood at the side door and boxes were going up into it marked in Arlene's green-ledger hand for THE PINES. Arlene stood on the sidewalk in her good coat with her pocketbook on her arm, and watched each one go.

"Raymond. I'll have my key back while you're passing."

He worked the old key off his ring, orange tag and all, and put it in her hand. She licked her thumb and counted it.

"One."

"That tag's about worn through."

"So am I." She looked up the hill at the bank. "The van goes at four, and I'm going in it."

The drive-up tube thunked again as he came in, and his own boots went across the marble. Judy's glass office had the lake trout on the wall and the bowl on the desk, and on her blotter, in her hand, a slush depth a quarter inch under the one Hollis had given the radio. Ellen was in one chair. On the other lay her phone, face up and still lit, and down it came his own store: the key cutter's whine, Danny cutting somebody a key.

He picked it up and ended the call and handed it to her, and sat down in the chair.

Judy looked at him over her glasses. "Is that Ray Gagnon on my chair?"

"In it."

She turned the release round to face him. "Sign it again. Here."

He wrote his name in the same scrawl he had put under Arlene's. Judy's seal came down on it and crimped the page, and he felt it through the desk.

She turned the next page to Ellen, and her pen clicked. "Eighty-eight hundred, out of your savings and onto Arlene's note. New money. Any of it his?"

"None of it is his."

Ellen signed it in one go and capped her pen. Then, low, to him: "What's left heats the camp through one bad winter."

He kept his hand on his knee.

Judy picked up her phone and called the wire room and gave it to them, and waited, and put the phone down.

"It's gone."

He looked past her, through the glass and the lobby's front window. Down Main Street the van pulled out from beside the pharmacy with Arlene in the passenger seat, and turned toward The Pines.

\* \* \*

The new K was cold glass the length of his forearm, bent in Laconia to the old script's curl, and at the top of the ladder he seated its electrode in the housing with the disconnect open on the pole below him and the shop's sheet in his shirt pocket. The old one had gone dark the winter Danny got his license, and the town had called the place Ettle's ever since.

Ellen held the foot of the ladder in both hands. It went steady under his boots, and he could feel her through the rails.

He came down with the old K, dark glass in the old curl, and laid it on the plow bank at the foot of the pole. The banks had gone gray in the thaw. Across the road water stood in long sheets around the bob-houses.

His ring hung at his belt with a gap in it where Arlene's key had been. Ellen took his key out of her vest pocket and laid it on her open palm between them.

"That's not asking."

She closed her fingers on it and opened them again.

"Ray." She let it stand a second. "Will you take it?"

He took it off her palm, warm from her vest. He opened the split end of the ring with his thumbnail and worked the key on a quarter turn at a time, slow, into the place Arlene's had hung thirty years, and she watched every turn.

The light was going. He stood at the foot of the pole under the sign's cabinet, and she stood at the disconnect with her hand on the handle.

"Throw her, Mrs. Daly."

He waited to be corrected. She threw it.

The transformer took hold, and he felt the buzz of it in the pole. Red came down over the plow bank and out across the wet road, onto the old K lying in the snow, lit by the letters it used to sit with and dark itself. From under the cabinet he couldn't see the letters.

"How's she read?"

Ellen walked out into the middle of Kettle Road and looked up.

"Both words."
