# Chapter 10: Both Words

Ray was taking the score pencils for the final down to a point with his pocketknife, the shavings curling off the blade onto her counter's worn front edge and smelling of cedar. Her car was snowed in out in the lot, so he had fetched her from the camp before light.

Danny had opened the store alone every morning since he signed its papers, the morning after the release, and Ray had not counted that drawer since. Nobody had asked him back behind it, so he was sharpening pencils at a woman's counter. In thirty years not one of his pencils had broken in a tenth box, and he had never once bragged about it.

The radio clicked.

"Kettle, Hollis. Four inches of slush on top of the ice and water up through every hole. Derby's Sunday is off. Your ten still held?"

Ellen keyed it. "All ten."

Her phone rang. She laid it face up between them and touched the speaker before she said hello.

"Am I on speaker?"

"Ray is here. Shall I take you off?"

"Leave him on. Slept four hours in the hospital garage with the seat back. I did the subtraction. Without the dollars. It's harder than it sounds. That money's why I sleep. If it goes in, I'm your bad winter."

"Yes," Kate said. "And in April, in person, you tell me about the night you came home off a midnight and sat in the car in the driveway till I left for school. All of it. I'll ask questions."

Ray knew the night; she had told it to him in the pit, and he had used it on her at this counter. The car and the driveway, and a girl walking past it to the bus, were new to him.

Ellen's hand came down flat on the counter beside the phone and stayed there.

"Yes," she said. It came a half beat late and lower than her counter voice.

"There you are," Kate said. "And call me first next time." A beat. "Like this."

"I'd have asked for less," Ray said, before he could stop himself.

"You weren't asked." A beat. "I'm coming up in April to see what I paid for."

She was gone. Ellen dialed Judy.

"My tip-ups are under four inches of slush," Judy said, "so you may as well talk to me about money."

"My savings go on the note."

"Your own money lowers the line slowly. It takes a good many of your dollars to buy down one of the winter's."

"When does the committee sit?"

"Ten tomorrow. They don't vote on a winter till it's over, and they vote on money that's arrived. Bring it in by nine and I'll hold it here till they vote."

"I'll call you back."

She hung up, and Ray supposed she was taking the hold. Then she dialed Kate, and the knife stopped halfway down a pencil.

"Judy will hold it until the committee votes. I want it sent to Arlene's note in the morning, before they vote. If they say no, none of it comes back."

"You called me first," Kate said. "It's yours, Mom. Send it."

Ellen dialed Judy again. Ray had a sentence ready about holds, and he folded the knife shut over it.

"Send it to Arlene's note at nine. If they say no, she's that much paid whatever happens to me."

"Then it goes at nine," Judy said. "I can't promise you ten."

\* \* \*

From locker fourteen, the whole house away from her counter, Ray keyed the handheld.

"Kettle, Gagnon. Request."

"Gagnon, say request."

"Request you come hold a key."

She set the Memorial cup on the scorer's table at five and six and came.

He worked the small key off his ring, where it had ridden six winters without once being put in anything, and set it in the padlock. She laid her fingers on it and he put his hand over hers, and the white seam along his finger lay across her knuckles. It took the two of them to turn the cold brass, and it gave with a small dry click.

On the shelf lay Linda's last league sheet, three strings in her pencil, with a three in the sixth box of the second string pressed in as hard as any strike. He unfolded it where Ellen could read it and laid it back. Then he took out the ball, small and holeless and heavier than it looked, cold from the outside wall. He left the door standing open and the padlock hanging open on its hasp.

Denise went by with her bag. "Don't you go bowling it sentimental on me."

He didn't answer for Linda.

Ellen kept her counter voice level. "Next year. Will you put her name on it again? I'm asking."

At the counter she stepped back from her gooseneck, and he used her microphone for the first time.

"From now on Linda's name goes on this Open every winter, and anybody who enters can win it, because Linda would've entered on day one."

On lane two the Legion's anchor, the Ostrowski boy, held his ball under his chin till it was said.

Denise said it to Ellen. "That's how a name goes on a cup, honey. The man it belongs to says so."

Then all ten lanes went at once for three strings, and once Ray saw Ellen at the fryer with her eyes shut for the length of one crash; he saw the Highway crew on ten with plow sand in their cuffs, in on the Saturday envelope Dale had laid back on her counter with the flap still stuck, and the Lewiston morning team out of the money by the second string, and Gil Arsenault in his good overcoat with a folded flyer in his glove, watching Pine Ridge go on to third, and Hollis with the clipboard, and himself on a settee with nothing to coach.

Denise had the tenth box on six. Her first ball went in a hair light and took the front of the rack and left the rest. Her second cleared the side and left the four horsemen standing at the back. She wiped her palm down her glove. Her third ball went straight up the old wood and took all four. Hollis marked it and said nothing. Lakeview Diner was done.

Ray had the tenth box on five with Linda's ball.

"You need all ten," Hollis said, with his eyes on the sheet.

His first ball brought the rack down to four. Up at the scorer's table Denise had her hands flat on the cup. His second took two of the four, and the two left standing were at the back and a long way apart. At the fryer Ellen had a basket stopped in her hand, dripping. Hollis's pencil was off the sheet. Ray let the third ball go as well as he had let one go in his life, and it went up the maple between the two of them, and the two pins stood.

He took Linda's ball off the return and set it on the house rack among the house balls, where anybody could reach it.

The room came up onto its feet. Denise's hands were still on the cup. Verna stood with her binder.

"The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins."

\* \* \*

Ellen had her glasses on from the cord, and Ray held the register tape flat on the counter under the heel of his hand and read her the envelopes off the stack, one at a time. The snack bar. The door, for the spectators. The Highway Department's, in her printing from Saturday. She wrote each total under the last in small upright figures and ran the column down once and up once, and her pencil came back to where it had started. The lot was empty out front. Behind them the house was dark to lane ten, and the only light in the building was the lamp they worked under.

"Nine hundred dollars under," she said.

Ray had been in and out of Arlene's green ledgers since before Danny could walk, and he had never said a number out of them he hadn't rounded. "Arlene's best winter in those ledgers was twenty-one thousand, near enough, the year the roof got done."

"Thirty is the line."

"And Kate said yes."

She took her glasses off and let them drop on the cord against her vest. "Come back to the pit with me."

She said it polite and exact, an order, and by now he knew that was what her voice did when she was frightened.

They went through the steel door by lane ten and down the catwalk in the dark, past the machines standing quiet, and she stopped behind seven.

The pit had gone cold with the motors stopped. Ray put his hand on seven's kickback, and the steel was cold right through his palm, and the grease smell came up off the machine anyway, warm or not. Through the gap under the masking the lanes ran away from them backward, sixty feet of old maple with nothing on it, lit only by the counter lamp far up the house. Ellen stood with her hands at her sides and kept them there.

"The night of the storm I told you two things and kept the third, because it wasn't in order," she said. "It is now."

She let a breath go, and he heard it.

"Third. I love you."

She said it plain enough to carry to lane ten, and then she stood there having said it.

Ray said, "Third item. You love me."

He looked at her the length of one breath. He had his own, and it had been waiting a while with no slot to go in.

"I've loved you since about the first night, when you kept me knocking at your door to finish your tape."

He had stood out in the snow that night and watched her through the door glass run her thumb the whole length of the strip and press it flat, as though nobody was out there at all.

She reached up and took his reading glasses off his forehead, where he had forgotten them some time during the count, and folded them, and put them in her vest. Then she put her hands inside his open jacket and took hold of the canvas at his ribs and held on. Her hands were cold from the tape and the envelopes, and he could feel them through the canvas, and she did not let go. He put his hand to the back of her neck, under her collar, where her hair was cut short and the skin was warm from the house, and he bent his head and kissed her slowly, with the whole dark building around them and nothing in it that needed him.

When he drew her in, the cord of her glasses caught on his jacket zipper and pulled her up short.

"Hold still," she said, against his collar. "My cord's in your zipper."

He held still. He found it with his fingers in the dark, a fine black cord gone two or three teeth deep into the brass, and he worked it back out one tooth at a time, which was close work, and she stood inside his jacket with her hands where they were and let him do it, and he could feel her breathing against his chin while he worked, and neither of them said a word about how long it was taking, and it took a while, and he didn't hurry any part of it. The cord came free. He laid it back against her vest with two fingers.

Then he kissed her again, and this time her mouth came up to his before he got there, and one of them made a sound, low, and afterward he could not have told you which. He held her, and she let him, and neither of them was keeping the time.

She put her forehead against his jacket and left it there. Far up the house the counter lamp burned by itself, and the committee would sit in the morning, and he couldn't keep from asking.

"And if they say no?"

\* \* \*

Ray left the truck down by the dock and walked up Main Street, and the bell on the store door went before he got to it. Danny stood in the doorway in his apron with the door held open on his shoulder, letting the cold in, which he never did. Behind him the old kitchen chair nobody sat in had been pulled out from the wall and set at the counter by the register.

"You coming in?" Danny said.

"You asking?"

"I'm asking."

"After the bank."

He went on up the hill and gave the sidewalk salt his full attention, every step.

Ellen was waiting at the bank's door, standing, with no gloves on.

Judy let them in by the side door before the lobby opened and went off down the hall for the wire forms. Her office was glass on two sides, with a framed lake trout on the wall and a bowl of butterscotch on the desk, and out in the marble lobby the loons on the calendar were still on February. Her swivel chair leaned to the left with nobody in it. Ray had nothing else in the world to do, so he sat in it, opened the pocketknife to its screwdriver, and went after the tilt screw underneath.

Judy came back with the forms. "Is that Ray Gagnon on my chair?"

"In it." He gave the screw a last quarter turn. "She leaned."

"Then sit in the one that's yours."

He moved to a customer chair. Ellen stayed standing. Judy took the forms out to the wire desk in the lobby.

Arlene came in by the side door Judy had left on the latch, in her good gray hat with the pin in the band and her pocketbook on her arm, and took the other customer chair, which left Ellen standing, which suited Ellen.

"Raymond. I'll have my key back while you're sitting."

The 'ninety-six key had been dead since the morning of the rekey, and on his ring for thirty years before that. He got the knife blade into the split ring and spread it, and worked the key round the coil and off with the orange tag riding along behind, and laid it in her palm.

She licked her thumb and counted it. "One."

"That tag's about worn through," Ray said. The R.G. and the year in her ballpoint had gone soft as cloth in his pocket.

"So am I."

She put the key in her pocketbook and shut the clasp. "I like to watch money come in." She went out through the glass to the bench in the lobby and sat there with her hat on and the pocketbook on her knees.

When the lobby opened Judy came back and sat down in her own chair, which held her level, and she noticed it and didn't say so. She pushed the butterscotch bowl an inch toward Ellen. Then she wrote a figure on a slip from Sunday's count and turned it round on the desk. From where he sat Ray could read it the wrong way up, and he read it.

"That puts you at the line, with nothing to spare."

Ellen signed the wire form standing. Judy's pen stopped over the line where the hold would go, and she looked at Ellen once, and didn't offer it again.

The wire went to Arlene's note, as Ellen had chosen.

After that there was nothing to do but wait.

Ellen stood at the glass wall with her hands folded in front of her and her eyes on the lobby, and in all that time he never saw her shift her weight. Judy opened another file, somebody's furnace loan by the look of it, and worked it page by page, and never once looked at the phone by her elbow, which took some doing. Out past the glass Arlene sat on the bench in her hat and watched the tellers count other people's money, at her ease, a woman who had put a little on a hockey game. Every few minutes the drive-up tube came down its pipe behind the tellers with a thunk, and Ray found he was keeping time by it.

He had nothing to fix. His ring sat in his jacket pocket light by one key, and his hand kept going in after it.

He laid out a no for himself, plainly, since nobody else in the room was going to. Arlene's note part paid, with Ellen's money in it and no bed bought with any of it, and Arlene back on the list behind a woman who had been packed since August. Then a foreclosure sale held out in the lot under the sign, and the man from Concord buying it for the land. And Ray at the edge of the lot with his hands in his pockets, kept out of the bidding by what he'd said in Arlene's kitchen, and meant.

He wanted to take her hand. She was standing, so he left it.

He made no offer.

It had been an hour and a good piece of another, he'd have said, when Judy's phone rang.

"Eighty minutes," Ellen said, without turning from the glass.

Judy picked it up. A man's voice came small out of the receiver, loud enough to reach his chair. "Judy. They're done upstairs. You'll want a pen."

"I have one."

She listened a long time with the pen in her hand and wrote nothing down. Then she said thank you and hung up.

"Approved."

She said the rest to Ellen. "I told them what my brother's boy told me at supper last night. You sent a hundred and fifty people home on the road agent's word and refunded at the counter, and next winter's Open already has a name on it."

Ellen turned from the glass. She pulled out the customer chair beside his, the one Arlene had left, and Ray, who had watched her sign Arlene's paper standing and knew she sat down only once a thing was decided, watched her sit down in it.

\* \* \*

Ray stood three rungs from the top of the ladder with the shop's carton open on the paint shelf, the tape slit, the new K lying in its packing, clear glass bent to the shape of a letter with nothing in it yet. On the drive out he had told her two true sentences. "Shop's man set the transformer this morning. I said I'd finish." The shop's man had also taken out the jumper Arlene's sign man put across the old K the winter it died, and the shop's instruction sheet was folded in Ray's jacket. The disconnect on the pole was open. She had opened it at his word and read the handle twice.

Ellen held the ladder in his gloves. Her eyes were on its feet in the packed snow and on his boots, where a ladder-holder's eyes go, and she did not look up at the work or ask what it was.

He lifted the K out of the carton and leaned out with it to the standoffs, and the ladder steadied under him. He felt it come up through the rails into his shins, her weight going onto the uprights a hair before he needed it there. He seated the glass in its clips and leaned the other way for the far end, and she was there ahead of him again, with no hand of hers anywhere on him, and he knew where she was by the ladder alone. He made up the two ends in their boots, as the sheet said, and gave each one a push to be sure.

Over the hill Arlene would be at The Pines by now in her good hat, her money gone down the wire that afternoon.

He came down with the empty carton under his arm. At the foot of the ladder Ellen pulled her right hand out of his glove, took the front key from her vest, and held out the key to him bare-handed.

"That's not asking."

"Ray. Will you take it?"

He took it. He got his thumbnail into the split ring and worked her key round the coil and onto it, into the gap Arlene's had left that morning, and it hung there with the others.

"Throw her, Mrs. Daly."

He waited to be corrected. She went to the pole and threw the disconnect.

The transformer took hold with a hum, and the whole sign came up red against the low sun, and the new K filled from one end to the other, and the red of it came down the pole onto the snow at their boots.

"How's she read?"

Ellen looked both ways down Kettle Road, which was empty both ways, and walked out into the middle of it, and stood there, and looked up.

"Both words."
