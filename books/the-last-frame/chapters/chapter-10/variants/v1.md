# Chapter 10: Both Words

Ray had the pocketknife open and a score pencil turning against the blade, and the shavings came off in thin cedar curls that he caught in his palm before they could reach her counter's worn front edge. Out in the lot Ellen's car sat under Saturday's snow, which was why he had driven out to the camp before light and brought her up in the truck.

Down on Main Street Danny would have the store open an hour by now. He had opened it alone every morning since the one after the release, when he signed the store papers with Ray's paper lying on the counter beside them, and Ray had not counted that drawer since. Nobody had asked him back behind it, either, so he was sharpening pencils at a woman's counter. Thirty years he had sharpened the score pencils for this house before a final, and not one had ever broken in a tenth box, and he did not see why he should let standards slip on the day it counted. He tried a point on his thumb.

The handheld clicked. Hollis had one of hers for the derby weekend.

"Kettle, Hollis. Four inches of slush on top of the ice and water up through every hole. Derby's Sunday is off. Your ten still held?"

"All ten," Ellen said.

"I'll be up with the clipboard."

So the derby had lost its Sunday to the slush, and the house had nobody's day in it but Linda's.

Her phone rang on the counter. She looked at the screen, and then she laid the phone face up between the two of them and touched the speaker before she said hello.

"Am I on speaker?"

"Ray is here. Shall I take you off?"

"Leave him on. Slept four hours in the hospital garage with the seat back. Don't. I'm fine. It's a good garage. I did the subtraction. Without the dollars. It's harder than it sounds."

Ellen waited.

"That money's why I sleep. If it goes in, I'm your bad winter." The speaker hissed. "Yes. And in April, in person, you tell me about the night you came home off a midnight and sat in the car in the driveway till I left for school. All of it. I'll ask questions."

Ray knew the night; she had told it to him in the pit with a pin in her hands, and he had carried it to her counter in the dark and used it on her. The car was new to him, and the driveway, and a girl going out past it to school. Ellen's hand came down flat on the counter beside the phone and stayed there.

"Yes," Ellen said. It came half a beat late, and lower than her counter voice, and he heard both.

"There you are," Kate said. "And call me first next time." A beat. "Like this."

"I'd have asked for less," Ray said, before he could stop himself.

"You weren't asked." Another beat. "I'm coming up in April to see what I paid for."

She was gone.

Ellen dialed Judy, still on speaker, and Judy answered over a percolator.

"My tip-ups are under four inches of slush, so you may as well talk to me about money."

"It's about the paydown."

"Your own money lowers the line slowly. It takes a good many of your dollars to buy down one of the winter's."

"When does the committee sit?"

"Ten tomorrow. They don't vote on a winter till it's over, and they vote on money that's arrived. Bring it in by nine and I'll hold it here till they vote."

"I'll call you back," Ellen said, and hung up.

She was taking the hold, then. It was the safe thing, and he had offered his name at Judy's once already and been told where she wanted him, and the knife stopped on a pencil half turned.

She dialed Kate. Kate picked up on the first ring.

"Judy will hold it until the committee votes. I want it sent to Arlene's note in the morning, before they vote. If they say no, none of it comes back."

"You called me first," Kate said. "It's yours, Mom. Send it."

She dialed Judy back, and Ray sat with the knife still and listened to her choose it, and said nothing, which was some work.

"Send it to Arlene's note at nine. If they say no, she's that much paid whatever happens to me."

"Then it goes at nine. I can't promise you ten."

\* \* \*

The trophy case stood across the house from the counter, with every lane between warming up for Linda's final. Ray keyed the handheld. He could have walked it.

"Kettle, Gagnon. Request."

Across the house Ellen lifted her radio. "Gagnon, say request."

"Request you come hold a key."

She came, round the Memorial cup she had set on the scorer's table at five and six to stand for the final.

He took the small brass key off his ring, six winters on it and never once used, and fitted it to the padlock on locker fourteen, and set her fingers on it, and put his hand over hers. The white seam on his finger lay across her knuckles. The padlock was cold and stiff, and they turned it together, and it held, and then it gave with a small dry click he felt in his hand before he heard it.

Linda's last league sheet lay folded on the shelf. He opened it and held it where Ellen could read it, and said nothing: three strings in her pencil, pressed hard enough to dent the paper, and in the sixth box of her second string a three, written down as carefully as every good box around it. He laid it back. Under it was the ball, small and smooth from her hand and heavier than it looked, and cold clear through from the wall. He held it against his jacket to take the cold off, and left the locker door standing open, and the padlock hanging open on its hasp.

Denise went by behind them with her bag, bowling today. "Don't you go bowling it sentimental on me."

He let her go by without an answer, his or anybody's.

Ellen stayed at the open locker, standing, and he could hear her keeping her counter voice level. "Next year. Will you put her name on it again? I'm asking."

He went to her gooseneck microphone, and she stepped back from it, and he used it for the first time and said it once, plainly, to the whole house.

"From now on Linda's name goes on this Open every winter, and anybody who enters can win it, because Linda would've entered on day one."

Nobody clapped. On lane two Judy's nephew, anchoring the Legion, stood with a ball halfway up and heard it out.

"That's how a name goes on a cup, honey," Denise told Ellen at the counter. "The man it belongs to says so."

Then three strings went down all ten lanes, and across the house, for the length of one crash, Ellen stood behind the counter with her eyes shut. The Highway crew bowled on ten with plow sand in their cuffs, in off the trucks, on Saturday's envelope, which Dale had laid back on her counter that morning with the flap still stuck down. The Lewiston team, a morning squad, was out of the money by the second string. Gil's four from Pine Ridge, Gil in his good overcoat with a folded flyer in his glove, would finish third. Ellen went over to the fryer, because Denise was bowling. Hollis had the clipboard at the scorer's table, and Ray sat on a settee between strings with nobody to coach.

Denise's tenth box came up on lane six. She tugged her bowling glove tight at the wrist and threw, and the first ball left a good part of the rack. The second left the four horsemen standing behind a deck of wood. She looked at them a while. Then she threw the third low and hard into the wood, and the wood took all four. Hollis marked it and said nothing. Lakeview Diner was done.

Ray stood up on five with Linda's ball. Hollis kept his eyes on the sheet.

"You need all ten."

The first ball went where he sent it and took the head pin and the whole left side, and left four at the back. The second came off his hand a hair late, and he heard it before he saw it; it took two of the four with their own wood and left two standing in the back corners. Denise had her hands flat on the cup. At the fryer Ellen held a basket up out of the oil and did not put it down. Hollis's pencil hung over the box. Ray let the third go as well as he had ever let one go in his life, and it went down the old maple between the two of them so close to each that he waited for the sound, and none came, and the two pins stood.

He took Linda's ball off the return and set it on the house rack between four and five, among the house balls, where anybody could reach it. When he turned, the room was coming up onto its feet, and Denise had both hands on the cup, and Verna was standing with her binder.

"The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins."

\* \* \*

The lot was dark and the house was dark behind the counter lamp, and Ellen had her reading glasses on, the cord looped behind her neck. Ray held the register tape flat under two fingers, since it kept curling back on itself, and read her the envelopes one at a time: the snack bar, the spectators, the Highway crew's envelope with its flap slit at last. He rounded each one, and she wrote down the exact figure off the tape, and they got through it like two men carrying a sofa up a narrow stair, one of them calling the turns. She added the column, and then added it again from the bottom up. Her pencil went down the paper with a scratch he could have picked out of a full house.

"Same number twice," he said.

"That's what twice is for."

She laid the pencil down.

"Nine hundred dollars under."

It was a smaller number than yesterday's and still on the wrong side, and she gave it to him as plainly as she would give out a lane. He had been in and out of Arlene's green ledgers more winters than he could count, looking up what a part had cost the last time.

"Arlene's best winter in those ledgers was twenty-one thousand, near enough, the year the roof got done. And it leaked by April anyway."

"Thirty is the line."

"And Kate said yes."

She took her glasses off and let them drop on the cord, and looked at him across the counter for a while.

"Come back to the pit with me."

It was an order, given pleasantly, and he knew by now that was her frightened voice. He went with her through the pit door past lane ten and along the catwalk, past the backs of the machines with their motors off, to behind seven, and she stopped there. The kickback steel was cold under his hand, and the pit smelled of grease gone cold. Under the masking he could see the lanes running away backward, lit by nothing but her one lamp at the far end of the house.

She stood with her hands at her sides. He waited. He had done a good deal of waiting on this woman, at doors and at counters, and he had come to like it.

"The night of the storm I told you two things and kept the third, because it wasn't in order. It is now."

She took a breath.

"Third. I love you."

He had heard her give out lane numbers in that voice, and refunds, and Dale's hour, and here was the same voice, polite and exact, with this in it. He gave it back to her in the one form she had ever trusted to arrive.

"Third item. You love me."

Her face let go at the corners.

He looked at her the length of one breath. Then he said his own, in his own words.

"I've loved you since about the first night, when you kept me knocking at your door to finish your tape."

He had stood out in the snow and watched her through the door glass press the strip flat with her thumb, the whole length of it, in no hurry on his account.

She reached up and took his reading glasses off his forehead, where he had forgotten them since the tape, and folded them and put them in her vest, and he put his hand to the back of her neck, under her collar, where she was warm, and her hands went inside his open jacket and took hold of his shirt on either side, and he bent his head and kissed her, slowly, because nothing in the building needed him and nobody was coming up the drive and he had the whole night to get it right.

He drew her in, and something caught.

"Hold still," she said, with her face against his jacket. "My cord's in your zipper."

He held still. The cord had gone round the pull of his zipper and down into the teeth, and he got his fingers onto it, which were too big for most things and good for small parts, and worked it back out of the teeth a little at a time, and unwound it once from the pull, and then again, while she stood in the cold of the pit and let him, and watched his hands do it. When it came free he kept hold of it a second longer than he needed, and then let it go and kissed her again, and one of them made a sound, low in the throat, and he could not have sworn which one. He was fairly sure it was him.

Afterward her forehead rested on his jacket, on the stitched name over his pocket, and he could feel her breathing through the canvas. The committee sat in the morning, and he had meant to leave it alone till then.

"And if they say no?"

\* \* \*

Ray left the truck by the town dock and walked up Main Street, with the sidewalk salt cracking under his boots. Danny stood in the door of the store in his apron. Behind him the old kitchen chair nobody sat in had been pulled out from the wall to the near corner of the counter.

"You coming in?" Danny said.

"You asking?"

"I'm asking."

"After the bank."

He went on up the hill, and the salt cracked under his boots a good deal quicker than it had below the store.

Ellen was waiting at the bank's door, standing, with no gloves on.

Judy let them in at the side door before the lobby opened and went off for the wire forms. Her office was glass on two sides, with a framed lake trout over the desk and a bowl of butterscotch on it, and her swivel chair stood a few degrees off plumb. Ray was in it with the screwdriver blade of his pocketknife in the tilt screw before anybody had told him to sit anywhere.

Judy came back with the forms.

"Is that Ray Gagnon on my chair?"

"In it." He gave the screw a last quarter turn. "She leaned."

"Then sit in the one that's yours."

He moved to a customer chair. Ellen stayed standing.

Judy went out again to the wire desk. The door behind them opened, and Arlene came in for her wire in her good gray hat with the pin, her pocketbook on her arm, and took the other customer chair, and left Ellen standing, which suited Ellen.

"Raymond. I'll have my key back while you're sitting."

The 'ninety-six key had been on his ring thirty years and dead since December, and it had not once crossed his mind to take it off. He opened the knife again, got the blade into the split ring and spread it, and worked the key round the coil and off. She took it from him, licked her thumb and counted it.

"One."

"That tag's about worn through," he said. R.G. and 'ninety-six in her ballpoint, gone soft as a dish rag.

"So am I."

She put it in her pocketbook and shut the clasp. "I like to watch money come in," she said, and went out to the lobby bench to wait for hers.

When the lobby opened Judy came back and sat in her chair, which held, and pushed the butterscotch an inch toward Ellen. Then she wrote a figure on a slip from Sunday's count and turned it round on the desk. Ray could read it from his chair, the wrong way round, and he read it.

"That puts you at the line, with nothing to spare."

Ellen signed the wire form standing. Judy's pen stopped over the box for the hold, and stayed a second, and moved on, and she did not offer it again. The wire went to Arlene's note, as Ellen had chosen.

Then there was nothing to do but wait, and Ray had never in his life been any good at it. Ellen stood at the glass wall with her hands folded. Judy worked another file and did not once look at her phone. Out past the glass Arlene sat on the lobby bench with her hat on and her pocketbook in her lap. Every few minutes the drive-up tube thunked through the wall, and he heard every one. He had nothing to fix; the chair was fixed. His ring sat light in his jacket by one key, and he kept finding that out.

A no came to this. A note part paid with Ellen's money in it, and no bed bought. A foreclosure sale. The man from Concord on the lot with a tape measure, at land value. And himself kept off the sidewalk outside it by his own word, given at Arlene's table.

He wanted to take her hand. She was standing, and he left his on his knee. He made her no offer of any kind, and it was the hardest work he had done all winter.

It went an hour and a good piece of another. Then Judy's phone rang.

"Eighty minutes," Ellen said, to the glass.

Judy listened a long time and wrote nothing down. She put the phone back in its cradle.

"Approved."

Then, to Ellen's back: "I told them what my brother's boy told me at supper last night. You sent a hundred and fifty people home on the road agent's word and refunded at the counter, and next winter's Open already has a name on it."

Ellen turned from the glass. She pulled out the customer chair beside his. He had watched her sign Arlene's paper standing up. She sat down in it, and he watched her do it.

\* \* \*

The shop's man had set the transformer that morning and taken out the jumper Arlene's sign man put across the K the winter it went, and now Ray stood on the ladder at the sign face with the shop's carton, the lid taped, and the shop's sheet folded in his jacket. The disconnect on the pole was open. She had opened it on his word and read the handle twice. "Shop's man set the transformer this morning," he had told her. "I said I'd finish." Every word of it true, and he'd had that trick off somebody.

She held the ladder in his gloves. Her eyes were on its feet in the packed snow and on his boots, which was where a ladder-holder kept them, and she did not look up at the work or ask. He felt her through the rails. Every time he leaned out, the ladder steadied under him, and the correction came a hair before he needed it. Arlene would be at The Pines by now in her good hat, her money gone down the wire that afternoon.

He came down with the empty carton. At the foot of the ladder she pulled off his right glove, took the front key from her vest, and held it out to him bare-handed.

"That's not asking."

"Ray. Will you take it?"

He worked it onto his ring, into the gap where Arlene's had hung thirty years until this morning.

"Throw her, Mrs. Daly."

He waited to be corrected. She threw the disconnect.

The transformer took hold with a hum, and the new K filled with red from one end to the other, and the red came down the pole onto the snow at their boots.

"How's she read?"

Ellen looked both ways down Kettle Road, which was empty, and walked out into the middle of it and looked up.

"Both words."
