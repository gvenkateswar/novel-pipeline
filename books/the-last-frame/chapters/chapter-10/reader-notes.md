# Cold read, Chapter 10: "Both Words"

**Warning, above the score:** I'd keep reading, but only because there are maybe two pages left. Chapter 10 settles every open question with a callback, one after another, and once the last one has gone off I don't want anything more from an epilogue. The money climax, the bank committee, takes place on the other end of a phone line between two people we never meet. It was the scene where I stopped caring.

## 1. Pull score: 4 / 10

At the end of this chapter I had almost no pull toward the epilogue. Everything the book promised gets paid, and paid neatly. The one good surprise is Ray losing the final by two pins. Apart from that, every payoff is something the book had told me to expect since Chapter 1, and each one shows up on cue with a closing line attached.

## 2. Where I got bored

- **Scene 1, lines 49–53.** "Your own money lowers the line slowly," Judy said. "It takes a good many of your dollars to buy down one of the winter's." Chapter 5 already told me this, and I didn't really follow it then either. A finale shouldn't be teaching me the loan rules again.
- **Scene 1, lines 43–69.** The phone ping-pong. Judy, "I'll call you back," Kate, Judy again. The emotional decision (Kate's yes) is already made, and the rest is routing instructions for a wire.
- **Scene 2, line 101.** "The Highway crew had lane ten, in off the trucks with plow sand in their cuffs and bowling on the refunded envelope Dale had laid back on her counter that morning with the flap still stuck down. The Lewiston team, a morning squad, was out of the money by the second string…" It's a roll call, and every name on it is there to tick off an earlier plant.
- **Scene 3, lines 121–133.** The count. "He read every figure off them to the cent… she wrote each under the last and added the column down and then up." Then I had to work out for myself that Arlene's best winter was $21,000 against a $30,000 line, which means Ellen beat Arlene's best ever. The book makes me do the arithmetic to get to the feeling.
- **Scene 4, line 219.** "He had time to think a no all the way through, so he did. Her savings would be in a part-paid note, and Arlene would go back on the list. The man from Concord would have the lot at land value." This is where I drifted hardest. It's a recap of the stakes, given during the one wait that was supposed to be tense.
- **Scene 5, line 241.** "The shop's man had also taken out the jumper Arlene's sign man had put across the K the winter it went out. The disconnect on the pole was open." I skimmed this.

## 3. Where I got confused

- **"I did the subtraction. Without the dollars… That money's why I sleep. If it goes in, I'm your bad winter."** I had to read this twice. My best guess is that if the savings go in, Kate becomes her mother's safety net. "Don't." in the middle of the same speech has no clear object either. Don't what? Don't fuss?
- **"Arlene's note."** I had understood that Ellen pays Arlene $250k on March 1 with Judy's loan. I didn't know Arlene was holding a note that savings could be wired onto ahead of time. Chapter 1's "at the note rate" is the only hint I can find. So when Ellen chooses "send" over "hold," I understand that it's generous, but not what it actually does.
- **"Nine hundred dollars under"** on Sunday night, then **"That puts you at the line, with nothing to spare"** on Monday. I take it the savings filled the $900 gap at Judy's exchange rate, but I'm guessing.
- **"the four horsemen."** I can't tell if this is candlepin slang or a specific leave. I assume four pins, but a listener won't know.
- **Linda's last sheet: "in the sixth box of the second string a three, written down as carefully as the good boxes."** I can tell this is supposed to mean something (she wrote her bad box down honestly?), but nobody says it and Ray doesn't react. It went past me.
- **The old kitchen chair** pulled out at the store. Chapter 5 says nobody ever sits in it, and the book never says whose it was. Lucien's? I could see it was a gesture but not what it meant.
- **"Throw her, Mrs. Daly."** Her not correcting him reads like the book's quiet proposal. But Daly is her name, not his, and in ten chapters the book has never told me whether she's divorced or widowed, or who Kate's father is. "Ms." was a running joke with nothing under it.
- **"Every word of that was true, and he had learned the trick off her."** Which trick? I suppose it's telling the true half (from Chapter 4), but it's the narrator nudging me, not something I worked out.

## 4. Where I stopped believing

- **The committee approval.** A credit union committee, which Judy warned "has never met you," says yes because Judy tells them what her nephew said at supper: "You sent a hundred and fifty people home on the road agent's word and refunded at the counter, and next winter's Open already has a name on it." The money plot has run for nine chapters, and it ends on a character reference passed along by phone. It's convenient, and it happens offstage. Ellen does nothing in the scene except wait.
- **The final comes down to Ray's last box with Linda's ball, against Denise, the woman who's wanted the cup longest.** It fits the genre, but it's very stage-managed: the two people with the most riding on Linda's name meet in the last frame. I let it go because Ray loses, and that I didn't see coming.
- **Kate says "Send it" without a beat.** Kate is the one person in the book who pushes back on Ellen, and here the cost of choosing send over hold disappears because she approves it at once. Ellen's actual choice (Arlene paid whatever happens) costs her nothing on the page. The safer option was to let Judy hold the money. The page does shut that option down (Kate says send, Ellen wants Arlene covered), but because nobody resists, no pressure builds.
- **Kate sleeping "in the hospital garage with the seat back."** It mirrors Ellen in the car in the driveway too exactly. It reads as the author lining things up, not as Kate.
- **Ray cutting in on a speakerphone call between a mother and the daughter he's never met: "I'd have asked for less."** I didn't buy that the Ray of nine chapters, who "offered nothing," says that. Kate's answer saves it.
- **The new K goes in the same day as the bank approval, with the shop's man setting the transformer "this morning" while Ray was at the bank.** It works on the timeline, but it's tidy.

## 5. Where it sounded like a machine

- **The callback parade.** In this one chapter I counted: "That's not asking" (Ch 1), "Mrs. Daly" uncorrected (Ch 1/4), the key on the ring (Ch 1/7/8), Arlene's orange tag (Ch 1), "I'm asking" (Ch 5), the third item (Ch 3), the readback (Ch 4/9), the kitchen chair (Ch 5), Ellen finally sitting (Ch 7–9), his gloves on her (Ch 6), "Kettle Maintenance" radio style (Ch 4), the K (Ch 1). Every prop gets taken out of the drawer and labelled. By the bank scene I was waiting for the next one instead of watching people.
- **Every scene ends on a neat line:** "I can't promise you ten." / "The secretary has Lakeview Diner by two pins." / "And if they say no?" / "Ray watched her do it." / "Both words."
- **Lines inside scenes that also land like closers:** "That's how a name goes on a cup, honey. The man it belongs to says so." / "So am I." / "Like this." / "I'm coming up in April to see what I paid for." / "Then sit in the one that's yours."
- **Everyone talks in the same dry one-liner register.** Arlene, Judy, Kate, Denise and Hollis all deliver short, flat, wry finishers. Kate's three-item style mirrors Ellen's on purpose, but Judy's "Then sit in the one that's yours" and Arlene's "So am I" come from the same machine.
- **The "said nothing" tic:** "He said nothing about it," "Hollis marked it and said nothing," "Ray heard her choose it, and offered nothing," "added nothing," "noticed, and said nothing," "made no offer." In this book, silence is the main way of having a feeling.
- **Stock romance phrasing:** "I've loved you since about the first night." / "one of them made a low sound, and he was fairly sure it was him." / "her face let go at the corners."
- **The narrator explaining after the gesture:** "Ray heard her choose it, and offered nothing." / "She was taking the hold, then." / "because she had always given him every number whole, and he meant to give her this the same."

## 6. What I think is going on

Nothing is hidden any more. Ellen clears the line on her savings, Arlene gets her bed, Ray picks Linda's name and loses her cup to Denise, Danny takes the store and asks his father back, and the K is lit. My prediction for the epilogue is Kate's April visit: she meets Ray and Ellen tells her the driveway story, or else it jumps to next winter's Open with Ray back on the clipboard and Ellen bowling a passable box. The uncorrected "Mrs." is setting up a marriage or something close to it. I expect the last image to be the two of them in his truck in his dooryard after supper with the heater on and the Bruins playing, which would turn his four empty hours into hers. I'd bet on that more than on a wedding.

## 7. The listener pass

- **Radio call signs:** "Kettle, Hollis." / "Kettle, Gagnon. Request." / "Gagnon, say request." Read aloud, a listener hears names with nothing to show who is talking to whom. It's worst at the start of the chapter, before Hollis has been placed in the scene.
- **Kate's speakerphone block (line 23):** "Slept four hours in the hospital garage with the seat back. Don't. I'm fine. It's a good garage. I did the subtraction. Without the dollars." A TTS voice gives every fragment the same weight, and "Don't." comes out as nonsense.
- **The phone relay (lines 43–69):** "I'll call you back." Then "Kate picked up on the first ring." Then "Ellen went back to Judy." A listener loses track of who is on the line. There are three calls in about a minute, and "Judy said" is the only anchor.
- **The count (lines 123–133):** "Same number twice." / "That's what twice is for. Nine hundred dollars under." / "Arlene's best winter since Normand… twenty-one thousand…" / "Thirty is the line." / "And Kate said yes." These lines are unattributed and alternate speakers. Aloud, I couldn't tell who said "Thirty is the line."
- **The final, ball by ball:** "took the head pin and most of the left side… two more went down, and three stood… took the front one of the three." A listener has to keep a running pin count with no total given, and "the four horsemen" isn't explained.
- **Two tableau paragraphs in a row with the same shape:** "Denise had her hands flat on the cup. Gil stood… The ball came back up the return." / "Across the house Ellen had the fryer basket stopped… Down on ten the Highway crew… on two the Ostrowski boy had his cap off. Hollis's pencil waited." Aloud this is a roll call, read twice.
- **The sign wiring (line 241):** transformer, jumper, disconnect, "read the handle twice." My eyes could skip it. A voice has to read every word.
- **What works aloud:** "Third. I love you." / "Third item. You love me." Also the Danny exchange ("You coming in?" "You asking?" "I'm asking.") and "Both words."

## 8. The romance

Yes, in places. "Hold still," she said, into his collar. "My cord's in your zipper." This made me smile. It's the first time in the book the two of them are clumsy together, and "her forehead rested near enough his chin that he felt her laugh once, with no sound in it" is real. "Request you come hold a key" is charming. Turning the padlock together with "the white seam along his finger" across her knuckles is earned, because I'd been following that scar since Chapter 1. "Throw her, Mrs. Daly." / "He waited to be corrected. She threw the disconnect." is the best romantic beat in the chapter, because nobody explains it.

The confession works mechanically ("Third item. You love me"), but I saw it coming in Chapter 3. "I've loved you since about the first night" is the line any romance hero says. I didn't laugh out loud anywhere in this chapter. The closest I came was "Is that Ray Gagnon on my chair?" / "In it."

## 9. What I want to know next

Very little, and that's the main finding. The only real question I have left is what happens when Kate comes up in April, meets Ray, and asks her questions. A smaller one: what was Ellen's marriage, and who is Kate's father? The book has never said, and the "Ms./Mrs." joke depends on it. I don't need anything else. The romance, the money, Linda's name and the sign are all closed, so an epilogue will just be a victory lap unless it takes on Kate.

### Did Kate's price surprise me?

No. Chapter 5 planted "the car-in-the-driveway voice," and Chapter 9 ended on "I'm going to want something for it." I guessed it would be the driveway story or "you'll let me meet him," and it was the first. It's the right price, and "All of it. I'll ask questions" is well put. But it costs Ellen something that happens after the book ends. On the page she pays nothing except a "Yes" that comes "a half beat late." The part that surprised me was Kate sleeping in her car in the garage, and that read as engineered.

### Did the ending pay what the book promised, and what did it cost?

- **The romance:** paid. The confession, the kiss, and the key given back by asking. Cost: nothing new in this chapter. The cost was all paid back in Chapter 7–8.
- **The money:** paid, but badly. Ellen clears it on her savings ("the rest" she swore to Kate), she's left with "one bad winter," and the committee says yes offstage. The numbers were never clear enough for the line to feel like a ticking clock, so the approval landed as relief at best. Her actual cost is the cushion, and that's a figure, not a scene.
- **Linda's name:** the best payoff in the book. Ray says it himself over her PA, bowls Linda's ball, needs all ten, leaves two, and puts the ball on the house rack "where anybody could reach it." Denise gets the cup. This cost him something on the page, and I felt it.
- **The sign:** paid, with "Both words." It's the right final image, and it was a sure thing from page one.
- **What else it cost:** Ray's coaching this season, his claim on the building, Arlene's key after thirty years, Arlene's bed delayed until the money came in, and Ellen's last line of savings. The only costs I felt were Ray losing the final and Arlene's key.

### Would I recommend the book, and to whom?

Only to a narrow reader. I'd recommend it to someone who wants a slow, very low-heat later-life romance with real New England texture, who enjoys procedure (pinsetter repair, plow calls, loan conditions) for its own sake, and who reads for a well-made sentence more than for momentum. I would not recommend it to a general romance reader or anyone who reads on audio. The contract and loan mechanics in the middle of the book (Ch 4–6 and the second half of Ch 8) would lose them, and they'd be right to stop.

### If I would have stopped reading

Not in Chapter 10, because I was too close to the end. The bank wait (line 219, "He had time to think a no all the way through, so he did") is where I'd have started skimming. In the book as a whole, the most likely point where I'd have put it down for good is the opening of Chapter 6: "It's a release. One clause, and the three of us sign it, and that backup paper's done with." That's the third legal-paper scene in a row, and the leads aren't together for it. The next most likely point is the opening of Chapter 4: "My word had two parts, and you heard one."

### What I'd still want from an epilogue

- Kate in April, meeting Ray, and the driveway story being told. That's the only promise still open, and the only scene I'd actually turn the page for.
- Ray's four hours after supper, filled. Show it without saying it.
- One Thursday that doesn't lose money.
- Ellen throwing one decent ball, or deciding not to bother.
- What I don't want: another round of callbacks (keys, gloves, "Ms.", readbacks). Those are used up.
