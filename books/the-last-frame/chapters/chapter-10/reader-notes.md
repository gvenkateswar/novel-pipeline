# Reader Notes: Chapter Ten, "Ice-Out"

## Where I got bored

- The sign install: "The electrician from the sign shop in Laconia, in a hard hat, opened the disconnect at the foot of the pole and went up, and Ellen held the ladder with both hands. Ray took the old transformer when it came down and handed the new one up. The electrician made up the connections and climbed down." I already knew how this would end, and this is a procedure being walked through just to reach the last line.
- The ice-out pool paragraph. "missed by two days, twenty-one hours, and ten minutes. She had told him the margin twice" is a cute bit, but it's the fourth time the book has used the joke that Ellen measures everything. The coda is already full of callbacks, and this one wore on me.

## Where I got confused

- "Funded... Two-eleven." Then the lawyer hands Arlene "the wire confirmation," and Arlene counts it with her thumb and says "One. That's all of it." What is she counting? I eventually took it as a callback to Arlene counting tape rolls, but I had to stop and work it out.
- Arlene's key: "Raymond brought it back yesterday... He said it was a loan." A loan of a key that opens nothing, to the woman who gave it to him? I guess it echoes the ledger loan, but I'm not sure what Ray means by it or why it matters.
- Arlene's "why me" answer ("You asked me the name of my boiler man") doesn't match what she told Ray in Ch 8 ("That one raised the price her first winter and lost two teams for it"). In Ch 8 the reason was something that happened after the sale. Here it's something that happened before. Maybe both are true, but the book doesn't connect them.
- "February second. 'They split.'" Took me a second to place it as the night of the league meeting.

## Where I stopped believing

- The "cash on hand" problem. Ellen beats both numbers in Ch 9, and then the book makes up a last-minute technicality ("the underwriter reads 'on hand' as money he can see") and fixes it with one question and a six-minute phone call. It reads like an obstacle added because the chapter needed one, not a real risk. Ellen, who has done this arithmetic three times standing up since Ch 3, never thought about when the deposit would land? That's the one blind spot this character shouldn't have.
- "He wants to see the safe from now on." Bankers don't talk like that. It's there to make a neat phrase.

## Where it sounded like AI

- The chapter goes through the book's callbacks like a checklist: butterscotch bowl, lake trout weight, embosser hinge now silent, Voyons donc, "Is that transformer certified?", the order joke, the knee, "Mrs. Daly" with no correction, lane 7 as the first machine. Each one is fine. Together they feel like loose ends being ticked off a list.
- Ellen's declaration recaps the plot: "If I had taken that check, you would always have wondered whether it was you I wanted or the fifty-five thousand dollars. You told me you would have known it was you. I believe you." I read Ch 7. I don't need the summary.
- "The town had called the place Ettle's for seven years." This drops into the middle of the action to explain the punchline before it happens.
- "She didn't soften it." / "Every figure on it was correct." Flat narrator stamps.

## Best moment

Ray's declaration:

> "Tuesdays I want out front. Bowling, with the team. Somebody else can listen to the tables. I'm sixty-one. I eat supper at a quarter past five, and I'm going to fix the hinges on anything you own, and write each one on the card. I love you."

It's specific, it's all logistics until the last three words, and only Ray would say it. The tightened settee bolts and rail before it set it up perfectly. A close second: "He told her he was spoken for, and heard her type it."

## What I want to know next

Nothing, and that's right for a last chapter. The only thing that still pulls at me is whether Ellen ever talks to Ray about Pine Ridge. In Ch 8 she worried she would "never know how much of the twelve-fifty had come back on its own." The book never comes back to that.

## Verdict

A warm, satisfying close that ties things off a little too neatly. The pit scene and the coda work. The bank scene is the weakest part. It invents a problem and then removes it.

## Questions

**Does the ending satisfy?** Yes. She keeps the building, he gives up the letter on his own, and the book doesn't throw a wedding at me (it gives me the wedding row on the pool sheet instead, which is better). The Judy scene adds tension nobody asked for, but the payoff holds.

**Did Ellen's declaration land?** Half of it. "She had said this once already, to her own windshield" and "It was you first" land hard, because the Ch 7 car scene earned them. Then she explains it all again, and it turns into a report. Ray's shorter declaration lands better than hers. Maybe that's in character for her (getting the order right), but I felt the length.

**Is anything left dangling that shouldn't be?** The Pine Ridge question (Ellen never finds out from Ray, and her worry about whether the twelve-fifty came back on its own is never answered). Arlene's two different reasons for picking Ellen. Ray's "loan" of the dead key. Everything else is closed.

**Does the last line work?** Yes. "Both words." is earned, because the dead K has been on the page since Ch 1 and the transformer setup was planted in Ch 5. It's literal, it's in Ellen's clipped voice, and it means Kettle Lanes is whole. It's a bit tidy, but it's the right kind of tidy for this book. The "Ettle's for seven years" line just before it weakens it by explaining the joke first.
