# Comparable Books — Angle 1: The Anniversary Rite (South Asian diaspora ghost horror)

*Source: the 2- and 3-star reviews Amazon shows for each book (sorted by "most helpful"), collected October 26, 2026 through the author's browser. 59 critical reviews read in total. Reviews are summarized in my own words, not copied, because they are other people's writing.*

## What readers loved
- **Atmosphere and the house.** The old ancestral house is the part readers praise most in *Japanese Gothic* and *Mexican Gothic*.
- **Folklore they hadn't met before.** Several readers say learning about hungry ghosts (*Bat Eater*) or rakshasa and ghoul myths (*The Devourers*) was the best part.
- **A tie to real history.** The 1877 samurai timeline in *Japanese Gothic* and the Mughal-era India in *The Devourers* drew readers in.

## What disappointed them (ranked by how often it came up)

1. **Slow first half.** This is the biggest complaint by far, raised for all four books. Readers say the first half to two-thirds drags, nothing happens, and they nearly quit before the ending. One *Bat Eater* reviewer said the book would have earned a higher rating if it were shorter.
2. **Not scary.** Readers expected horror and got a sad family drama with mild ghosts. This came up for *Japanese Gothic*, *Mexican Gothic*, and *Bat Eater*.
3. **Confusing second half and ending.** Readers lost track of what was real. Dual timelines, portals, and big twists left them unsure what happened. This came up for *Japanese Gothic* (several reviewers), *Mexican Gothic*, and *The Devourers*.
4. **Overwritten prose.** Readers called out repeated comparisons, piled-up metaphors, and "flowery" writing. One counted how often "as if" appeared. This came up for *Japanese Gothic*, *The Devourers*, and *Bat Eater*.
5. **A protagonist they couldn't connect with.** Readers found side characters more interesting than the lead, or the lead flat. This came up for *Japanese Gothic*, *Bat Eater*, and *Mexican Gothic*.
6. **The theme felt preachy.** Several *Bat Eater* reviewers felt the racism theme was one-note. One *Devourers* reviewer called that book self-important.
7. **Unflagged disturbing content.** Animal cruelty (*Japanese Gothic*), sexual violence (*The Devourers*), and incest and cannibalism (*Mexican Gothic*) made readers quit. They wanted warnings.
8. **The culture felt like a backdrop.** One *Mexican Gothic* reviewer said the book didn't feel Mexican at all. Readers buy these books for the culture and notice when it's only scenery.

## The opening: what a new book could do

1. **Use the novella length as a selling point.** The top complaint is the slow first half. At 30,000 words, the ritual and the first sign that something is wrong should arrive in chapter 1. The blurb can promise a tight, one-sitting read.
2. **Make it actually frightening.** Keep the grief, but give the horror real scenes of fear, not only metaphor. Readers felt cheated when the "horror" was mostly sadness.
3. **Let the ritual drive the plot.** The steps of the shraddha (the rice balls offered to the dead, the crows, the rules of who may and may not perform it) should be the mechanics of the haunting. Culture that works inside the plot answers the "only a backdrop" complaint.
4. **One clear point of view, with rules the reader can follow.** Avoid a second timeline and avoid a twist that needs explaining. Readers forgave mystery but not confusion.
5. **Plain, controlled prose.** Fewer comparisons and metaphors. The forbidden-phrases list in the story bible should include "as if" overuse.
6. **Show the diaspora theme through events, not lectures.** Readers pushed back when a book explained its theme. Let the nurse's estrangement come out in what she does.
7. **Put content notes in the front matter.**

## Data limits
- Amazon only shows a handful of critical reviews per book, sorted by "helpful." That is a fair sample of what bothers readers, but not a statistical one.
- *The Devourers* had only 6 two-star reviews shown and *Japanese Gothic* only 3.
