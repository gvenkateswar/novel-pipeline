## What readers loved

1. **Banter and humor inside the danger.** Call Me Anytime's 5-star reviewers single out the phone-call banter as very funny, cute and fast. This is the strongest positive signal for a protection plot.
2. **Found family and emotional payoff.** Savage Truce's 5-star reviewers say it made them laugh and cry and matched the author's earlier series, with strong found-family. Series-opener readers want a cast they will come back for.
3. **Opening chapters that grab.** Even Lights Out's critics say the first chapters were strong. Readers reward an early hook.
4. **Heat is not the problem.** Per the review notes, nobody on this shelf complains that heat is present. Lights Out 3-star readers often rate spice 4 of 5.
5. **Writing quality when the promise matches the book.** Pen Pal readers who expected a mystery liked the writing. A reliable, comfortable read (Peaks and Promises, near-zero criticism) holds readers.

Do not lose: the humor and chemistry, a strong opening, a cast worth a series, frank heat.

## What disappointed them

Ranked by how often the complaint appears across the notes. A complaint is "recurring" only where three or more different reviewers raised it.

1. **The heroine makes senseless choices in danger ("too stupid to live").** Raised by 8 or more reviewers on Call Me Anytime alone, who describe a childlike heroine with no common sense. Recurring, but on one book in the notes. Other books' notes do not mention it.
2. **Second-half collapse: the suspense fizzles or turns absurd.** Seven reviewers on Call Me Anytime say a funny first half gives way to a thin or silly plot. "Many" Lights Out reviewers say strong early chapters lost them (several DNF'd). Many Still Beating reviewers say the dark premise lasts a few chapters and then becomes slow, repetitive and predictable. Recurring on three books.
3. **Heat replaces plot.** Many Lights Out reviewers call it smut-first with thin characters and little story. Five Call Me Anytime reviewers say the leads mostly want each other while little happens and the suspense arrives late and thin. Recurring on two books.
4. **Marketing oversold darkness or a romance payoff.** Many Lights Out reviewers say it was not as dark as promised. Pen Pal's dominant complaint (hundreds of reviews) is that the blurb sold a steamy prison pen-pal romance and the book is a twist mystery with no conventional HEA. Many Still Beating reviewers say the BookTok hype did not match the book. Recurring on three books.
5. **Rushed or amateurish prose and thin characters.** "Several" Still Beating reviewers describe fan-fiction-feeling prose. Several Savage Truce fans felt it read like a different author. Exact counts were not given, so I treat this as likely recurring, not confirmed.
6. **Euphemistic sex vocabulary.** Reported in the Savage Truce cluster (counts not given). One book only, so not proven recurring. It is a useful low-cost note given the "frank, sensory" brief.
7. **Heroine's body size mentioned so often it became the book.** Savage Truce only, several readers. One book, so not shown as recurring.
8. **Insta-love and "missing depth" at short length.** Three Call Me Anytime reviewers. This meets the three-reviewer threshold but on a single book, and the length of that book was not collected, so it does not show how readers judge a 30,000-word book.
9. **No trigger warnings for heavy content.** Several Still Beating reviewers. Single book.
10. **Convoluted timeline (Pen Pal), damaged print copies (Lights Out).** Not relevant to A3.

Not enough evidence: Peaks and Promises has only two mild 3-star reviews, so it says nothing about complaints.

## The opening

Each item traces to a complaint above.

1. **Write a competent heroine.** Trace: complaint 1. Beth (a ferry deckhand) can read weather, boats and the island, and her risky choices should have reasons the reader can see. Fear and mistakes are fine; childlike choices are the documented problem.
2. **Fix the suspense, so the second half is the strongest part.** Trace: complaint 2. Use the ten-day ferry deadline, a closed cast, and a leak suspect from chapter 1, with each chapter tightening the circle. Resolve the plot with a payoff set up early, not an absurd turn.
3. **Make every sex scene move the plot as well as the relationship.** Trace: complaint 3. Tie each scene to a trust decision about the possible leak, so heat and danger carry the same question. This matches the brief's rule that each scene changes the relationship.
4. **Write a blurb that matches the book.** Trace: complaint 4. Say "open-door romantic suspense, HEA, series opener". Keep packaging PG-13, but do not promise more darkness or a different ending than the book delivers. Add content notes in the book's front matter if the plot includes violence.
5. **Use frank, specific language in the love scenes and keep the heroine's description varied.** Trace: complaints 6 and 7. These come from one book each, so treat as low-cost insurance, not a proven gap.

## Length verdict

The evidence does not show that a 30,000-word open-door protection-plot opener works on this shelf. None of the six comps is known to be short, and the page counts were not collected, so I cannot say how readers react to length on these books. The only length signal is three Call Me Anytime reviewers who say insta-love and short length left the book missing depth. That is thin, but it matches the earlier scan finding that no short open-door romantic suspense book charts.

What the evidence does show is that the "missing depth" complaint comes bundled with others: thin plot after a strong start, banter without incident, and heat standing in for story. To avoid it, a 30,000-word book should:

- Spend its words on incident, not on repeated wanting. Every chapter should advance the danger or the leak question, not only the attraction.
- Compress time, with the ten-day deadline stated early, so a fast romance reads as earned by pressure.
- Build the relationship through choices under threat, not insta-love declarations.
- Keep the cast small (about 5 to 6 named characters) and make each one count as suspect or ally.
- Close the main plot and the HEA within the book, and keep the book-2 hook to a single loose thread. Pen Pal shows what happens when readers feel the ending did not deliver.
- Price and describe it as a novella, so length is expected and not discovered.
