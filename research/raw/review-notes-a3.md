## What the Romantic Suspense top 50 looked like on October 27

The shelf is split three ways: traditionally published thrillers (Mary Burton, Freida McFadden, Karin Slaughter, Rhys Bowen, Colleen Hoover, Lisa Scottoline), dark romance cross-listing (Corrupt, Hunting Adeline, The Ritual, Den of Vipers, several mafia titles), and a smaller band of indie romantic suspense proper (Michelle Heard, Max Monroe, Navessa Allen, Kendra Elliot and Melinda Leigh under Montlake, A.J. Rivers). Pure protection-plot romantic suspense by indie authors is a minority of the list.

## What the critical reviews say (grouped, own words)

**Savage Truce.** Fifteen critical reviews. Clusters: (1) the heroine's body size is mentioned so often it became the whole book for several readers; (2) euphemistic sex-scene vocabulary ("manhood") annoyed readers who wanted frank language; (3) long-time fans felt it read like a different author and that the ending was building to something that didn't arrive; (4) a few are mild 3.5-star "good opener for a new series" notes. 5-star reviewers: made them laugh and cry, as good as the author's earlier mafia series, strong found-family.

**Call Me Anytime.** Forty-four critical reviews, the most useful set for a protection plot. Clusters: (1) the heroine is "too stupid to live", acts like a child, and makes no-common-sense choices in the danger beats (eight or more reviewers); (2) funny first half, then the plot fizzles or turns absurd in the second half (seven); (3) the leads spend most of the book wanting each other with little happening, and the suspense arrives late and thin (five); (4) insta-love and a short length that left it "missing depth" (three). 5-star reviewers: the phone-call banter is very funny, cute, fast.

**Lights Out.** Hundreds of critical reviews on a BookTok hit. Clusters: (1) not as dark as the marketing promised (many); (2) smut-first with thin characters and little plot (many); (3) strong opening chapters, then it lost them and several DNF'd (many); (4) several are about damaged print copies, not the story. 3-star reviewers often rate spice 4 of 5 and story 2 or 3 of 5.

**Pen Pal.** Hundreds of critical reviews with one dominant complaint: the blurb sold a steamy prison pen-pal romance and the book is a mystery with a twist ending and no conventional HEA. Readers felt baited. Secondary: convoluted timeline. Reviewers who went in expecting a mystery liked the writing.

**Still Beating.** Clusters: (1) the dark captivity premise lasts a few chapters and then the book becomes a slow, repetitive, predictable romance (many); (2) rushed, fan-fiction-feeling prose (several); (3) no trigger warnings for heavy content (several); (4) BookTok hype did not match the book.

**Peaks and Promises.** Two 3-star reviews, both mild: enjoyed it, but not the author's best. Nothing structural.

## What this means for A3

- Heat 3–4 is the norm on this shelf and nobody on it complains about heat being present. Complaints are about heat replacing plot (Lights Out, Still Beating) and about euphemism (Savage Truce). Frank language with a real plot is the sweet spot the brief already describes.
- The single most-repeated complaint across the protection-plot comps is the heroine making stupid choices in danger. A competent heroine is the cheapest differentiator available.
- Second-half collapse is the other big one: the suspense is set up and then thinned out or resolved absurdly. A fixed deadline and a suspect among the cast (as in the A3 premise) address this directly.
- Blurb honesty matters more here than in any other set: Pen Pal and Lights Out were punished for marketing that oversold darkness or heat. The brief's PG-13 packaging must still describe the book accurately.
- "Missing depth" at short length is a live risk (Call Me Anytime). The novella has to earn its length with incident, not banter.
