# Chapter 1 — Night Freight

**Day 0 to Day 1 — Thursday to Friday**

Beth checked the propane truck first, as she always did, because it was the one that could kill everybody. Her gloves stayed in her float-coat pocket; neoprene told her nothing about a binder. Her bare hands told her everything: the handle laid down along the chain and pinned, the links so tight they didn't ring when she rapped them. Good. The plumber's van behind it had a chain lying slack at the forward D-ring. She took up a quarter turn on the ratchet, felt it seat, tugged the chain where it met the deck plate, and moved on.

Sleet ticked off her hood and went orange as it fell through the floods over the ramp. Under her XtraTufs the diesel idled at nine hundred, a thrum that came up through the steel into her feet.

Ned leaned out of the wheelhouse wing. "Beth. I can work it short. Go on home."

"Kenny's in bed with a bucket," she called up.

Nothing came back. She hadn't worked a Thursday night since last winter, when Ned gave the night runs to Kenny because Kenny wanted the night money. Tonight Kenny was throwing up in his mother's bathroom, and the district didn't sail a car deck with nobody on it.

The dealer's reefer sat nose to the ramp in the other lane, the biggest thing aboard. She worked the front binders. Then she put her palm flat on the box, low, beside the door seal, out of habit.

The seal wasn't cold.

Not warm either. Just November, the same as the bulwark and her own hand. She looked up at the Thermo King on the nose of the trailer. The display was dark, not cycling, not blinking a setpoint. Switched off. And no sound. Six years of Thursdays that unit had run so loud you raised your voice over it, because live product rode continuous, and a dealer who let his box die with lobsters in it lost money by the pound.

She took her hand back and went aft along the trailer to the rear binders.

The rear doors were open.

A man in a black hood under an oilskin stood at the doors with a hand truck, lifting wax-box cartons up to the driver, who stacked them inside. The hood came forward past the man's face. The driver was heavy and quiet and working fast. He shoved the stack to even it, the top carton caught on the door frame, and its lid sprang.

Under the lid, live lobsters in wet rockweed and newspaper, claws banded, moving slow in the cold. Under them, where the rockweed had slid, plastic. Vacuum-sealed, squared off like bricks. Blue showing through.

The driver's hand went still on the carton. The face in the hood didn't move at all.

"Lid's sprung," Beth said. "You'll lose your count."

She reached past the driver and pressed the lid down with the heel of her hand until it seated. She pulled the load strap over the stack and cinched it, counting cartons under her hand. Fourteen. Then she bent to the rear chain, laid the binder handle down, pinned it, and slapped the box twice, the same sign-off every truck got.

She walked on aft, toward the cars at the stern.

The deck sat lower as it went. At the stern the chain hung across the transom gate, and the black water slopped four feet below it, close enough to hear. The ring buoy sat in its bracket on the port bulwark with its line in the clips beneath. A spare binder had worked out of the bin beside it and lay on the deck plate. She bent and stowed it. A loose binder rolled, and a rolling binder broke ankles.

She didn't look back. She crossed the stern and came forward along the deckhouse wall at the speed she always walked, which was not fast. At the deckhouse door she passed the ramp-light switch without touching it. Then the outside ladder. The rungs were iced, and she counted them going up. Eleven. There had always been eleven.

In the wheelhouse she stood at the window with sleet running off her hood and looked down.

The man in the hood was walking off the boat. He pushed his empty hand truck up the transfer bridge against the last two cars coming on, and he favored his left leg, a hitch at every step, small and regular as a bad wheel. He didn't look back either.

Then the ramp started coming up.

---

Ned had the wheel in one hand and the rag in the other. He wiped the glass and left a gray streak where the sleet had been, and the wipers went back and forth across it, losing.

Beth went to the aft bulkhead. The lighting panel was a gray steel box with labels in Ned's block capitals on tape gone the color of tea: FWD FLOODS. AFT FLOODS. RAMP SODIUMS. RUNNING. She threw the floods down and the ramp sodiums off and left the running lights, and the car deck below went from orange to black, a red glow on one side and a green on the other. Her hands did it every departure without her.

Ned backed them off the slip. When the bow swung clear he eased the throttles up, the thrum climbed to cruising, and the windows buzzed in their frames. She took the stool beside the radar.

Most nights she'd go below, where the crew mess had the good heater and the bad coffee. Ned glanced at her and said nothing.

The wheelhouse smelled of burnt coffee and wet wool and the warm dust of the electronics. The radar swept the harbor and painted the breakwater green. Below, the reefer's cab sat dark except for the dash, the dome light off, and against the glow she could make out the driver's head, turned up toward the wheelhouse windows. She couldn't see his eyes. The angle was enough.

They cleared the breakwater. Across the harbor the fuel float at Tess Marr's was lit, the light pole burning white over the two pumps, and a dragger lay alongside with her deck lights going. Red hull, white wheelhouse. Beth knew the boat. The *Margaret Ann*, from away, with Gloucester painted on her stern. She'd been in and out since the summer before last.

At four that afternoon Beth had stood at Tess's counter and slid her paycheck across in its envelope for the morning bag. Tess pushed her glasses up into her hair, initialed the deposit slip, and set the envelope on the stack with everybody else's pay. The bag rode the morning boat back to the credit union, half the island's money in a canvas zip bag with Tess's initials on the tag. Then Tess reached over the register and tugged Beth's braid like Beth was nine.

"That tank loan's eighteen hundred a month, Bethie." Tess dropped the slip into the canvas bag. "For dirt with steel in it. Christ on a bike. I could've bought a lobster boat and sunk it on purpose and had something to look at."

"You'd have to learn to drive it first."

"I'd hire you. I'd pay you in tank." Tess laughed the big laugh, the one they could hear out on the float. At six o'clock she'd have turned the pump sign to CLOSED. Every night for nineteen years, six o'clock.

Nine now, ten minutes out of the slip, and the float was lit.

Cash after hours wasn't a crime. Somebody from away wanted diesel at night and paid for it, and Tess needed every gallon she could sell to keep that loan off her neck.

Ned watched the compass. The float lay off his port bow, bright as a gas station, the only lit thing in the harbor. He didn't look at it once.

"You're quiet," he said.

"Sleet."

He reached up with one finger and turned off his hearing aid. Out past the bow the bay was black all the way to the mainland.

---

Mid-bay, her phone said No Service, which she'd known it would. The VHF over the chart table sat on 16 and 13, its mic on the hook. She looked at the mic. Every boat in the bay listened to that radio, and every kitchen with a scanner on the counter. She left it hanging.

She went over it instead. Twenty-two minutes to Pruett Harbor. Fourteen cartons in that trailer, maybe more behind. A wax box that size held forty, fifty pounds of lobster. She didn't know how many bricks a carton held. She knew the weight of the stack from how the driver had shoved it.

Halfway across she always made one round of the deck. She stayed on the stool.

"Binders are good," she said.

Ned nodded. In eleven years he had never let her skip the round. Not in fog, not in January, not the week her father died. He let her skip it now.

At Pruett Harbor the sodiums hung over the mainland ramp and made the slush on it look like brass. She lowered the ramp from the wheelhouse controls, climbed down and worked the lines, bow and then spring, and the transfer bridge clanged as it took the ramp. The reefer was first off, nose to the ramp. She had to release its binders herself, standing beside the cab.

She kept to the routine. Bare hands. Handle up, tension off, the chain unhooked from its D-ring and laid in the bin. The forward chain, then the rear, which she had pinned an hour ago with the driver's hand resting on the carton above her. The cab window stayed shut. She could hear his heater fan and smell his cigarette through the door seal. She kept her eyes off the glass.

When she was done she stood in the shadow of the bulwark with her phone low at her hip, where a deckhand checks the time. The reefer rolled up the ramp. The sodiums lit the rear plate and the trailer number stenciled on the doors, and she took two frames without lifting her hand.

At the head of the ramp his brake lights came on and held. And held. Then he went, and the van and the propane truck followed him up.

The phone showed one bar.

Ned was out on the wing ten feet above her, calling for the stern line. The deadhead left in six minutes. Up in the lot the truck's taillights sat red at the gate, idling. Beth put the phone in her bib pocket and zipped it. Not with that driver up there behind a shut window, looking down at a lit deck. Not in front of anyone. Not yet. Not in front of—

She threw off the stern line.

The ramp came up empty. Ned brought them around for home, and the car deck behind her was bare steel and chain.

---

She wrote it in pencil at the kitchen table, on the back of the ferry district's tide calendar, plain as a line in the fuel log.

*8:40 p.m. Cutter slip. Reefer, nose to ramp. Unit off. Display dark. Box not cold.*

*Rear doors open. Two men. Driver stacking inside. 14 cartons seen.*

*Top carton lid sprung. Live lobster, rockweed, newspaper. Under: plastic, vacuum-sealed bricks. Blue.*

*Hood, oilskin, left leg.*

*9:44 p.m. Pruett Harbor ramp.* She copied the plate off the photograph letter by letter, then the trailer number under it, then checked both against the screen, twice.

The woodstove was banked low. In the front room her father's clock went on with what it did. Passing the kitchen door, she tapped the barometer with one knuckle. The needle dropped a hair and settled. A thousand. Down four since supper.

At the window the harbor lay black except for the float, still lit. She watched it with her hands in her bib pockets. At twenty to one the light went out. A minute later the *Margaret Ann*'s deck lights slid off the float, swung, and settled on the guest mooring, dead below the house.

She didn't dial 911. A 911 call went to county dispatch, and county went out over the scanners that sat on half the kitchen counters on Cutter, next to the bread. She took the phone book out of the drawer under the wall phone, found the state police barracks, and dialed it direct.

The stove clock said 1:10. She'd waited on purpose. Ten minutes off the boat, that truck pointed back at whoever chained it; three hours down Route 1, the only road south, it was anybody's bad luck.

A man answered with his name and the barracks.

"There's drugs in a lobster truck," Beth said. "Off the Cutter Island ferry at Pruett Harbor, quarter to ten tonight. It's headed south, I'd think."

He asked her to slow down. She hadn't been going fast. She gave him the plate and the trailer number, read off the calendar. He asked how she knew what was in the truck.

"I chained it down. A lid sprang."

He asked what she saw. She told him. He asked how much. She said fourteen cartons and she didn't know what was under the rest. He asked her name.

"Beth Calloway. Elizabeth. I'm the deckhand."

He took her number. He read the plate back to her and she said yes. Then he asked, in the same voice as all the rest, "Did anyone see you?"

Her free hand was on the wall beside the phone. It had been there since he picked up. The plaster was cold.

"Two men," she said.

When it was over she hung up and stood with her hand still on the wall. Her fingers smelled of chain and diesel. Her name was in a barracks log on the mainland now, next to a plate number and a time, in somebody else's handwriting.

The back door had a thumb-latch and nothing else. She lifted it and shoved the door with her shoulder to seat it, and the latch came down with its iron clack, a sound she knew in her sleep and would never make by accident. She wedged the chair from beside the woodbox under the latch bar.

She put the kettle on, then sat down in her father's chair, turned to face the mudroom door, still in her bib pants.

---

The phone rang into a gray kitchen. She had it before the second ring.

"Elizabeth Calloway?"

"Beth."

"Ms. Calloway, this is Trooper Dana Ames, Maine State Police." A woman, plain and level, reading from something. "The vehicle you described was stopped on Route 1 south of Pruett Harbor a little before three this morning. The driver is in custody."

Beth looked at the chair under the latch. "Okay."

"A federal agent will be calling you today. The Marshals Service is sending someone out to you on the first boat. Until then, ma'am, don't go to work."

"Which boat?"

A paper moved. "The eight o'clock out of Pruett Harbor."

"That's the first one out of Pruett," Beth said.

When the trooper was gone she took the pencil and wrote under the rest of it, on the tide calendar: *6:10 a.m. Ames. Rt 1, before 3. Custody. 8:00 boat.* The kettle sat cold at the back of the stove, shoved off the heat at some hour she didn't remember. Her neck ached from the chair.

The sleet had quit sometime in the night. The wind hadn't. It had backed into the northeast and was working at the north gable, a long push and then a shudder. She reached over the sink and turned up the weather radio on the shelf.

The voice had changed its mind since supper. A storm warning for the coastal waters from this afternoon. Wind east, backing northeast, gale force by evening, storm force overnight. A coastal flood warning for the evening high tide.

She tapped the barometer. Nine ninety-six. Down four more since one o'clock, and the day not started.

She had to call Ned. She stood at the wall phone with her hand on the receiver and decided what she wasn't going to say, the way she'd decide which line to throw first. Then she dialed.

Ned picked up on the first ring too. Behind him a coffee maker was gargling, and she could hear his boots on his kitchen linoleum, already on.

"I'm not coming in," she said.

The line hissed.

"I'll work it short," Ned said.

That was all. She stood with the cord wound once around her fingers for the why. It never came.

"I'll get Kenny out of bed," he said.

"He's sick."

"He'll live." And he hung up.

She put the dead receiver back.

At the window the light was coming up the color of dishwater. Nine lobster boats lay to their moorings in the inner harbor, bows to the northeast, every one an islander's she could name by the hull. And the dragger. The *Margaret Ann* rode the guest mooring with her deck lights still burning, pale in the daylight.

Somebody had a radio going aboard her. It reached the house thin and broken by the wind, a fiddle and then nothing and then a fiddle. A figure stood on her afterdeck, too far off for a face, too far for a hood. It was turned toward shore.

The guest mooring lay right below this window.

Beth reached over and switched off the kitchen light.

# Chapter 2 — The Weather Hold

**Day 1 — Friday**

The *North Reach* came into Cutter with the sea on her bow and spray coming back over the ramp in sheets. Dane stood aft of the passenger cabin with his back to the bulkhead and his right side clear, and the diesel came up through the deck into the soles of his boots. They were the wrong boots. He had known it since the first wet deck plate at Pruett Harbor.

Below the rail the car deck held one pickup and a pallet of wood pellets under a tarp, and it smelled of cold hydraulic oil. The sky had come down to the hill, the color of wet slate.

While the boat was still a hundred yards out he read the people at the head of the slip, because anyone who met a ferry in this weather had a reason. Two men in orange bib pants stood by a handcart with their hands in their pockets, cold and there for the pallet. An old woman in a plastic rain hat had both hands on the head of a cane, waiting for something with her name on it.

A man held a phone up at arm's length, screen to the sky, getting nothing. Two women with coffee cups had come down to see. At the head of the ramp a man in a canvas coat stood with his arms folded, and he was the one Dane would look at twice.

That many people had turned out on a November morning for one pickup, a pallet and a man in a soft-shell jacket, and he did not flatter himself that it was the pallet.

There was no deckhand. The ramp came down from the wheelhouse controls and rang on the transfer bridge, and the captain came down the outside ladder himself, big and slow, one hand on the rail for every rung. He threw the bow line to one of the men in orange and worked the spring alone, favoring a hip.

A short woman in a red vest came down the ramp from the road, laughing over her shoulder, reading glasses pushed up into cropped gray hair. She handed a canvas zip bag with a seal tag up over the rail, and the captain put it inside his jacket. A town without a bank sent its money across on the boat. He moved on.

The captain looked at him then: at the soft-shell, wet through at the shoulders, at the boots, at the bag, the ramp and the sky, everywhere but Dane's face. It was the look of a man who had been told on the phone before dawn who would be on his boat, and had decided he would not be the one to say so. That was fine. A man who kept quiet about one thing usually kept quiet about others, which was worth knowing about the only captain on the island.

Under the hill, alone on a mooring, lay a steel dragger with a red hull and a white wheelhouse. Her stern had swung to the wind, and he read the transom: MARGARET ANN. GLOUCESTER, MASS. An out-of-state boat on a guest mooring the morning after a trooper pulled a lobster truck over on Route 1 might mean nothing, but her deck lights were burning in full daylight, and that he liked less.

He would write it down tonight. He would remember it either way.

Before he stepped off he ran the day once more. They would be back aboard for the two o'clock, a second deputy would meet them at Pruett with a car, and the site was ninety minutes up the road from there. Pike had called it a one-day job, and in his experience the jobs that went wrong were the ones somebody had called simple.

This one came down to a woman he had never met and a date in Portland, and his work was to get her there with nothing on the way that a lawyer could use. The subpoena was in his inside pocket against the notebook, and it would stay dry if he kept his elbows in.

He walked off behind the pallet. The man with the folded arms watched him to the top of the ramp. The old woman looked at his boots.

The Harbor Road climbed from the slip between a stone wall and a row of fish houses, and he read it as he went for who could see what. From the head of the slip you had the first two hundred yards; from the store dock, where a fuel float ran out under a dark light pole, the road as far as the church; from the church, the top of the hill. At the top stood a gray-shingled Cape with a big center chimney, and its kitchen window looked down on all of them.

Someone stood in that window. Then the window was empty.

The front door opened while he was still three steps from it. She stood in the doorway in a black watch cap, one hand on the frame, and her eyes went to his hands before they came to his face, which was what he would have done in her place and what almost nobody did. He had expected someone frightened, and she looked instead like a woman who had made up her mind about him and would let him try to prove her wrong.

---

He held the leather folder open long enough for her to read it. Deputy United States Marshal. Reyes, Dane. A photograph with less gray at the temples.

"Ma'am, I'm here to—"

"Beth. Or Calloway." She stepped back from the door. "Not ma'am."

"Ms. Calloway."

She let it stand.

The kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and her float coat drying on its hook. He wiped his boots twice on the mat without being asked, and he didn't sit.

The subpoena was one sheet. She read it standing, all the way down. United States District Court. Grand jury. Portland. *Monday, November 23*, nine a.m. She folded it once and slid it under the bottom of the barometer's frame by the kitchen door, where the wall held it flat. On the way back she tapped the glass. Nine ninety-four.

He gave her the rest standing at the counter. The driver's name was Pelletier. He was in custody and would see a magistrate today. The cartons were what she'd thought. Pressed pills, in bricks.

"Fentanyl," he said.

She had both hands on the counter. She left them there.

"You're not in the program. You're in the waiting room for the program." He said it like a man who had said it before and still didn't like it. "I'm the waiting room."

"Where's the waiting room?"

"We take the two o'clock boat, and another deputy meets us at Pruett at two forty-five. After that you'll be in a place on the mainland until the grand jury."

Below the window the *North Reach* was backing out of the slip, her stern swinging to the wind. Nine-fifteen.

"She's leaving now," Beth said.

"The other deputy can't be at Pruett before two forty-five, and I won't sit you in an open terminal lot for five hours with one gun." His hand, on the counter near hers, stopped on the word *lot*. Then it went on as if it hadn't. "May I use your phone?"

He dialed from memory. She fed the stove a split of maple and listened to his half. "Reyes. I'm at the house and she's with me. We're holding for the two o'clock, and I'll want the car at Pruett at two forty-five. Yes. Understood."

He hung up. She tapped the barometer. Nine ninety-two.

"It won't run," she said. "Pack if you want. You'll be unpacking by two."

"Tell me why you think so."

"Wind's backed northeast. The glass is down four since breakfast. And Ned won't put her into that slip on a falling tide with the sea building."

"I'll plan on the two o'clock until the captain cancels it, because that's his call to make, and I'd rather unpack a bag than need one I didn't pack." He glanced down the hall. "In the meantime I'd like to walk the house."

She took him through it. Upstairs, two rooms under the eaves nobody slept in. Her own room off the downstairs hall. He looked at every window from the side, never straight on. In the mudroom he stopped.

The chair from beside the woodbox was still wedged under the latch bar. He looked at it, then at her.

"Last night," she said.

He put two fingers under the latch, lifted it the little the chair allowed, and let it fall. It came down with its iron clack.

"It sticks," she said.

"I heard it," he said, "and I expect the whole house does."

---

By half past eleven the sleet was back, a dry rattle on the north windows. The Icom handheld sat on the kitchen table on 16 and 13.

Dane walked the property while she stood at the sink under the window and washed nothing. He stayed longest at the corner of the yard where the guest mooring lined up with her window. She'd lined that one up herself at dawn.

The woodbox was half empty and it would be dark by four. She pulled the chair out from under the latch, put her shoulder to the door and shoved the latch up. It stuck. "Damn thing," she said, and shoved again, and it gave.

Three armloads from under the lean-to, sleet already in the bark. She was stacking the third when he came around the house and saw the chair.

"I'd like that chair to stay off the door, if you can live with it," he said.

She straightened with a split in each hand. "Front door opens on the road. Anybody comes that way, a chair under this latch shuts us in."

"That's one reason." He laid his hand on the door, not the latch. "But the latch is a better alarm than a chair, because nobody lifts it quietly."

"I'd hear it from my bed." She carried the chair to the stove and set it there.

He went back out to finish the shore side. He came in at ten past one with his hair flattened wet against his head, and she had a dish towel in her hand and was holding it out before she'd decided to. He took it. He dried his hair standing at the stove and hung the towel over the back of the chair.

At a quarter past one the handheld opened.

"All stations, all stations, this is the Cutter Island ferry *North Reach*. The fourteen hundred departure is cancelled. Next departure to be announced. *North Reach* standing by one-six and one-three."

Ned's radio voice. Not a word extra.

Dane looked at the radio, then at her. "You were right about the boat. I'm going to stop arguing with you about water."

"Lamp oil's under the sink," she said. "Chimneys on the pantry shelf. Wicks in the drawer."

He went through the other ways off the island out loud, and closed each one.

"I won't use a private boat. Any boat in this harbor could be part of it, the one on the guest mooring included, and a witness who leaves on a neighbor's boat leaves a trail of whose boat it was."

"LifeFlight lands on the ballfield," she said.

He looked at the brick in the middle of the house. "I thought about moving you tonight, but I don't have another house I've walked, and this one has a chimney a round won't go through and a door I can hear. We stay here tonight. Half this island watched me walk up your road, but nobody knows you stayed, and nobody will."

Past the breakwater the bay was white to the horizon. Nothing small was getting out of that harbor today.

"Okay," she said.

---

By half past five it had been dark for more than an hour. The shades were down in every room. Dane had asked for the overhead lights off, and one kerosene lamp burned low on the kitchen table, because a lamp threw less through a shade than a bulb did.

At four, filling the lamp, she'd told him the rule. At six Tess ran the check-in net on 68. Miss it and Walt Grindle's fire crew was on your porch by seven, and everybody on the net heard every name.

"Then I'd rather your name didn't go out to everyone on the island with a radio," he'd said.

"Leave it to me."

At twenty to six he zipped his jacket. "I want to see what the house shows from the road."

He went out the front. She heard his boots cross the porch and then nothing. She took the receiver off the wall and carried it to the mudroom doorway, as far from the front of the house as twelve feet of cord would reach, and dialed the store.

"Marr's." Bright and fast, a register drawer banging shut behind it.

"It's me."

"Bethie."

"I'm home. Don't send anybody. Keep it off the air."

"Course, hon. I'll count you by phone." Beth could hear the pencil going in the spiral book Tess kept open by the register. "You okay?"

"I'm okay."

"Heard you had company off the morning boat."

"Don't ask me, Tess."

Tess didn't. Something small hit the counter at her end and rolled, a coin, and she said "Hell's bells" under her breath and laughed. The laugh came late.

"I'm net control tonight, for my sins," Tess said. "And emergency management director. Since town meeting. I was slow putting my hand down." The drawer again. "When the power goes, that generator eats forty dollars of gas a day. Channel 9 if the phones go. Like the old days."

Channel 9 was a girl in the wheelhouse of the *Ruthie C* with the mic in both hands, calling the float to say they were coming in. It was Tess on the other end every time, answering before Beth had let go of the key.

"Like the old days," Beth said.

She hung up and stood with her hand on the phone. The front door opened and the cold came in ahead of him. He shook sleet off his shoulders onto the mat.

"The shades are good from the road. I couldn't tell which room the lamp was in."

"I've taken care of the check-in."

He nodded and left it there.

She turned the lamp down a notch.

---

At nine she stood at the kitchen window with the edge of the shade against her shoulder and her fingertips on the glass. High water was close. Every big sea boomed in under the slip, and she could feel each one come up through the pane into her hand.

Then a sound she had never heard and knew anyway: steel cable parting, a shriek high enough to come over the wind. A long grinding after it, steel on timber. Then every light in the harbor went out at once. The store dock, the church, the windows down the hill. The refrigerator quit. The kitchen was the lamp and the red seam of the stove door.

At the slip the *North Reach*'s deck floods were still burning, swinging as she surged. In their light the transfer bridge's seaward end hung down and twisted, half in the water, the sea going over it white.

Her float coat was off the hook and one arm in it before she had decided anything. Ned was alone down there. She stopped with her arm in the sleeve. A witness running down the Harbor Road in the dark was what somebody was waiting for, and the radio would tell her more than the slip could. She took the coat off and hung it up. Dane had watched her do it, and said nothing.

The handheld opened on 16, then again on 13, the same words twice. "All stations, all stations, this is the Cutter Island ferry *North Reach*. The Cutter Island transfer bridge is damaged. The vessel is secure. There will be no further service. *North Reach* standing by one-six and one-three."

Squelch. Then Ned again, slower, the radio voice wearing through. "Ten days, if the state gets a barge out."

"Ten days," Beth said.

Dane lifted the wall phone and listened. She could hear it from the table: a fast busy, every house on the island calling out at once. He hung it up, took the Iridium off his belt and went out the front into the yard, where there was nothing over him but sky. She watched through the gap at the edge of the shade. A dark shape with the stubby antenna up, his back to the wind.

He came in ten minutes later and sat across from her, and spoke slower than he had all day.

"That was Pike. My supervisor in Portland. Monday's the last day the grand jury sits in time. If they don't indict Pelletier that day, he gets a hearing by Friday, and the case starts coming apart. The defense won't give us more time."

"Ned says ten days."

"Ten days with today in it is Sunday the twenty-second, which leaves one to spare." He let it sit. "If the ferry's back Sunday, we're on it. If it isn't, I'll find another way, and it won't be a neighbor's boat. Pike calls back at eleven-twenty every night, and I'll take it on the porch."

Beth got up and tapped the barometer. Nine eighty-six. Under it the subpoena lay against the wall, the date showing.

---

At eleven-twenty she heard the front door.

She was on her bed in her clothes, boots on the floor where her feet would find them. Across the hall she could see a slice of the front room and the bottom of its window, where he'd left the shade up two inches to watch the road from the couch. Dane stepped off the end of the porch into the open with the antenna up and something small and flat in his other hand, facing the road. His voice was low and the wind took all of it. Five minutes by her father's clock. Then the door, and the couch taking his weight.

The stove was banked to a red line. The wind came at the north gable in long shoves.

At eleven-forty the latch lifted.

It stuck. It dropped back a hair. It lifted again.

Lift and shove was one motion to a hand that knew this latch. This hand was trying it in pieces.

She didn't reach for the lamp or the flashlight. She put her feet on the cold boards and her shoulder on the wall so the floor wouldn't talk. Four steps to her door. Seven down the hall. Dane was already sitting up on the couch, boots on, his right hand off his knee and at his hip.

She bent until her mouth was at his ear. "Back door. Someone's on the latch."

He didn't ask if she was sure. He pointed once at the chimney mass, and she got behind the brick and put her back to it. The cold of it came through her shirt. She knew every board in this house. He had the gun. She let him go to the door.

She heard all of it. His weight crossing the kitchen, not fast. The latch going still. The mudroom door yanked inward, and the storm coming into the house all at once, wind and sleet on the floorboards. Feet on wet ground outside, running, going away. Then only the wind.

After a while, one beam of his flashlight, down on the back step.

The door shut. She heard him lift and shove the latch and drop it home. Clack. He came as far as the kitchen doorway.

"There was one set of boots, and it went across the yard toward the alders and the shore path. The sleet's already filling them in."

"That latch sticks," she said. "Whoever that was has never been in this house."

"Beth." He hadn't said her name before. "Who knew you were here?"

"You."

It was out before she thought, and it was true the way she meant it. Past him, through the doorway, the wall phone hung in the stove's red light with its cord in loose coils toward the floor. Her eyes went over it and on.

He didn't answer. He went to the stove, opened it, laid two splits on the coals and shut it. Then he took the blanket off the cushions and laid it over the arm of the couch, a step from her, and left it there without holding it out.

The porch at eleven-twenty. The antenna up. His face to the road.

She stayed where she was, her back on the brick. Dane stood in the kitchen doorway with the stove behind him and the sat phone's stubby antenna showing above his jacket pocket.

She didn't step out.

# Chapter 3 — The Check-In Log

**Day 2 — Saturday**

Beth set her own boot down beside the print, heel to heel, and weighted her tide card under a beach rock next to both for scale. Rain came off the eave in a sheet a foot from her face. Behind her the mudroom door knocked against its latch with every gust. Nobody was holding it now.

She took three pictures. No Service across the top of the screen.

The print was longer than hers by two fingers. Lug sole, deep cleats, a chevron at the instep. The slush under the eave had held it since 11:40. The right heel had gone in deeper than the left. Out at the edge of the step, where the eave quit, one more pair still showed both feet. Same again. Right heel deep. Whoever stood here carried himself on that side and kept it there.

At 6:20 she had lifted the kitchen receiver and gotten nothing. Not last night's fast busy. Not a hum.

The door knocked, then stopped knocking. Dane came out with two mugs and stood where the eave covered him and the side yard ran straight away from him to the alders. He handed her one.

"He's never been in this house," she said. "And he'll come back to it."

"Why would he come back to a door that didn't open for him?"

"He didn't get what he came for. And he ran for the shore path, not the road. He knows where it goes."

He looked at the print, then at her boot beside it, then at the alders where the tracks had gone in. He looked at the heel longest.

Below them the harbor was gray rain with boats in it. A quarter mile, then nothing. The guest mooring ball bobbed bare in the slop, tipping to every sea. She counted the boats she could see, left to right. Nine. The red hull wasn't one of them.

Eight left on Ned's ten, if the state found a barge.

"We move tonight," Dane said, "while he still thinks you're in this house."

"Ned caretakes houses. Three are shut and close enough. The Lathams' on the cove road. Hallorans' up behind the church. The Ashby place on the north point."

"How many ways into each of them?"

"Lathams' has two. Hallorans' has the road and the church lot. Ashby's has the drive. The shore path over the bar, at low water. Nobody comes in that way."

"And who can see the door from outside?"

"Half the cove, at Lathams'. Anybody at church, at Hallorans'. Nobody at Ashby's. Water on three sides."

"Then it's the Ashby place, if the bar is the only other way in."

He'd asked about the door, not the house. She'd have picked a mooring on the same question.

She set her mug on the step. "Hiding gets noticed out here. Being seen is normal. I'm going to the store."

"That's a room full of people who all know you, around a stove I can't see past." He didn't move from the eave. "In daylight, then, and before noon. You stay where I can see you, and you tell me who in that room is from away."

Those were good terms. "Okay."

She lifted the rock off the tide card. Rain off the eave had already found the print and was running into it, softening the cleats, filling the chevron. By the time she had the card folded into her pocket the heel was gone.

---

At ten she was in the mudroom with one leg in her Grundéns and a dry sock in her hand. Dane had said at breakfast that he called Portland at ten. She wasn't listening for it. The wind leaned on the north gable, let go, leaned again, and the wall between her and the dooryard was one board and a course of shingle.

She had the bibs up and one strap over her shoulder when the wind quit.

"Go ahead."

His voice, out past the eave where he'd walked for sky, carried along the shingles. Low. Then nothing for a long time. Water off the eave.

"When?"

Nothing.

"No. She doesn't know."

A gust came down on the gable and ate whatever followed. When it slacked she got "—daily. Understood," and then the wind again.

She stood through the lull with the strap in her fist.

*She.* Her, or somebody in Portland. A wife. A judge. Asking would tell him she'd stood here with her bibs half on and listened. She clipped the other strap.

He came in with his shoulders dark from the rain and his voice exactly where it had been at breakfast. She poured coffee. He took the chair against the wall with the window on his left. She saw him choose it. She took the other one before she thought about it.

Then she thought about it.

Out the window a pickup came down the Harbor Road at a crawl, wipers going. Russ, on his storm rounds. She was up and into her float coat.

"The fewer people who know someone tried that door—"

"Somebody saw his tracks. Or saw a man from away on my porch at midnight." She zipped the coat. "By supper it's a story, and there's no constable in it. Tell Russ, it's his business. Don't, it's everybody's."

He followed her out and stood at the corner of the dooryard where he could see the road both ways.

She walked into the road with one hand up. The truck stopped and the window came down. Russ leaned his forearm on the door frame as if sitting up straight were optional. His beard was dark with wet.

"Calloway. You're out in it."

She gave it to him like a log entry. Eleven-forty. The back latch, tried twice. One man, toward the shore path. A print under the eave, gone now, but she had the picture. She held the phone to the window and he looked at it a long time.

"Big foot," he said. "So I'll write down that a big-footed man is walking around Cutter at night. That narrows it to about forty of us." He handed the phone back. "I'll walk the shore path this afternoon. Somebody tries your door, that's mine. I mean it."

He looked past her at Dane. Took him in, boots to hair, and asked nothing at all. Then he put the truck in gear and held it on the brake.

"Where you sleeping tonight? Not asking for me. Asking because somebody will."

"She's covered," Dane said from the dooryard, "and the fewer people who know where, the longer that stays true."

Russ took his cap off, looked into it, and put it back on.

"Asking because somebody will," he said, and let the truck roll.

---

The bell rang over the door. Coffee and kerosene. Out back the generator roared flat and steady, the only engine running on the Harbor Road. Every light in the store was on.

Dane went to the stove and put his back to the post office wall, where he had the whole floor and the door. Six people in the room. Beth named them to herself, then said it low at his shoulder. "Island. All six." She went on to the counter.

Tess came around it before she got there. A hand on Beth's arm, a tug on the braid. "Bethie. Out in public. Generator's eating forty a day, and I've got half the north road on credit till the barge."

On the back corner of the stove a soup pot rattled its lid. Mrs. Dyer's. Tess carried it up the hill at one.

Ned sat on the stool by the stove in his green Johnson Woolen jacket, telling it to the room like a forecast. "Sea came in under her. Lifted the whole boat." A long pause. "Then the cables went. Both of them. Like a goddamn rifle shot." Another. "Seaward end went down. Twisting, going down."

"All right, Captain," Tess said. "We've got the picture. Some of us were in it."

Ned looked at his hands. "District says they've got a crane barge—"

"—out of Rockland Wednesday, if it lays down." Tess rang up the water jugs without looking up.

"I'll put it out at noon," Ned said.

It hadn't been on the radio. He'd called the district on the booster phone in Tess's back office, the only phone on the island still working, and he told Tess everything. The whole island knew that.

Tess bagged jugs, cans, coffee, batteries, matches. Enough for a house with its water shut off. While she worked, Beth read the spiral log open on the counter, upside down. Friday, 1800 net, a column of names in Tess's round hand, and halfway down: *Calloway — home (phone).* Saturday's 0800 column had a tick beside her name. No call from her this morning, not with the line dead. Tess had counted her anyway.

Tess saw her looking. She squared the log to the counter edge with two fingers, top, then side.

Off the air, as Beth had asked.

Tess's phone lay face-down by the register. It buzzed against the wood. Tess picked it up already smiling. "Hold that thought, hon." She went through the curtain into the back office.

Dane came to the counter for coffee, his phone in his other hand. One bar.

Tess came back through the curtain, sliding her phone into her vest.

"I've got signal in here," Dane said. "I didn't think anybody had signal on this island."

"Booster's on the generator. Only signal on the island, so don't you tell anybody or I'll have the whole town in here charging." She leaned on the counter. "Coffee's double for you, though. Off-island rate."

"That seems fair to me."

"It isn't. That's why I like it."

Beth's eyes went from the phone in Tess's vest to the log on the counter: *Calloway — home (phone)*, and on Saturday's 0800 a tick for a call she had never made.

*Tess knew I stayed.*

It came whole, the way green water comes over a bow. Beth stepped back from the counter and turned her face to the bread rack by the door, and she read the bags. Country Kitchen white. Two of rye. The end of the last boat.

The night her father died, Tess came up the hill at two in the morning in her slippers and wound his clock, because Beth's hands wouldn't do it. She stood at the shelf in the front room turning the key, and she didn't say one word till it was done.

Beth kept her eyes on the rye. She held on to the clock until the other thing let go.

The bell. A tall man in a good Musto jacket. Clean-shaven. No one on a dragger was clean-shaven.

"Pack of Golds, Tess. And whatever the gentleman's having."

"I'm all set," Dane said. "Thank you, though."

"Rough night to be from away." The man looked at Dane once, about the length of a handshake. Dane didn't look away.

Tess rang the Golds wrong, said "Hell's bells," voided it and rang them again. Her fingers went slow on the drawer with a man standing over it.

Lyle Arsenault. The *Margaret Ann*'s skipper. He'd bought Beth a coffee at this counter two summers ago. That was all he was to her.

Past his shoulder, through the front window, the red hull lay along Tess's float, deck lights burning at midday. Country music came thin off her deck when the door opened. Her lines were doubled, bow and stern. The boat she'd lost at dawn had been tied here all morning.

He took the cigarettes and went out, and the bell rang after him.

"From away," she said at Dane's elbow. "The dragger's his."

"She hails out of Gloucester, which I read off her transom yesterday." He didn't look up from the cup. His eyes went to Ned, then the door. She couldn't tell what he made of either.

He went back to the stove. Four feet off, under the generator, she heard him ask it. "On Thursday, when you loaded the dealer's truck, was the reefer running?"

"He always runs his unit." Ned, to the window. "Always has. Thermo King, it's a good one, they run all night on those."

"Then I'll put down that it was running," Dane said.

Beth's palm went cold. She'd had her hand on that box. A dark display. A door seal no colder than November. She'd known Ned's tell since she was twenty-two, as well as she knew the *North Reach*'s engine note: more than anybody asked for meant he was lying. Kenny had worked the Thursdays since last winter. Ned had seen that truck every week she hadn't.

But Ned lied about the boat's business. Weights, manifests, what the freight contract paid. He always had, to keep her running. He didn't lie about people.

She said nothing in front of the room.

Ned turned on the stool and put his back to it, and looked out at the rain.

Beth picked up the bag of jugs. "I need a tank filled," she said, to nobody.

---

The propane cage stood behind the store with the generator ten feet off it, exhaust heat on her left cheek and the cold on her right. She stood with her back to the roar so it would cover what she said.

Ned came out after her without being asked, the cage key on its orange float. She held the padlock while he worked it. Rust came off on her fingers. He hooked a twenty-pound cylinder to the bulk tank and watched the gauge, because it was something to do with his hands.

Dane stood in the store's back doorway, where he could see her and the lane.

"I need the Ashby place," she said.

"For how long."

"Till the barge."

He worked a key off his ring without a question, a brass Schlage with a paper tag on it. He tore off the tag and put it in his own pocket.

"Generator hasn't run since the power went. Battery's down. Prime it before you crank or you'll flatten it."

"I know."

"I know you know." A long time. "Nobody but me and God."

He put the key in her palm and kept his hand around hers. Big, cold, rough as pot warp. "You stay off my boat till this is done."

She told him she would.

Neither of them said *reefer*.

She walked the cylinder to the back door and handed Dane the key without a word. He put it in the inside pocket of his jacket and zipped it.

"He lied to you about the reefer," she said, low. "And I just took his key."

"He lied about the truck. That's a man who looks the other way for money, which is different from a man who tries doors." Dane watched the store's back window. "Nobody tries a door he has a key to. And every other house on this island, somebody besides Ned knows where the key is."

He shouldered the cylinder. "I asked Portland for a boat this morning, and the Coast Guard won't put a crew in that sea until it lies down, so for now we're here. I'd rather be in the one house only Ned knows I asked about."

---

The Ashby drive ran a long way from the north road to the porch. She'd walked it in the dark with a jug in each hand.

Now the shed. Concrete under her knees, the cold coming up through the bibs. Diesel. The green Generac housing stood open, and the gap between the unit and the shed wall was barely a body wide, and she was in it.

"Light."

It came over her shoulder and stopped on her hands. She found the lift pump by feel, then by sight, the hand primer lever on its flank. She worked it and counted under her breath. Twelve. Twenty. Thirty-one. At thirty-eight the lever stiffened and the pump went from hollow to full. Fuel up. Her fingers stank of diesel and were too cold to feel the lever's edge.

The light hadn't moved. Not off her hands once. Not to the door, not to the wall.

"Panel's on your end," she said.

She came back through the gap. He hadn't stepped out of it, or the shed hadn't left him room to. She came up turned half into him with her shoulder against his chest, and the light fell to the floor between their boots. His breath was at her temple, warm and slow, and then not slow. She could feel him breathing through her float coat. Neither of them moved.

She reached past him and held the crank switch over.

The starter ground once on the low battery. Twice. The diesel caught and came up to a roar, and the bulb in the ceiling came on, and there they were, lit, closer than the dark had let them be.

He stepped back first. His free hand came up toward her and went to the shed door instead. He held it open against the wind, and when she went past him he had already picked up the heavier jug from beside the pad.

Halfway across the yard, with the generator going behind them, she asked him. She'd decided to.

"Why are you out here alone?"

He answered without making her wait. "It was a one-day job. I'm on loan to Portland because they were short this month. Take a witness off on the two o'clock boat, hand her and the paperwork to a second deputy at Pruett, and be back at the motel by supper."

The wind pulled at the jug, and he shifted it. "The storm came twelve hours early, and nobody's come out since. I packed these boots for a parking lot, and I knew it when I packed them."

She could hear that it was true. It went as far as it went.

The house smelled of mothballs and cold wallpaper. Every shape in it wore a sheet. They kept one room lit at the back with the shades down, and he went through the front rooms in the dark to see what the drive could see. She pulled the sheet off a couch in the room off the kitchen, where she had the back door in sight, and sat.

At eleven-twenty she heard the porch door and his boots, then his voice, low under the generator, too far off for words. She didn't get up. *Daily. Understood.* It sat in her head in his voice, beside the man in the shed whose hand had gone to the door instead of to her. She didn't try to make them agree.

She got up off the couch and set her XtraTufs by the back door with the toes facing out, as her father had left his when he went to sea. Then she lay down again in her clothes, with the voice on the porch still going.

# Chapter 4 — Glassing the Harbor

**Day 3 — Sunday**

Twenty-two.

Beth went along the moorings again, left to right, the binoculars braced on her fists and her elbows in the lichen. Spray came up over the lip of the granite in fine cold handfuls and beaded on the lenses. She wiped them on her cap and went again.

Twenty-two boats where there should be twenty-four. Then she had the other two: the *Gray Lady* and Hartley's *Lisa B.*, both hauled the week before the storm. Twenty-two was right.

"All home," she said.

The wet had come through her bibs at the knees and hips. Behind them the woods path dropped back into spruce. Anybody looking for her would watch the north road, because it was the only road.

Dane lay a body-length above her where the granite humped, so he could see the path mouth over his shoulder. He shifted, and something in his boot squelched.

"These were sold to me as waterproof," he said.

"They are. Up to the top."

"That's a narrow definition of waterproof."

The harbor lay under her like a chart. The store dock, with the *Margaret Ann*'s red hull along Tess's float and her deck lights still burning. The *North Reach* starboard-to the stone pier, the transfer bridge beside her twisted down into the water like a dropped ladder. And on the Harbor Road, small and gray, her own Cape with no smoke at the chimney.

At ten to seven a skiff came out from behind the dragger. Aluminum, riding low. One man, hood up, rowing. No outboard. On a Sunday with the wind gone north, an outboard would carry to every kitchen on the harbor.

She kept the glasses on him. Short, choppy strokes. He came across the inner harbor and passed under the *North Reach*'s stern, where Ned stood on the after deck in his green jacket with a pump hose over his shoulder. The rower lifted one hand off the oar.

Ned turned his back and went on working the hose.

Refusal. Or an answer, from a man who answered most things with his back. She couldn't tell, so she didn't say it.

The skiff nosed in at the fish pier ladder. The man made fast, climbed, and started up the pier.

Head down. Left leg favored. The right heel coming down hard every step, as if the planks owed him something.

Her free hand closed on a fistful of lichen.

"That's him."

Dane didn't move. "What makes him the one?"

"The walk. Thursday night, off the ramp, he walked like that." She kept the glasses up. "You saw the heel yesterday. I didn't say it matched. I wanted to watch him walk before I said."

"Most witnesses I've had would have said it on the first look and been sorry for it later." He said it to the harbor, not to her.

"Hold them still for me."

He slid down the rock beside her. She braced the glasses on the granite and he pressed his phone lens to the right eyepiece. His cheek was an inch from hers. His breath was the warmest thing on the hill. She didn't move.

The shutter clicked four times.

The man didn't go to the store. He walked the Harbor Road, past the church, past the Pinkhams' woodpile, and turned up her driveway.

He went round the back of her house.

She counted. Thirty. Sixty. Eighty. At ninety-one he came round the front and stopped in her dooryard with his face to the kitchen window, the one that looked down on the whole harbor. He stood there while she breathed twice.

Then he walked back toward the pier.

She followed him down the ladder and into the skiff, then passed the glasses up without looking away from the water.

"Third piling from the ladder, Reyes. He's bailing."

Dane put the glasses up, and neither of them spoke while the man below bailed with a cut-down jug and never once looked up the hill.

---

Below the spruce, the Union Church sat white on the Harbor Road with nobody walking to it. Its bell rope knocked against the clapboards in the wind.

Dane walked back out onto the bare granite with the Iridium, because it needed sky. Beth stayed in the trees with the Icom against her ear on 68, the volume down to a thread.

"Cutter net, Cutter net, good morning, this is Marr's, net control. It's Sunday. Reverend says church is cancelled and pray at home. I say pray near your stove. Checking in by list. Dyer, go ahead."

Mrs. Dyer went ahead. The chimney was drawing, mostly. It drew better in a west wind, and it had drawn beautifully the winter of the big ice, when Harold was still alive to clean it. Tess let her run all the way down on her own.

"Chowder's coming up at noon, Ida, and I'll bring Thursday's paper, so you'll know how it all came out." A breath, and then to all of them. "Anybody short on generator gas, come down. Pay me in April, or pay me in lobsters, I'm not proud. Everybody I can't reach I'm counting home and dry unless somebody tells me different."

She didn't say Calloway. She didn't need to.

The day Beth's mother left, Tess had driven her down to the boat in a borrowed truck and bought her a hot chocolate in Pruett Harbor, and let her hold it till it went cold. Neither of them had mentioned it since.

The wind dropped. Dane's voice came in off the granite.

"It's the sternman, the one who favors his left leg. She picked him out at six hundred yards."

Wind. Then: "Yes."

A long gap. "I'll ask her."

Six hundred yards was her. *I'll ask her* was her. On the phone he called her *she*, as he had on Saturday: *She doesn't know.* She set the two side by side and left them there.

He came back into the trees with his collar dark from spray. "Quist ran the boat." He read her face. "She's the case agent, Homeland Security, and whatever I see on this island goes to her. She has a Gloucester registry, Arsenault as owner and two crew listed. Her people take the boat when the sea goes down, and until then she'd prefer we do nothing clever."

"He's as stuck as we are."

"He is." He zipped the phone inside his jacket. "I could walk down there and have him by lunch, and then you'd be up here alone, with the only one of me on this island gone and no way off the rock. If I go after him, I've left you, and he isn't the job they sent me out here to do. You are." He looked down at the red hull. "What I can do is make sure he never sees you."

He nodded at the radio. "Who's that?"

"Tess."

"She keeps that whole net talking without once sounding like she's in charge of it."

"She's good at everybody."

"Cutter net secured," Tess said in her ear. "Stay warm, stay off the water. I mean it, Captain."

Beth switched the handheld off and clipped it inside her float coat, and they started down.

---

She heard the truck before the trees opened. A Ford with a lifter tapping, idling rough in the cold. Russ's.

Dane put a hand out. She shook her head.

"Duck into the trees and he sees the tracks after and wonders. Walk past and he waves."

The path came out on the old camp road, two ruts and a grass hump. Russ's pickup sat in the ruts with its exhaust hanging low and blue in the spruce. Russ leaned on the tailgate with a thermos cap of coffee going cold in his hand. A pipe wrench and a bucket in the bed.

"Calloway." He looked at Dane and then at the bucket. "Checking camps. Somebody's got to make sure the summer people's pipes freeze in an orderly way." He tipped his head at the hill. "You come down off the top? *Margaret Ann* still tied up at Tess's?" Nobody had answered him. "Course she is. Nobody's going anywhere."

Dane stopped in the near rut and watched him. Russ's free hand lay easy on the tailgate, the left ring finger short a tip.

"Heard a Generac running out on the point last night," Russ said. "Ned doesn't usually start the Ashby one till the power's been out a week."

"Who else on this end of the island would have noticed it?" Dane said.

Russ thought about it. "Anybody up the north road with ears. That's four houses. Two of them are deaf."

He pushed off the tailgate. "Walked your shore path yesterday. Alders to the ledge, ledge to the cove road. He knew the way. Rain had the rest." He opened the door. "Still mine, Calloway. You need anything, I'm on 68. Or the town office. Mostly I'm in this truck." He got in and went bumping off toward the next camp with the tailgate rattling.

They walked out along the ruts. Dane watched the spruce on the uphill side.

"A man who tells you he knows where you sleep is doing one of two things. He's warning you, or he's watching what your face does when he says it. I watched him the whole time, and I couldn't tell you which one that was."

"Russ has known where everybody on this island sleeps since he was twelve."

"Then four houses with ears know it too," Dane said, "and we can't take back a generator we've already run. If anybody comes up that lane asking about it, we move."

"He knows where you are."

"He knows where the generator is. Out here, as far as I can tell, that comes to the same thing."

---

The Ashby generator surged twice after lunch and quit. Beth heard it from the kitchen and knew the sound: air in the line. Dane had topped the day tank from the jugs that morning after letting it run nearly dry, and the pickup had sucked a bubble.

She had her gloves off before she got to the shed.

The door was propped open on the driveway for light. Concrete, diesel, the green housing standing open, cold enough that her breath sat in front of her.

"Your hands," she said. "Not mine. You don't learn it watching."

He took his place at the filter. He set himself so the open door was in front of him and the generator was between him and the wall. She'd known he would.

"Bleed screw. Top of the filter. Crack it a turn."

He found it and cracked it.

"Primer lever. On the side of the pump. Work it."

He worked it. Foam came up around the screw, then spit, then foam again, and he kept at it until the fuel ran clean and steady with no bubbles in it.

"Close it."

He closed it. Diesel had worked into the creases of his fingers. She took the wrench and went down to the injector lines and cracked the first nut half a turn. Fuel ran over her own fingers, cold enough to ache.

"Walk me through what the ferry pays," he said.

She did the second nut. She decided.

"Winter hours. Three days and the freight run. Summer's better." The third nut. "Bank took my father's boat eight years ago. The *Ruthie C*. On paper. He was out hauling the day they did it. The sale didn't cover the note, so I paid the rest. Four years." The fourth. "House is mine. No mortgage. I pay taxes on his fish house on the cove, which nobody's opened in four years."

"What's in it?"

"His stove. His paint." She wiped the wrench. "I've got a hundred-ton master's license in a drawer."

"And I'd guess it has stayed in that drawer since the day it came."

"Asking Ned for the wheel is asking him to be old."

*Day 6, night*

At twenty past eleven Dane went out under the sky for his call. At twenty-six past he came back in, slid the notebook into the inside pocket of the soft-shell on the door nail, ran the zipper shut and set the bar in its brackets.

Beth watched the pocket close. Then she lifted the jacket off the door nail and hung it on the one in the post beside the stove.

She took the box out of the store bag and set it on the bench beside his watch. Tess's Hershey bar stayed in the bag. He read the end of the box and put it down.

"You can stop this at any point," he said, "halfway or after, and I'll stop."

"I know."

"Then I'd like to hear you say it."

"Yes." She took him by the front of the fleece. "Come here."

He kissed her slowly, a man with all night, which was the one thing they didn't have. She got the fleece off over his head, then the T-shirt, and turned his watch face down on the bench. He laughed once, through his nose, at the watch.

The cot frame complained when it took them both. Her father had built it for one man and a nap between hauls. The canvas under the blanket was salt-stiff and smelled of old oilskins and hot iron.

His mouth went along her collarbone and down to her breast, and she arched to meet it. By noon she'd be in a car on the mainland behind a deputy she'd never met, inside someone else's plan. She put her hands in his hair and held his head where it was, so the car would have to wait.

"You're still reporting this," she said.

He lifted his head. "Yes."

"Good. Don't change it for me."

"I won't."

She found the box by feel on the cot rail, tore one loose and put it in his hand.

Above them a hank of her father's net twine hung from a rafter nail and swung each time the frame took their weight. She watched it swing, then shut her eyes.

She turned her head on the blanket to find the door.

She didn't know where it was. Behind her head, or past the stove, or off her feet somewhere. Since the night on the freight deck she had known the door in every room she stood in, and which hand for the latch. She reached for it now and didn't have it, and she let that be true, and turned her face back to his.

"Beth."

"Don't stop."

He didn't. He kept it slow when she didn't want slow, until she did, and the cot frame knocked the wall planks, slow, then not. The lantern hissed on its low wick. "Who are you?"

"You've seen the credentials."

"Not those."

He looked at the lantern for a while.

"Four years ago, in Boston," he said, "I had a witness."

He turned his head on the blanket and looked at her. He opened his mouth as if there was more. Then he closed it, and leaned in, and kissed her instead, at the corner of her mouth first and then properly.

The drum burned down to coals and smelled of hot oil and old rust. *Day 7 — Thursday*

At ten to six the bait shed smelled the way it had at noon, only colder.

Beth stood inside the roll-up door, raised to her waist, with her back to the wall. Dane had the Icom low at his ear on 22A. Outside, the harbor was the gray that comes before color. Two hours past high water and going out, and the ramp ran steep to the float.

At 5:52 the lifeboat's lights showed off the harbor mouth, white over red, lifting and settling on what was left of the swell.

She went under the door in a crouch to the head of the ramp and stopped where the shed's corner still covered her.

The float lights were dark.

Not their hour. In November the timer held them till seven. Ned set that timer. Ned never let those lights go dark.

She looked for the *Margaret Ann* at the store dock. The dock was empty. She found the red hull on a mooring off the fish pier, one she'd never seen her use, with a straight line across open water to this float. Every night for a week she'd burned end to end. This morning she was black.

On the float, tied short on the outside, lay a skiff. Gray fiberglass, high-sided, no name, no patches. Not island. Not the dragger's aluminum sixteen. Its outboard was down, the lower unit in the water. Nobody tied up to a float in a swell with the leg down to grind on the weed. Not unless he meant to leave fast.

"No," she said. "Not this. Back."

Dane was at her shoulder. He keyed the Icom. "Coast Guard motor lifeboat, this is Deputy Reyes on two-two alpha. Abort, abort. Landing is not secure. Do not approach. Over."

"Reyes, Coast Guard. Copy abort. Standing off. Out."

Down on the float the skiff's outboard coughed and caught.

They went back under the door. Between the totes, through the herring and the dark. Her hand found the back door's latch where she'd left it. Behind them the outboard rose to a howl as the skiff ran off the float.

She got the door open.

The crack came off the water, flat under the outboard's howl. Sixty yards out, by the sound. The skiff had turned broadside to the float, the shed's front square in its line.

Corrugated metal banged at the harbor end. The round came through. It went by a foot behind her head, where Dane was, between her and the harbor, and he jerked against her. Beside the frame a coin of gray light opened in the back wall. He drove her through the door.

"Fuck," he said.

Gravel. The lane. Fourteen steps to the seawall.

A second shot clanged into the shed somewhere behind them and went nowhere.

Off the harbor mouth the lifeboat's lights swung, showed red only, and went away.

The Icom in Dane's fist came alive. "Any station, any station, this is Cutter Fire, Chief Grindle, at the firehouse. Was that gunfire at the town landing? Does anyone require the EMT? Over."

Nobody answered him.

They ran down the shore lane under the seawall. Dane's right hand was clamped over his left arm above the elbow, where the round had grazed him through the sleeve. Blood came dark between his fingers. Out across the harbor the outboard went small, and smaller.

*Day 7, after*

She took them home by the grown-over path off the cove road, through alders that slapped wet across their faces. At the bend where the path looked back down on the lane she stopped and looked once. Empty lane. Gray water. Nothing coming.

Inside the fish house she sat him on the bench, put his own right hand back over the wound and hers on top of it, and pressed.

"Hold it. Harder than that."

He held it. The blood had run to his wrist and was drying there.

"I have to report this now."

"You're bleeding."

"I know, and the phone still only works outside." He stood, and took the sat phone out onto the ledge under the low sky. The wind had already backed.

Through the open door she heard enough. The lifeboat had stood off until the shot went out on the air, and then its rules had taken it home: no landing under fire, no second try with a system coming in by dark. The window was shut.

"They had the time." A long gap. "No. Nobody." Another. "Only us."

She stood at the stove and laid it out in a row.

She had heard him set the time and say it back to Portland. The man on the ledge had the hour and the place, and so did the voices he gave his day to every night. So did one other. She left that one out of the row.

Three days and a storm until the ferry. No other way off.

Out on the ledge the first fine rain came over the spruce and settled on his shoulders. He kept the phone at his ear and his bloody hand clamped on his arm.

She had moved. She stood on the stove side of the room with the door in front of her, and she knew exactly where it was.

# Chapter 7 — His Handwriting

**Day 7 to Day 8 — Thursday night to Friday**

*Day 7, afternoon*

The strip's paper backing came away yellow and brittle, and the old glue under it smelled like a closed drawer.

Beth laid it across the middle of the graze first. With her thumb she pushed the edges of the cut together, then pressed the strip down over them. The cut had quit seeping about two, after she'd held pressure on it all morning and again at noon. The skin held. If she had said a name on Wednesday, it would be a clean arm.

Dane sat on the edge of the cot with his left elbow braced on his knee. His right hand was clamped round his left wrist, holding his own arm still for her. His watch was on the end of the workbench by the cot. She'd had him take it off before she started. The arm would swell.

"It sticks wet."

"Then it's better than anything the Service has ever issued me."

*Day 7, night*

At twenty to eleven the wind had found every gap in the shingles. Rain drove against the landward door and the door held. The stove was low.

Beth got up to feed it and stepped on his jacket.

Soaked through. She lifted it to hang on the nail by the stove. It came up heavier on one side. She got the collar over the nail. The jacket swung open at the inside pocket, zipper still down from the last call on the ledge, and something slid out and hit the planks.

The notebook. Yellow cover, face down, splayed.

She bent for it. It had fallen open. At the top of the left-hand page, in a hand that wasn't hers or her father's, was her name: *CALLOWAY, E.* Under it, a dollar sign.

She carried it to the workbench instead of the pocket, turned the lantern up a quarter turn and held the page to the light. It had her name on it.

*D2 Sat 1000 — Quist. $9,000 cash deposited subject's acct, D1 a.m., w/ paycheck, via store bank bag. Hundreds. Teller flagged.*

She read it twice.

She had never seen nine thousand dollars in one place. Her last statement had said four hundred and twelve dollars and some cents, and she had known what every one of them was for. Somebody had put it in her account to make her look bought.

Her eye went past *bank bag, D1, paycheck* to the top of the line. Saturday. He had known since Saturday.

Sunday in the Ashby generator shed, diesel on her hands, him at the filter asking about money. *Anything come in lately?* She'd told him about her father's boat. About the bank. She'd told him because she'd thought he wanted to know.

On the cot he breathed slow and even. Six feet away. His holster was on his hip under the blanket. His watch lay on the end of the workbench by his head, set for a quarter past eleven.

She turned back to the first page.

*D4 — no change.*

*D5 — no change.*

*Fuck.*

*Day 7, eleven*

She did not wake him.

He was armed and trained, and he read people for a living. If he was the leak, telling him she knew was the worst thing she could do. If he wasn't, he'd explain, and she couldn't check an explanation. She could check a binder by its handle.

Now, then, while the storm covered sound and kept people indoors. By daylight he'd read it in her face before she said a word.

Seventeen minutes. On the cove road he'd have her before the bend, one arm or not. The shore he couldn't follow. Low water had been at five past ten. The ledges would be out for hours yet, rockweed and gravel and granite all the way round to the church, and nobody walked that in the dark who hadn't been born to it.

The ferry, then. Steel, lit, moored to stone. A fixed VHF with the power to reach the mainland. A wheelhouse door that locked from the inside.

She wouldn't call from it in the dark. Sixteen was open to every set in the harbor, and the *Margaret Ann* kept hers on. A woman alone on a lit boat at night who had just told the whole harbor where she was would be bait. She would lock the door and wait. At first light she'd call the Coast Guard on sixteen and ask for the marshals in Portland. For Pike, by name. It was in his book. Portland could leak it if it liked. By then half the bay would have heard it, in daylight, with witnesses, from a woman who was still alive.

Ned. Ned had told Dane that dealer always ran his unit, when she'd had her bare hand on the box and it was dead. Ned ate supper at Tess's table every night of the week. She held both of those and didn't step round them. Ned could have the boat. He couldn't have the wheelhouse unless she opened it.

Not the store. Too many people in and out, and a phone in the back office. She stopped there. She thought of Tess going up the hill at one with Mrs. Dyer's soup, in any weather, and kept her mind on the soup.

Her go-bag was by the door. She shouldered it. She took the Icom in its freezer bag out of the side pocket, switched it on through the plastic, rolled the volume down to a whisper and set it scanning. She could listen. She wouldn't key it. She zipped it inside her float coat, against her ribs.

The sat phone stayed on the bench. The gun stayed on his hip, where his hand would find it before he was awake. None of it was hers. Nobody but the two of them knew this door, and nobody had come to it. He'd be safe here asleep. She wasn't safe anywhere with him.

The book stayed open. If she went without a word, he'd think she'd been taken. He'd come out into the dark after her with a gun, and someone would get shot on the cove road. The open page would tell him she'd gone on her own, and why.

She turned the lantern down to a bead and let the stove go.

On the cot his right hand lay open on the blanket. His jaw was dark with a week's stubble. At the edge of the gauze her strips showed white.

She didn't touch him.

At the door the cold came off the planks against her face. She put her hand on the bar.

"Beth," he said.

She stopped with her back to him. His breathing hitched, and then it evened out, long and slow. He'd said her name asleep.

"Deputy," she said, under her breath.

He didn't hear it.

She lifted the bar out of its brackets and lifted the door on its hinges, as her father had taught her so it wouldn't scrape, and stepped out into the rain. She shut it behind her on the open book.

*Day 7 into Day 8, night*

No moon. No light. She went down off the granite blocks onto the cove ledges instead of up the path, and turned along the shore toward the harbor. Rockweed, gravel, ledge. She walked it by the feel under her boots and by thirty years of walking it. Rain came sideways off the northeast and found the gap at her collar.

At a quarter past eleven she was round the first point, under the bank below the cove road. She stood with her shoulder to the clay and listened up at the road. Wind in the alders. Rain. If a light came down that road, it would be his.

None came.

Slow going. What the road did in forty minutes the shore did in two hours: ledges to cross on her hands, the brook mouth to wade, the flood coming a sea at a time over rock she'd walked dry at eleven. Barnacles took the skin off the back of one hand. By the time the Union Church showed pale on the bank above her, the flood had been making three hours, and the point below the church was going under. She climbed through dead bracken to cross it by the churchyard and drop down on the harbor side.

It swung as it came. Long stride, short stride. The beam dipped on the short one.

She knew that walk. Lookout Hill, the skiff, the man favoring his left leg.

She was over the wall before she'd finished knowing it. Down behind the lean-to woodshed, on her knees in the bark and the old snow, the minister's cord wood stacked between her and the road. Split oak. Wet bark. Against the clapboards the bell rope knocked every time the wind leaned on the building.

The light came on slow. It went into a dooryard across the road and came out again.

A radio broke squelch on him. A burst, a low voice she couldn't make out, the hiss tail. The light moved again. It stopped. Another burst. It moved.

Against her ribs the Icom was scanning. She found the volume knob through the plastic and turned it to nothing. A squelch break would carry farther than her breath.

The gun and the phone were behind a door she had shut herself. Out here there was a woodpile, and nobody on the island who knew where she was.

# Chapter 8 — What Ned Told

**Day 7 to Day 8 — Thursday night to Friday**

*Day 7, quarter past eleven*

The watch went off by his head, a thin chirp against the rain, and Dane was sitting up before it finished.

The lantern was down to a bead. The stove had burned to a red seam at the door. He let the room come to him, door first.

One door, landward, its bar lifted out of the brackets and leaned upright against the frame. The loft hatch shut. The window on the water side black, and under it the cove slapping at the ledge. The nail where her float coat had hung all week held nothing.

He turned the lantern up. The Iridium was on the bench, inside his reach, and the Glock was on his hip. Her bag was gone from beside the door, and the Icom with it. She had left the alarm alone.

The notebook lay open on the workbench at *CALLOWAY, E.* and the dollar sign, its spine squared to the bench edge. Rage would have fed it to the stove. Left like that, it said she had read it level and gone on her own feet.

He read it as a stranger would: her kitchen door to the minute, the Ashby key, the landing, *Do not disclose to subject*, and a column of *no change* in a hand that made no change look like suspicion. To anyone who didn't know what he knew, the pages proved that a man had slept beside her and written her down.

He went out onto the ledge with the flashlight off. The grown-over path, the cove road beyond the alders, the shore below him: all of it black, and none of it would tell him which way she had gone. She had a quarter of an hour on him, on an island she had walked since she could walk, and he had one arm and boots that slid on wet rock.

At twenty past eleven he got the antenna into open sky. Pike answered on the first ring.

Quist came on a minute after.

He gave it from the beginning, because that was the only version that would stand up later.

"The witness read the credibility log tonight. She left the detail on her own a little before twenty-three hundred, unarmed, carrying a handheld VHF." He waited out a gust. "Since the night of Day 4 I've been in a relationship with the protectee. I named it to her then. I'm naming it to you now. There is also one location we've used since that night that I kept out of the log, and I'm telling you that I kept it out, not where it is. I'm asking to be relieved and taken off this detail on the first boat that can reach the island."

The line hissed.

"All of that goes to Moreau," Quist said. The Assistant US Attorney, the man who would put Beth in front of the grand jury. "And from Moreau to the defense, Deputy. You know that."

"Yes."

"I'll have relief out there the first hour the sea allows," Pike said. "Until then you're still the detail. Act like it."

"Who outside the Service had the landing time?"

"Coast Guard station. And county emergency management, which passes it to the island. I'll have the name for you at seven."

"Where is she?"

"I think she's gone to the ferry. It's steel, it has a fixed radio and a door that locks, and the captain taught her the boat."

"Can you raise the boat?"

"Only on sixteen, and every set in the harbor would hear me ask where she is."

"Then you don't ask," Pike said. "And you don't walk that road one-handed in the dark with their man ashore. You'd get shot, or you'd lead him to her. She left on her own and she left you the page. Hold where you are until first light in case she comes back to it, then go to the boat. That's an order, Deputy, and I'm logging that I gave it."

He said he understood, and she was gone.

He had handed Pike the detail and Quist the case, and handed Moreau a deputy any defense lawyer would be glad to meet, and all it had bought him was the right to tell her before she heard it from someone else. Now it bought him a night in a shed, waiting on a woman he had taught, in his own handwriting, not to come back to him.

Back inside he fed the stove and left the bar where she had leaned it.

The Ashby key had come from the captain, and on Saturday the captain had lied to his face about the reefer. On the morning of Day 1 he had watched the store's bank bag, with the nine thousand in it, go from Tess Marr's hands inside that same captain's jacket. Now she had gone to his boat. She would call it safety, and he thought she would be half right, and that she would not let him aboard to tell her which half.

He sat the night on the low bench facing the door, with the Glock on his knee and the stove going, and it was the longest he had kept still since Boston.

When the window went to slate he shouldered the go-bag on his good side, drew the Glock and held it low, and went up the grown-over path through the alders.

A gust leaned on the deckhouse.

"Wednesday," she said. "On the ledge. You said local emergency management, and I knew it was Tess before you'd finished. I split kindling and said nothing." She kept her eyes on his. "That's mine. Yours is still yours."

"Portland told the county, because the procedure says you tell them before you use a town landing, and the county passed it to the island's coordinator. I didn't know until this morning that on Cutter the coordinator is one woman behind the counter at the store. When I said 'only us' on the ledge I meant the detail, and I should have asked who else those words covered, and I didn't."

He turned the notebook face down on his side of the table.

"From here you hear everything I hear, both sides of every call, as near as I can give them to you. And I want every name that comes into your head, the ones you'd rather not say as well, when they come and not after."

"Tess." She didn't stop for it. "Ned. He tells her everything at supper. And Russ. He heard the Ashby generator Saturday night and told us so on Sunday."

"Russ didn't hear about the landing until the fire company was called out for the standby," Dane said, "and by then it had already gone to whoever was waiting for it. Whoever had all three had them at one table."

She looked up at the overhead, and the wheelhouse above it.

"I've had a hundred-ton master's license three years," she said. "Never used it."

"What would you run?"

"This. Someday." She kept her eyes on the steel. "If Ned would ever be old."

"Ned will be old the day somebody he trusts asks him for the wheel," Dane said, "and not one day before. Until then he'll mind you, the way my mother minded my father. She dispatched for the Lowell police for twenty-six years, and every cruiser in the city knew the number of his bus. If he was late home she'd put it out as a welfare check, and some patrolman would pull up beside him at a light on Bridge Street and tell him to call his wife."

The laugh got out of her before she knew it was there. It hurt on the way out, low in the ribs.

Her left hand lay on the table between them, scar up. His came across and stopped an inch short of it, and waited.

She closed the inch.

"The heel was on the Harbor Road at one this morning," she said. "By the church. Somebody was talking him along on a radio."

Ned came in off the deck for the green jacket and shrugged into it, filling the doorway.

"Somebody's got to get Russ," he said. "I can walk the Harbor Road and nobody looks twice."

"He's ashore with a radio and somebody on the other end of it," Dane said, "and he knows your face. I'd rather you stayed on your boat."

"Russ won't come for a voice on a radio with the whole harbor listening. He'll come for me at his door."

Dane looked at him a long moment.

"Then go now, while it's daylight, on the open road where the houses can see you, not the shore. *Day 8, three o'clock*

The binoculars lived in a box by the helm, and Beth had them on the store dock.

The *Margaret Ann* lay alongside the fuel float. Red hull, white wheelhouse, her deck lights burning in broad day. A hose ran from the diesel pump up over her rail. Two men in hoods worked her deck: trawl doors chained up, totes stacked and strapped, hatch covers dogged down. Stowing for sea.

The glass had come up four since noon. Outside the harbor mouth the white water came in sets now, with long gray between. By dark the northwest would have the sea laid down outside, and she'd go. Hours before anybody's relief.

On the console the Icom quit scanning and held on sixty-eight. Since the second system came in yesterday, Tess had added a roll at three, for the back-road people who went to bed at dark.

"Cutter check-in, this is net control at the store."

Tess read a roll at a gallop. Always had, names and jokes and the price of propane in one breath. This voice set each name down by itself and waited before the next. Allen. Burgess.

Then: "Calloway."

The wait after it ran longer than the others. Then Coombs.

Beth knew that voice. Her father had used it at the bank, reading his own numbers off a page with the loan officer at his elbow.

Tess had kept that name off the air all week because Beth asked. She'd say it only for somebody beside her who wanted to hear who answered.

She put the binoculars back on the float. One of the hooded men had stopped work and stood looking up the dock at the store.

Tonight the boat sailed. The one person left who could put Arsenault in that store was the woman reading the roll. He would not leave her on the island behind him.

"Dane."

He came up the companionway. She gave it to him, the hose through the roll.

"She read my name. On the air. In a voice that isn't hers."

He took the binoculars from her and looked at the float a long time, then at the store windows.

"Then I'm calling Pike now," he said, "from the wing, with the door open, and you'll hear my side of it. I'll give you hers word for word when I'm off."

*Day 8, half past three*

He came in off the wing and shut the door on the wind. He didn't sit.

"Here is what she said, as near as I have it. The patrol boat and the Coast Guard's are both at their mainland stations, and neither will run the ledge field across the bay in the dark with the swell that's left, so first light is the earliest anyone from the mainland sets foot on Cutter. The state police won't cross until the boats do. If anyone runs blue lights up to that store, Arsenault is off the float with Tess aboard before they're past the church, and we never see either of them again. She's authorized me to hold this boat and let them come to me if they come. She told me, and I'm giving it to you the way she said it, that she has not authorized bait."

Beth looked across the water. The *Margaret Ann* had cast off from the float and gone back out to her mooring off the fish pier, deck lights burning, to wait for dark.

"Then we don't go to him," she said. "He comes to us."

"Go on."

"I call Tess on nine. Tell her I'm alone on the boat tonight. My voice. Not Ned's. Not yours. She passes it on. He has to come here before he sails."

"Why the boat?"

"Steel. I know it blind." She looked aft through the glass, down at the deck going blue in the last light. "Foot of this ladder to the stern gate is thirty-four steps. Eleven years in the dark. He's never stood on it."

He looked down at the deck, at the two trucks in their chains and the open lane beside them.

"If I put you behind that door with me and we wait for daylight, Tess has until dark. If we do it your way, whoever comes has to cross thirty-four steps of dark steel that you know and he doesn't, to reach a door I'm standing in front of. That's the hold Pike authorized, with the door left where he can find it, and it's as near her line as I'm willing to go." He turned from the glass. "Then here is what I want. When we know they're coming, the brow comes in and gets chained, so the only way aboard is from the water. The floods stay on, and the breakers for them are on that panel, behind this lock, where nobody but you can reach them. A man coming over the stern under those lights is a man I can see."

"And when we know it's the water, they go dark. Then he comes over the stern blind." She put her hand on the panel. "If the floods go dark, I come down the inside ladder to the car deck."

"I'd rather you were behind a locked door when that happens, where anyone who wants you has to get past me first."

"In the dark you can't find that deck. I can."

He looked at his arm, then across the water at the store, and didn't answer that.

"Stern gate stays chained," she said. "Thigh high. Anybody coming forward off the quarter comes along past it. And the ring buoy stays in its bracket aft with the line flaked clear. November water, whoever goes in has ten minutes."

"You're planning to fish him out."

"I'm planning not to watch anybody drown off this boat."

"Pike hears all of it from me before anything happens," he said, "and you'll be standing beside me when she does."

Far down the pier road Ned was coming in the last light, big and stooped, alone, the spare radio in his fist.

Dane took the Iridium out of his jacket and held the wing door for her. Out on the wing the northwest wind took his collar. He turned his face up to the clearing sky and bent his head to hers so she could hear the line, and when Pike answered, he began to report.

*Day 8, eleven o'clock*

The box truck's aluminum was cold through two layers at his back, and Dane let it be cold.

She didn't answer him.

The heel came after her, fast and uneven. A second shot at sound was a guess, and its flash would show the marshal where to aim. He wanted his hands on her. She backed along the bulwark, her feet reading the plates. The ring buoy bracket went past under her fingers.

The gatepost hit her hip. She turned her shoulder to the sound.

Close now. His breath. The right heel landed, and his weight came onto the bad left leg for the last step.

She drove her shoulder into his hip.

He went back against the gate chain at the height of his thighs. It held. He didn't.

Something hard hit the plates and skidded forward into the dark. The gun. Still aboard.

The water took him. Out of the black below the transom came a huge, sucking whoop, a man's whole chest pulling in at once. Then silence while he was under.

She had the ring buoy out of its bracket. Her hands knew the bracket.

He came up choking off the port quarter. She threw at the sound. Line ran out through her bare palms, burning, and the buoy slapped the water.

"It's at your head. Arm through it. Both arms."

The line went taut and jerked.

"Kick. Face up. Kick."

She sat back and took him in hand over hand, the wet rope stiffening in the cold. Forty-eight degrees. She worked him along the hull to the recessed quarter ladder and held him against the plate.

"Rung's at your right hand. Hold it."

Behind her a ratchet clicked, and chain fell slack with a long rattle. He'd found the release one-handed, where she had put his hand before dark.

Then his boots, fast along the port bulwark. He stopped at the gatepost, between her and the water. A holster snapped.

"Marshal." Dane's voice, level, no louder than in her kitchen. "Both hands on the rung, Shane."

He said it once.

"Knee on the bottom rung," Beth said. "Now the next."

She took Doucette under the arms and hauled. Dane's good hand came down past her shoulder and found the hood, and between them they got him up the rungs and over the gate chain. He lay on the plates shaking so hard his teeth clattered.

Dane rolled him onto his face, knelt on his back and cuffed him one-handed, the ratchet of the cuffs loud and small.

Beth fetched a navy blanket from the mess locker in the dark and put it over him.

The Icom woke in her float coat.

"*North Reach*, *North Reach*, Whitcomb." Russ, clipped and formal, his breath still short under it.

She keyed. "Whitcomb, *North Reach*. Go ahead."

"I have one in custody at the store. Lyle Arsenault. Taken in the back office with the store's radio in his hand. He was calling the skiff back. Doucette had his handheld off, for quiet. Over."

Dane, beside her in the dark, let out a breath through his nose.

"*North Reach* copies one in custody. Say the status of Tess Marr. Over."

"Tess Marr is unhurt. Found on the office floor with the mic cord round her wrist. Sitting up and talking. Over."

"Copy unhurt. *North Reach* has one aboard, Doucette, in cuffs. No injuries. Over."

"Copy. Holding my prisoner at the store till daylight. Whitcomb standing by one-six."

"*North Reach* standing by one-six."

Dane's good hand came up to her jaw, cold, and he kissed her once and let her go.

She walked forward to the deckhouse door, found the ramp-light switch by touch and pushed it up.

The sodiums over the ramp warmed slowly, dull pink and then orange, and the light came back down the car deck. Doucette shaking under the navy blanket at the stern gate. The gun against a chock. The chain in the box truck's tie-down, still ringing faintly.

---

*Day 9, three in the morning, then first light*

Toward three, with Doucette asleep by the heater in the crew mess, Dane stood on the open deck with the Iridium and Beth beside him, his head bent to hers so she had both ends of the line, and gave Pike the night from the beginning. Relief would come the first hour the sea allowed. That was first light, as Pike had said at half past three. Two deputies had to drive up from Portland to meet the boats.

At first gray they ran the brow back out to the stone, and Russ walked Arsenault down the pier.

He walked him on the inside, one hand on the chain of the cuffs and the other at his elbow, his own holster side turned away. Arsenault went where he was put: clean-shaven, the good Musto jacket zipped to the chin. He said nothing.

Tess came behind them in her red vest. Ned walked beside her, his hands in his pockets. He had sat the night in Russ's kitchen by the scanner and heard sixteen before midnight. At first light he'd walked down to the store, and he was on its step when Russ brought them out. A foot of cold air lay between his sleeve and hers, all the way down the pier.

"Town office has no power," Russ said at the foot of the gangway. "You've got heat, light, steel, a lock and a federal officer."

"I'd have expected him to have that back door covered," Dane said.

"It opens inward. A man inside hears it before you're through." Russ tipped his head up the hill. "There's a cellar bulkhead behind the propane cage. Every kid on Cutter has used it. Nobody from away knows it's there. The stair comes up behind her desk. He had the radio to his mouth and his back to the stair. He had the cuffs on before he'd turned around."

He looked across the harbor at the *Margaret Ann*, white-lit on her mooring. "The second crewman's still aboard her with the lights on. He's not going anywhere. Marine Patrol can have him."

Dane took Arsenault from him at the brow and walked him down to the crew mess with Doucette, and stayed with them both.

"She isn't under arrest," Russ said, low, to Beth. "I'm not sure what she is."

Tess stood at the foot of the gangway. A red band circled her right wrist, darker at the bone. Beth stood at the top. Twelve feet of aluminum brow lay between them.

Tess's hand came up toward Beth's braid, the old reach, and came down empty.

Beth didn't step back. She didn't step closer.

"Russ, put her in the passenger cabin. The stove's lit."

When the sun cleared the hill Ned climbed the ladder to the wheelhouse. He hadn't brought coffee.

The northwest wind had dropped to nothing. Through the glass Beth could see down into the passenger cabin window. Tess sat upright on the bench by the stove with her hands in her lap, looking out at the water.

Ned took the mic. He turned the knob on the fixed set, up from nine, click by click, to sixteen. Beth let him.

"All stations, all stations, this is the Cutter Island ferry *North Reach*." His radio voice, clipped and correct. "First departure Sunday at first light. Passengers and light freight. *North Reach* standing by one-six and one-three."

# Chapter 10 — First Boat

**Day 9 to Day 11 — Saturday to Monday**

*Day 9, noon*

Two engines came across a harbor so flat it held the sky, and quit at the pier.

Marine Patrol lay outside the *North Reach*, the Coast Guard outside her. A tall woman came across first, black hair pulled back hard, navy raid jacket over a down vest, face the color of the pier stone. She stood on the granite until it quit moving for her, then found Beth at the rail and held the look until she was sure of her.

The older of two deputies met Dane at the brow and read from a sheet, flat. Supervisory Deputy Pike's instructions. Deputy Reyes was relieved of the protective detail, effective now, and removed from the case. A referral had been made to internal affairs. He would cross on the first boat and report to Portland on landing.

"Understood," Dane said.

He gave his own handover, left hand tucked into his soft-shell. "The witness is aboard and unhurt. The cottage on the Harbor Road was compromised the first night, the Ashby place on Monday and the town landing on Thursday, and all three went through the same back office, which Agent Quist will want before anyone sweeps it. Two prisoners are in the crew mess. The weapon is on the car deck against the chock where it fell."

His eyes never once went to the rail.

Quist held out an evidence bag. Dane brought the notebook out of his inside pocket, its yellow cover gone soft at the corners. *Subject and I.* A lawyer in Portland would read that line. Beth's hand closed on the rail.

He put it in the bag.

"It goes to Moreau," Quist said. "All of it goes to the defense, Reyes."

"I know it does, and so does she."

Marine Patrol walked Doucette across first, stiff on the bad leg in a borrowed sweatshirt, then Arsenault, chin down in the Musto collar. The *Margaret Ann*'s second crewman already sat in the patrol boat's stern, cuffed.

Quist ran her thumb along the seal. Dane's hand went back to his inside pocket, found the lining flat, and came out empty.

---

*Day 9, early afternoon*

The passenger cabin stove was going. Somebody had brought Tess coffee in a paper cup, and it sat on the bench beside her with a skin on it.

She sat where Russ had put her, upright, in the red vest. The band on her right wrist had gone purple at the bone. Her glasses were up in her hair with no pencil in them.

Quist sat across from her. Beth stayed by the door, where steam had crept up the glass at the corners and stopped.

"Do you know what was in the cartons, Ms. Marr?"

"Pills."

"Blue tablets," Quist said. "Pressed with a pharmacy's stamp and a score down the middle, so they look like something a doctor gave you. One is enough to stop a man breathing. A man who thinks he's taking something for his back."

Tess's hands came off her lap and gripped her knees.

"Oh, fuck," she said, small. Then, to the floor between her boots: "Oh, Bethie."

Quist told her she was under arrest, and for what, and that she could have a lawyer. Her voice didn't change for any of it.

Tess didn't wait to be asked. "Back office floor, by the booster shelf. The board lifts. Gallon freezer bag. Every boat, every gallon, two winters of diesel after six. I add it twice."

Quist wrote it down.

Then Tess looked at Beth.

"The first days I believed him. Scare you off before the grand jury, nobody hurt." Her thumb went to the purple band. "After the landing I didn't. Thursday noon I told him at my back door I was done. He said the *North Reach* would burn at the pier with Ned asleep aboard her, and I'd stand on the float and watch."

Beth didn't come off the door.

"The nine thousand, Tess."

"Quarter past six Friday morning he was at my back door with an envelope, because the truck never called in at four, and he said a paid witness is a witness nobody believes."

Nobody said anything after that. The stove ticked. Tess looked at the cup beside her.

"Make Ned eat something that isn't coffee."

Beth crossed the cabin and picked up the cup. She tipped it over the rail outside and came back with a sleeve of saltines from the mess locker, and zipped it into her float coat where Tess could see. Tess's mouth moved and made nothing.

Quist cuffed her in front and set the right cuff clear of the bruise, and checked it with one finger.

The deputy walked her down the brow. Ned stood at the pier end with his hands in his jacket pockets, and Tess passed within reach of him. Her hands lifted against the chain, an inch toward his sleeve, and came down. His stayed where they were.

---

*Day 9, half past three*

The light had gone long and yellow. Marine Patrol was back for the second trip, idling against the pier, a low burble she could feel in the stone.

Quist stopped Ned at the foot of the brow with her notebook out.

"Monday, Captain. You give me the year. Every Thursday you saw and didn't."

Ned took his left hand out of his jacket. The freight book was in it, the Thursday sheets dog-eared. He put it in her hand.

"You took no money."

"No."

"If your statement reads the way you told it to Ms. Calloway, I don't see a charge, and I won't ask the Coast Guard to look at your license. The dealer's license will be gone by Monday. His contract goes with it."

Ned stood with it. Out past the moorings a cormorant came up with something silver and went back down. Then he lifted one finger to his left ear and turned the hearing aid off. Beth had watched him do that to a hundred people. She had never seen him do it to news.

Quist went down into the patrol boat and sat on the engine box with her face to the horizon, and the boat backed off and went out past the breakwater, getting small. The deputies stayed. So did Dane, a man with no detail and a seat on the morning boat.

Up the hill a porch light came on. Then a kitchen window, and another, house by house up the Harbor Road as the cable crew brought the grid back. Behind the store the generator stopped. In the quiet it left she could hear her own boots on the stone.

At the top of the hill one window stayed dark. Hers. She had switched that light off herself the first morning, with the dragger on the guest mooring below it.

---

*Day 9, night*

The older deputy walked her house at dusk with a flashlight, every room and both doors, and went back down to the boat. From the wheelhouse he had her kitchen window; from the gate, the Harbor Road.

The kitchen was as she'd left it the evening they went. The dish towel he'd dried his hair with still hung over the back of the chair. She put the overhead light on, the switch she'd turned off with the dragger watching, and stood under it. Then she relit the stove: birch bark, kindling, two splits of maple from the woodbox. The draft caught, and the pipe began to hum.

Her father's clock in the front room had run down at twenty past four on some day she hadn't been home. She wound it with the key from the shelf and set it going by her watch.

Behind her the mudroom door shut. Dane lifted the latch and shoved, one motion, and the iron dropped home with a clack.

She waited for her shoulders to come up at the sound. They didn't. She stood with the poker in her hand and listened to the house, and the house had nothing to tell her. The refrigerator hummed. The clock went on. A little wind at the gable end, and under the window the harbor, flat, with nobody on it.

She raised the shade. Below, the guest mooring ball rode alone on black water, the town's lights laid across it. Half the harbor could see into her lit kitchen, the older deputy included. She pulled the shade down again. Not for fear. For the deputy.

Dane had sat down at the table with his jacket still on. He'd laid both hands on the oilcloth under the light, where she could see them, and he left them there.

"I can go down to the boat," he said. "I'd still be on it in the morning, and nothing about Monday would change. I'd rather you told me which than leave me to guess."

She came around the table and took his hands off the oilcloth, the right and then the left, her thumb kept clear of the strips under his sleeve.

"Stay," she said. "Yes. That's both your answers."

He stood up into it. He'd had the boat's coffee; she tasted it on him. His good hand went to the back of her neck and stayed, warm, while the other hung where she'd let it go.

In her room off the hall she worked the fleece off his bad arm first, because he couldn't lift it, and he held still for it as he had for the strips. She pulled her own sweater over her head. The flannel sheets were cold enough that she laughed out loud getting in, and he sucked air through his teeth beside her, and that was what let the last of her go: nobody in the house to hear them.

He took one from Tess's box. Neither of them said her name.

He came down over her on his good arm. She took his left hand, the bad one, brought it to her breast herself, and let go only when he'd stopped being careful with it. She was wet, and he went into her in one long slide, and the ease of it was the strangest thing yet. No cot. No boots by the bed, no ear left out on the dark. His cock inside her, slow, her knees up around him, her own mattress, the clock down the hall.

"If you want me to stop," he said against her temple, "for the arm or for any reason or for none, say so and I will."

"I don't."

Somewhere in it she quit listening. She found that out afterward. Eleven years of tying up at night, and she had never once heard the engine quit, only the quiet after. When she came it was with her mouth open on his good shoulder, and what she heard all through it was her own voice, out loud, in her own house. He came a moment after, her name said low and once, and then his whole weight, which she let him give her.

Her back went down into the mattress and stayed there.

He lay with his face in her hair until his breathing evened. Then he turned his head.

"Pike hears about tonight from me, on the porch, before it's anywhere else, and you're hearing that first."

"I know."

The clock went on down the hall. She found his hand on the quilt.

"What's Christmas in Lowell?"

"My father drove the Bridge Street route for thirty years and retired last spring, and he still goes in for the Christmas shift, because the depot is short and he's done it every year so the young drivers can be home. He wears a Santa hat the transit authority has asked him in writing not to wear. My mother starts the ham at five in the morning, cloves in rows you could sight down, and keeps her old scanner on the counter so she can tell the table where his bus is." His thumb found the scar on her palm. "I'd like to put you on that bus, front seat on the right, where he'll tell you who used to live on every corner and be wrong about half of them."

"Christmas is two runs," she said. "Kenny'd cover. I'd need a reason."

"The ham is eleven pounds, and there are three of us."

"That's not a reason. That's a load."

"Then here's the reason. I'd like to stand in my mother's kitchen on Christmas morning with you in it, and not have to describe you to anybody."

She lay there a while.

"Hand me my watch."

He reached across her with his good arm and found it on the nightstand. She tipped the face to the gap under the shade and read the date, which she already knew.

"Kenny owes me two," she said.

He laughed into the pillow. Then he got up, pulled on his jeans, took the Iridium out of his jacket and went down the hall, and she heard the front door open on the cold and didn't get up to see who it was.

---

*Day 10, first light*

The diesel settled at nine hundred, and she had it through her boot soles and into her socks.

The harbor was glass, the sky coming pink through the forward windows. Ned came up the companionway a step and a rest at a time, a cup in each hand. He set one by her hand on the console. She put the saltines beside his. Then he didn't take his chair. He stood aside from the wheel with his back to the chart table.

"Take her off," he said.

She took the wheel.

Below, Kenny Thibodeau had his color back and his hat on crooked. In the passenger cabin the two deputies sat by the stove, and Dane sat across from them with his bag between his boots. She leaned out the wing. "Single up. Hold the aft spring."

Kenny cast off bow and stern and stood by the spring with a turn on the bitt. She put the wheel over and gave her a touch ahead. The spring came taut and groaned, the stern leaned into the fenders, and the bow walked off the stone, slow, the twisted bridge sliding across the forward glass.

"Let go aft."

She backed her straight out into open water and came ahead and around. The *Margaret Ann* lay on her mooring off the fish pier, dark, yellow tape across her rail. The fuel float was dark. The store had a light in it, grid light, ordinary.

Ned drank his coffee. He ate a saltine, then another. He said nothing about the reefer, or Tess, or her father standing in this wheelhouse eleven years ago asking him to take on his girl.

At Pruett the sodiums still burned over the ramp, pale in the daylight. She brought the *North Reach* into the slip, lowered the ramp, and climbed down to the trucks. Propane first. Handle up, tension off, the chain out of its D-ring and into the bin.

Quist stood at the ramp head under the sodiums, steadier on land.

"Doucette fired both rounds at the landing. The second crewman ran the skiff's outboard, and he's been talking since Marine Patrol took him off yesterday."

"You thought I took the money," Beth said.

"I did. Now I don't."

That was all she gave it. Beth would have trusted a sorry less.

The deputies had a car running at the ramp head, and a second idled behind it for Dane. He came up the ramp with his bag on his right shoulder and stopped in front of her.

"Come on the Friday boat," she said.

"There's a Friday boat every week until April, so you'll have to be more specific than that."

"Any of them."

He took her bare hand in his good one, in front of Quist and both deputies, and held it for a breath, warm over cold. Then he went to the second car, and she got into the back of the first with the subpoena folded in quarters inside her coat.

---

*Day 11, Monday morning*

The grand jury room on Federal Street was too warm and smelled of radiator dust. No judge, nobody for the other side, a woman at a machine taking every word.

She gave Moreau the night freight as she'd written it on the tide calendar, down to fourteen cartons, that she saw, and the plate at 9:44 at the Pruett ramp.

He set a copy of a credit union deposit slip in front of her. Her paycheck on the first line, in her own hand. Under it, *Cash — $9,000.00*, pressed so hard the copier had caught the dents as shadow. Nineteen years of store receipts. She knew that hand.

"For the record," Moreau said, "Teresa Marr has given a statement that she placed this cash with Ms. Calloway's deposit."

"I never saw it. I never knew it was there. Before that paycheck I had four hundred and twelve dollars."

"Ms. Calloway, this is a question a defense lawyer will ask you at trial. I'd rather ask it first. While Deputy Reyes was protecting you, did you have a sexual relationship with him?"

One tall window held gray daylight over the roofs.

"Yes. I chose it. He told me before it happened that he'd report it, and he did."

"Thank you, Ms. Calloway. That's all."

On Tuesday, at the Pruett slip on the morning freight, her phone found one bar and rang, and Dane read her the indictment the way it was written, without hurrying any of the names: Pelletier on four counts, voted Monday before the jurors went home, and Arsenault, Doucette and Teresa Marr in a second, returned that morning. She heard it out with her bare hand on a binder, and when he was done she let the binder go.

---

*Three weeks later, a Friday*

From the passenger deck Dane had the whole car deck below him: the line of trucks in the orange of the floods, the lanes between them, the raised ramp ahead, the stern gate and its chain aft. He had put his back to the deckhouse wall without deciding to, and he supposed he would be doing that for the rest of his life.

The frost had whitened the rail. His boots were XtraTufs now, bought in Pruett the first Friday, and Beth had looked at them and said nothing at all, which he had taken as the review.

This was the island's own freight, propane and diesel and groceries. The dealer's Thursday boat was gone and wasn't coming back.

The costs had landed about where he'd told her they would: a suspension served without pay, a letter in his file that would still be there the day he retired, and a transfer to the District of Maine, which was short of deputies and had said so in writing. When he leaned on the rail the seam on his left arm pulled, pink and new.

Below him Beth walked the trucks with her gloves off, her bare hand going to each binder handle and resting there long enough to judge it before it moved on. For ten days he had watched those hands and written down what they did, and every line of it had been true and none of it had been the point. Now he watched them and there was no page, and he found he didn't miss it.

Since the first of the month she'd stood relief watches at the wheel on the Sunday runs, on her own license. Ned had asked the district board. Russ had told Dane that on the float last Friday, and added that Ned would go to his grave before he told her.

At Christmas Kenny was taking her two runs, and she was coming to Lowell to ride the Bridge Street bus in the front seat on the right. His father had called twice already to ask whether she would mind the hat.

Quist stood beside him, seasick and upright, and opened her jacket far enough to show him the edge of a manila envelope.

"You're off it, so you can know this much. Her ledger. Two years of night fuelings, three harbors, one account on the mainland. Every column added twice. I have never seen a cleaner fucking ledger in my life."

He took the outside ladder down to the car deck. Beth was at the box truck's rear binder, and she didn't look up until he was beside her in the lane.

"I love you," he said.

"Hand me—" She stopped, her hand out for the next strap, and left it in his instead. "I love you."

Then she turned her face up, and he kissed her briefly in the spill of the floods, both of them cold to the nose.

The *North Reach* came into the Cutter slip under the ramp sodiums. The transfer bridge hung level on its new cables, the steel still bright. Russ stood on the float below the slip, leaning on a piling as if standing were optional. When the ramp came down Quist walked off it to him with the envelope, and from the ramp head Dane heard all of it, because she didn't trouble to lower her voice.

"I need somebody who knows every boat in this bay by its exhaust, and I can't tell a dragger from a skiff unless I'm being sick over the side of one. That ledger has three harbors in it and one account. Work it with me this winter."

"Do I want to spend the winter in a cold room reading Tess Marr's arithmetic with a federal agent who can't stand up on a float?" Russ said. "No. I'll have my traps out by Tuesday."

Russ had decided somewhere around *three harbors*. His hand had come off the piling then, and it closed on the envelope now as if it had been waiting there all along. Up the hill the fuel float at the store was dark, the way it should be after six.

Beth went down the lane to let the trucks go, and told him to bring the light, and he brought it.
